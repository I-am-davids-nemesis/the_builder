Hale had been relieved in the small hours and was back on the door by late morning, and the boy still hadn't moved.

He moved a little after that. Hale heard it through the hand's width of open door: a long breath in, and a stop, and a sound that wasn't a word, the sound a man makes when he tries to sit up and every part of him writes in at once to complain. Then nothing for a while. Then, very quietly, to the ceiling:

"Oh. Oh, that's not right."

The bed creaked. Something was put down on the floor, carefully, and then something else, more carefully; the good foot and the splinted one. There was another stop.

"My *hair* hurts," said the boy.

Hale looked at the far wall of the corridor and didn't smile, because it wasn't his place, and because the boy had come up the corridor the night before with half his face gone plum-colored, and Hale had seen what was on the coat.

He didn't hear the count arrive. He never did. There was simply, after a while, a second voice in the room, low and level and in no hurry.

"The bath is drawn, sir."

"I can do it myself," said the boy. "Really. Thank you. I don't want to be a—"

"Arm, sir."

"Yes. All right."

They came out together, the boy in yesterday's shirt with his weight on the count's shoulder and his stick in his other hand, and went down the corridor to the bathing room, round the corner, at the boy's pace, which was slow. Hale went behind them, because the boy was his duty wherever he went; and at the green-tiled door the count looked back at him once, pleasantly, and Hale stopped, and took up his post outside it. Steam came out round the edge of the door and smelled of rosemary.

He heard the bath take the boy, and the boy's breath go out of him in a long hiss that ended in something close to a laugh. He heard the count say something about the cut over the eye, and the boy say "It's fine," and then "Ow," and then "It's fine." He heard a comb. He heard the boy, after a long while of water and quiet, say in a smaller voice, "Was anybody hurt? Last night? Apart from her. Did anybody else get—"

"No, sir."

"Good," said the boy, and then, after a pause, "Good."

When he came out of the door at last he was dressed in plain clean things that weren't his: a white shirt, a gray coat that fitted him better than anything he'd brought, the trouser leg laced again over the splint in black ribbon. Hale hadn't seen any clothes go in. He'd been combed. The bruise along his jaw had gone from plum to the color of a thundercloud. He stood in the doorway on his stick and blinked at the light in the corridor as if it had been put there to annoy him.

"Morning, Hale," he said. "Sorry."

"Sir."

"Breakfast," said the count, behind him, "is waiting in the yellow room, sir. Back past your door, along to the end, and on the left. You'll be expected."

"Expected," said the boy. He looked at the length of the corridor, which was long. "Right. Thank you, Burks."

He set off. Hale watched him go, the stick and the step and the stick, and when he turned back the door of the bathing room was standing open on green tiles and a bath already drained, and the count was nowhere at all.

* * *

Daritha Scove had been waiting for a little over an hour and was on her second cup, and she had used the time.

The yellow room was a family room. That was the first thing she'd marked, coming in. Not one of the receiving rooms off the great stair, with their portraits of dead kings and their chairs set out for petitions, but a small room at the end of a private corridor with a round table in it, a cloth the color of butter, a sideboard of covered dishes going quietly cold, and windows open on a garden where somebody, in spite of the drought, had kept the roses alive. There were five chairs at the table besides hers, and one empty, and the empty one had been set with a cushion on the seat and a stool beside it.

So. A family breakfast. She had been asked to a family breakfast.

She'd been engaged by the Sorveys a little over a month ago, and had made her first approach, through the proper door, a fortnight after that; and had been answered, very civilly, that the queen would bear it in mind. Then, last night, the king had stood up in the long room and given the Morgans a present, and the Sorveys had sent round to her house before it was light: *today, if it can possibly be done.* She'd written to the palace expecting to wait a week. She'd had her answer by the first bell after dawn. *Her Majesty will be glad to receive Mistress Scove at breakfast.*

That was fast. In her experience, fast meant someone had a reason. And a breakfast, here, with no court and no one to repeat what was said, meant that whatever answer she took away, nobody was going to give it to her in front of an audience.

She understood that. She approved of it, professionally. A refusal in a room full of people was the kind of thing that got made into nursery stories.

She sat with her hands folded on her little leather case and considered the women round the table.

The queen she knew, as everyone knew the queen: dark, composed, perfectly kind, with a face that gave away exactly what she meant it to and not one thing more. The Duchess of Morgan, beside her, was a surprise. Daritha hadn't been told she'd be here, and she was of more interest than anyone else at the table, because the Duchess of Morgan had a daughter of an age to marry and had been given a remarkable present by the boy in question not fourteen hours ago. The duchess drank her tea and said very little, and when she looked at anything, she looked at it as if she were working out what it would fetch.

And the princesses. The elder was tall and red-haired and very handsome and plainly bored, and had been arranging and rearranging the folds of her skirt for an hour, and watching Daritha while she did it. The younger had hardly said a word. She'd gone red twice for no reason Daritha could see, and she had a stone jug at her elbow with a cloth over it, and kept touching the handle.

Four of them. And one of Daritha. She'd had worse odds; not often.

The door opened.

He was smaller than she'd been told. She'd been told; everyone had told her; but there was a difference between being told and seeing him in the doorway, a slight young man on a stick, in a gray coat, with his jaw gone black and blue and a cut over one eye that had been seen to. He looked about fifteen. He looked at the table, and at the five women round it, and his face did something she'd seen grown men's faces do on the threshold of a room full of their creditors.

"Oh," he said. "Sorry. I didn't—I'll come back."

He'd actually started to turn round.

"Tor," said the queen. "Sit down."

He sat down. He did it the way he did everything, Daritha would learn, at once and with an apology: lowering himself into the chair with the cushion, getting the splinted leg up onto the stool with both hands, propping the stick against the table, where it slid, and catching it, and propping it again. When he'd finished he looked round at all of them with grave politeness, as though he'd come to be examined.

"Good morning, Your Majesty," he said. "Your Grace. Highnesses." His eye lit on Daritha and stopped, and he gave her a careful little bow from the waist, as much as the chair allowed, without the least idea who she was. "Ma'am."

"How are you feeling, darling?" said the queen.

He opened his mouth to say *very well, thank you*. Daritha watched him decide not to. It was a small decision and it cost him something.

"Not very well, Your Majesty," he said. "I'm sorry. My head's—" He put his fingers to his temple and took them away again. And then, not to anyone, under his breath, in a tone of real grievance: "It isn't *fair*. I didn't even *drink* anything."

The younger princess made a small sound.

"I'm sorry," said the boy again, to the table at large. "I'm not very good company. I know I'm not. Please forgive me. I'll be better in a bit, I expect, I just—" He shut his eyes, briefly, against the light off the cloth. "It's very yellow in here."

The younger princess stood up. She took the cloth off the jug and poured something into a cup, and it came out slowly, in a way Daritha didn't like the look of at all: thick, and brown, and faintly speckled, with a smell that reached across the table and stood there.

"Here," the girl said. She put it down in front of him. Her ears had gone pink. "For your head. It's for afterward. Cook makes it for the guards, for after a rage, and Papa's had it, and Alphie, and it's horrible, everybody says so, but it works, and you've got to have it."

The boy looked at the cup. He looked up at her.

"After a *what*?" he said.

"A rage. Yours. Last night."

"I didn't have a rage." He said it kindly, the way you'd correct a child who'd got muddled about a story. "I'm not—I couldn't. That's nobles. I had my shield on. It was the duke that—"

"Tor," said the princess, "*drink* it."

He looked at the cup a moment longer. Daritha saw him weigh it: his own certain knowledge of what he was and wasn't, against a headache that had plainly got its fingers into every corner of him. The headache won.

"All right," he said. "Thank you. Should I—"

"Fast," said the princess. "All at once. Don't stop. It doesn't get nicer if you wait for it, it just gets cold."

He drank it all at once.

Daritha had been in a great many rooms where a great many things were swallowed, and she had seldom seen a face do quite what his did. It went through surprise to dismay to something like grief, and came out the far side into a sort of stoic wonder, as if he'd been shown something terrible about the world and was resolved to bear it like a man. He put the cup down with care. He wiped his mouth.

"Thank you," he said hoarsely. "That's dreadful. It must be very good for you."

The elder princess laughed out loud, and covered it, a beat too late, with her hand.

"Tor," said the queen, who had not laughed, though something had happened at the corner of her mouth, "I'd like you to meet Mistress Daritha Scove. Mistress Scove is a go-between. She arranges marriages."

Daritha inclined her head. "Master Tor."

The boy looked at her. Daritha had sat across a table from perhaps two hundred young men at the moment of being told who she was, and she knew all the faces they made: the blush, the swagger, the sudden cold attention of a younger son doing sums. She had never seen this one before. It was the face of someone being handed bad news by a doctor, and resolving to be brave about it.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, I'm sorry. Who was it?"

There was a pause.

"I beg your pardon?" said Daritha.

"Whoever it was. I didn't mean anything by it. Really. If I was too forward with somebody, or said something, or—I don't always know, here. What counts." He'd gone red. "You didn't need to come all this way. Honestly. I'd have understood. You can tell her I'm very sorry, and I won't bother her again, and I hope she—" He hesitated. "Which one was it?"

Daritha found that, for perhaps the first time in twenty years of practice, she had nothing ready to say.

"Nobody," said the queen gently, "is turning you down, Tor."

"Quite the contrary, Master Tor," said Daritha, recovering. "I come on behalf of a family who wish to propose a match. With you."

He looked at her. He looked at the queen. He looked, for some reason, at the empty cup, as if it might have done something to him.

"Is this a joke?" he said. His voice had gone careful and quiet. "Because if it is, I don't—I'd rather it wasn't. Please. Not about that."

"It is not a joke," said the queen. She said it with a firmness that surprised Daritha a little. "Nobody in this house would make a joke of a marriage, Tor. It's far too serious a thing. I promise you."

He turned to Daritha, as if for the second opinion of a stranger who had no reason to be kind to him.

"It's quite true," said Daritha. "But before anything else, I must ask. Are you open to marriage, Master Tor? In principle?"

"In—" He thought about it. He actually thought about it; she watched him. "Well," he said. "Yes. I suppose so. Someday. I always thought I would, sometime. I hadn't thought it would be—soon." He looked doubtful. "My mother would like it. Yes."

"Then I'll go on." Daritha opened her case and did not yet take anything out of it. This next part was delicate, and she meant it to be. "My clients are a family of some substance. Of very considerable substance, in fact. In the ordinary way of things, I'll be frank, Master Tor, the difference between your station and theirs would be thought to run against you." She let that sit for exactly one breath. "But I'm given to understand that in your case the ordinary way may not apply. That your station may be—other than it appears. It may even be that it runs the other way. Higher than my clients'." She turned, very lightly, toward the queen. "I wondered whether Your Majesty might—"

She watched the faces. That was what she'd come for, as much as the answer.

The queen's was pleasant and closed. The duchess drank her tea. The elder princess raised her brows a fraction, with real interest, the interest of someone who hadn't heard and would like to. And the younger princess went red to the ears, all at once, and looked hard at the jug, and said nothing whatever.

*So,* thought Daritha. *Somebody saw something.*

The boy only looked puzzled. "I'm a baker's son," he said. "From Two Rivers. I don't think it runs any way but the one."

"Mistress Scove," said the queen, kindly, "perhaps you'd show him the card."

It was not quite a rebuke. It was not quite not one. Daritha took the card out of her case.

It was a beautiful card. The Sorveys did that kind of thing well: heavy cream stock, cut by hand, the edges gilt, and in the corner, pressed in, the little ring of knotted rope with the letter in it. The name was written across the middle in a fine dark hand. *Meredith Anne Sorvey.* She laid it on the yellow cloth beside his plate.

He looked down at it.

Daritha saw it happen. It wasn't the name that did it; it was the mark in the corner, the ring of rope. His eyes went to it, and stayed, and his face went still. Not cold. Still, and very polite, the way a man's face goes when he's been shown into a room and recognizes the furniture. She'd seen that face before on suitors. It was never about the girl.

"Thank you," he said. He didn't pick the card up. "I don't think I've had the pleasure of meeting Miss Sorvey."

"Very likely not. She's spent the summer at her family's house in the country."

"I'm sure she's—" He stopped, and chose. "I'm sure she's very nice," he said firmly, to the card. "I expect lots of people in a family are."

The elder princess made a noise into her teacup.

"I'm very honored," the boy went on. He'd folded his hands on the cloth. "Very. Only I'd have to talk to—" He looked round the table, and his eyes fell on the queen with a sort of relief. "To my advisors. Here. Before I could say anything. I'd have to ask Your Majesty. And I'd hate to—I wouldn't want to pull a lady like that *down*, ma'am. Socially. People would talk, wouldn't they? If she married a—someone like me. It wouldn't be fair to her."

It was a good excuse. It was, Daritha thought, a really rather good excuse for a young man who had a cut over one eye and had just drunk that. She'd have let it stand.

The royal women did not let it stand.

"Nobody in this kingdom," said the queen, "would think a girl had been pulled down by marrying you, Tor. Quite the reverse."

"You made a *river*," said the younger princess, in a rush, "and a whole county's got water now, and everyone's talking about it, everyone, Lady Westry's cook's brother lives in Ford and *he* says—"

"And you're nice," said the elder princess. "Which nobody ever is. People would be furious with her."

The duchess set down her cup. "My husband," she said, "has talked of nothing but the flying rig since the ninth bell last night." She picked up her cup again. That appeared to be all.

The boy looked from one to another of them with something like betrayal.

"I'm very busy," he tried. "With the building. I'm not home much. Not ever, really. I go off for days. It wouldn't be much of a—"

"Builders' wives are used to that," said Daritha. "My clients understand it perfectly."

"I haven't got much money."

"Tor, darling," said the queen, "you have more than most of the Hill. I've seen what Sara Debri is holding for you."

"I've got a *leg*," said the boy, a little desperately, and then seemed to hear himself, and went redder.

Daritha waited. She was good at waiting. In her experience, if you waited long enough, a young man who didn't want a match would tell you why; and the why was the thing you took home.

He was looking at the card. At the ring of knotted rope. His mouth had gone tight at one corner, and she thought, for a moment, that he was going to say it, whatever it was; and then she saw him decide not to, the way she'd seen him decide about the headache. He'd been brought up not to say unkind things about people at table, she thought. Somebody had brought him up very carefully.

"There's—" he said. He stopped. "There's something already. A sort of—prior—" He made a shape with his hands, a small careful one, as if he were holding something that might come apart. "An understanding. It's not settled. I'm in the middle of it. It's complicated, and I'd rather not—" His eyes went, for one instant, to the Duchess of Morgan, and away again very fast. "I'd rather not say. If that's all right."

"Tor," said the queen. "I'm afraid Mistress Scove can't take *I'd rather not say* home to a family."

He looked at the queen. He looked at the duchess, who was looking at him now over her cup, quite steadily. He looked at the ceiling, briefly, as if there might be something useful on it.

"The present," he said. "To the Duke and to—to Your Grace. Last night. The rigs." He swallowed. "It wasn't only a present. It was meant to—so you'd think better of me. Your Grace and the duke. Because I'd like to marry Trice." He said it to his hands. "Someday. When I can. If I can get a title from the king, then it would be all right, wouldn't it, and I could ask. That's all I'd need. A title." He paused. "That's all."

He had gone red from the collar to the hair. Daritha, who had listened to a great many young men explain how they'd come to be in love, recognized something in the order of it. It was the face of a man telling the truth with the pieces in a slightly different order from the one they'd happened in, and knowing it, and minding. But it *was* the truth, or most of it; she'd have staked her fee on that. And nobody else at the table seemed to notice the order at all.

The Duchess of Morgan put down her cup.

She looked at the boy for a long moment, and then she smiled. It was a small smile and a measured one, and Daritha couldn't have priced it to save her life. Then the duchess turned her head and looked at the queen, one look, and the queen looked back; and whatever went between the two sisters in that look went by Daritha like a carriage with the blinds down.

"I see," said the duchess. And no more.

"Oh, that's not *fair*," said the elder princess.

Everyone looked at her. She'd put her chin in her hand, and was pouting, quite openly; it was the first thing Daritha had seen her do all morning that didn't look practiced.

"I'd have asked first," she said, "if anyone had told me we were allowed to."

"You did ask," said the queen. "I said no."

"Well, I'd have asked *properly*."

"He could be a knight," said the younger princess.

She said it suddenly and loudly and went red at once, but she kept going. "Easily. He could. It's three great deeds, isn't it, and then Papa says yes, that's how Sir Barnaby got his, and the river's one, because that's a whole county saved, nobody could say it wasn't. And the tanners' children are two. So he's only got one more to do."

"Knights count," said the elder princess, in a different voice, sitting up. "They're noble. A bit. Enough."

"Enough to work with," said the queen thoughtfully.

The boy was looking at the younger princess with his mouth slightly open.

"But it can't just be anything," she went on, warming. "Not for the third. It's got to be enormous. You'd have to kill a monster. Or sail all the way round the world, in a boat you'd made yourself, by yourself—"

"Would it have to be *on* the water?" said the boy faintly. "The whole way?"

"—or," said the princess, "since you're a wizard, it could be magic. Something nobody's ever made. A real masterwork. Not copied, it has to be new." She ran out of breath and sat back. "That would count. Master Abelard says that's how the old ones did it."

The boy said nothing. He'd stopped looking at her. He was looking at the window, at the roses, at nothing; and for a moment his face went quiet and far off, the way Daritha had seen jewelers' faces go when you put a stone into their hand, and then it came back.

"I think that's a very good idea," said the queen. She said it as though it had just occurred to her, which Daritha did not for one moment believe. "I'll speak to the king for you, Tor. But you mustn't count on it, darling. He may very well say no."

"Yes, Your Majesty," said the boy. "Thank you. Of course."

Daritha closed her case.

"Then I'll tell my clients," she said, "that there is a prior claim, under negotiation, and that Master Tor is honored by their interest and isn't at liberty to answer it at present." She rose. "Which isn't a no."

"It isn't," said the queen pleasantly. "No."

"Keep the card, Master Tor," said Daritha. "Circumstances change."

She saw him flinch at that, very slightly, as if he'd been handed a card like it once before and told much the same; and she filed that away too. She curtsied to the queen, and to the duchess, and to the princesses, and to the boy, who struggled to get up for her until the queen put a hand on his arm; and she went out into the private corridor, past the guard on the door, with her case under her arm.

She'd come for one answer, and she was going home with three. The boy was spoken for, after a fashion. The crown meant to make him a knight, and had decided so this morning, at breakfast, over the jam. And nobody at that table would say one word about whatever had happened in the long room last night; which was an answer too, and the most expensive of the three.

The Sorveys, she thought, would want to know all of it. She'd see what they paid for it.

* * *

The door closed behind the go-between, and the boy let his breath out.

It went on for a long time. Karina, watching him over her cup, thought she'd never heard anyone let out so much breath at once; he went down in his chair as it went, an inch and then another, and put one hand flat on the yellow cloth, the way a man does when he's just stepped off a boat onto a jetty and isn't yet sure the jetty means to stay still.

"Rocks," he said, to no one, under his breath. "All the way down."

"What?" said Karina.

"Nothing. Sorry." He sat up. "Nothing, Highness."

*Highness.* He'd been Highness-ing her for a week. She'd never minded before.

She didn't know why she minded now. She sat and arranged her skirt and looked at him and thought about it, and didn't like where it went. She'd asked Mama. Half for a joke. Mama had said no over the marmalade and Karina had laughed and so had Varley, and it had been a joke, it had been a perfectly good joke, and now Trice had gone and *done* it, or he had, and it wasn't a joke at all, and Karina had been sitting right here when it happened, and nobody had so much as looked at her.

That was all it was. Trice had got there first. Karina didn't like coming second. That was all.

Her mother had got up and was lifting the covers off the dishes on the sideboard one by one, which a queen didn't do, and frowning at them.

"Well," said her mother. "Eat something, Tor. Eggs. Mercy, there's the ham you like." She put a plate in front of the boy with rather more in it than he could have eaten in a day, and sat down, and looked at him, and Karina, who knew her mother's faces, saw that she was worried and didn't know what to do about it. "And then I don't know what's to be done with you."

"Done with me, Your Majesty?"

"Rich won't want you outside the walls today. Not until he's seen Winchester, and the council's done shouting, and there's an answer about"—she made a small gesture that took in the go-between, the card, the knighthood and the whole of the long room—"all of it. He'll want you somewhere safe. And somewhere people can see you being perfectly calm." She sighed. "And I can't think of a single thing for you to do. There's nobody your age in the palace but these two, and you can't ride, and you can't walk the gardens on that leg, and I'm not having you sit in your room all day being stared at by Hale."

The boy put down his fork.

"I could make some things," he said. "Small ones. If that's all right. If there's a table somewhere, and nowhere I'd set anything on fire." He was brightening, Karina saw, the way the lamps brightened when someone turned up the wick. "There's a few things I've been meaning to do. Little things. I wouldn't be any trouble."

Her mother looked at him for a moment as if he'd handed her a gift.

"Darling," she said, "you can have any room in the palace you like."

"Of course," said Karina, "you'll still need your third deed."

He looked at her warily.

"And last night doesn't count," she said.

"*Karina*," said Varley.

"It doesn't. It can't." Karina set down her cup. "You can't be knighted for it. He made a joke of it. He stood there in front of everyone and told Winchester they'd had a quarrel over a *lady*, and that she was going home with *him*, and then he told Winchester they were *friends*. That isn't a great deed. That's a story about two men and somebody's wife. Nobody gets knighted for a story about somebody's wife."

"He was hit about forty times!" said Varley. She'd gone pink all over. "I was there, you weren't even near, you were behind the bishop. He was hit and he *got up*, he kept getting up, and she'd have died, Karina, she was *blue*—"

"I'm only saying how it'll sound."

"It sounded *brave*."

"It sounded like a joke, and it was meant to. He *meant* it to." Karina turned to the boy. "Didn't you?"

The boy looked from one of them to the other, like a man watching two dogs decide which of them gets the bone, and being the bone.

"I'd honestly rather it didn't count," he said. "If that's all right."

"*See*," said Karina.

"That's not what he *means*—"

"If you'll excuse me," said the boy. He was getting up. He was getting up the way he'd sat down, in stages, the leg off the stool with both hands, the stick found and lost and found again. "Your Majesty. Your Grace. I think the medicine's working. I think I should go and—find a table. And start. Thank you for breakfast. It was very—thank you." He bowed, to her mother, to Aunt Mercy, and, after a fractional hesitation, to Karina and Varley together, as if they were one dangerous thing. "Highnesses."

And he went. He went faster than Karina would have believed anyone could go on a stick, and the door shut behind him, and they heard the stick go away down the corridor, quick, quick, quick, like someone running from a fire.

"Well, really," said Karina. "We're not *that* bad."

Varley threw a bread roll at her. Their mother didn't even look up.

Karina caught the roll and put it on her plate. Across the table, beside the boy's untouched eggs, the go-between's card was still lying where he'd left it, cream and gilt on the yellow cloth, with the little ring of knotted rope in the corner. Nobody else was looking at it.

She reached over and picked it up, and turned it over once, and slipped it into her pocket.
