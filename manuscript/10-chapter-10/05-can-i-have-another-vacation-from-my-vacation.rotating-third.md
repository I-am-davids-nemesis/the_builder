The orangery had been somebody's pride once, and then nobody's for a long time.

It ran along the south side of the private wing, a long room with a glass roof on iron ribs and a wall of tall glass doors that didn't open any more, and the light came down through it white and even and warm as bathwater. Most of the panes were whole. A few had cracked and been mended with strips of lead, in a hurry, years ago, and one had gone altogether and had a board nailed over it, so that there was a square of shadow on the floor that moved round the room as the day went. Down the middle ran two long tables of scrubbed pine, gray with age, with drawers underneath full of string and rusted shears and little paper packets of seed that had long since given up. Against the walls stood the tubs, great wooden ones bound with iron, a dozen of them, with dry earth in them gone hard as brick, and in the earth the stumps and gray sticks of whatever had grown there. One of them, at the far end, nearest the glass, still had a tree in it: a small dusty tree with leathery leaves and, high up, three hard green lemons the size of walnuts, which it seemed to be keeping out of spite.

It smelled of old soil and warm dust and, faintly, of the lemons. It was very quiet. Hale was outside the door.

Tor had looked at it from the doorway that morning, on his stick, with the queen's footman waiting behind him to hear which room it was to be, and had said, "Oh, *this* one, please," as if he'd been shown a puppy.

Now he sat at the end of the nearer table with his leg up on a crate, and a sheet of paper in front of him, and the pen in his hand, and wrote at the top of the paper, in his neat school hand, *Things to do*.

He underlined it. He felt better at once.

"Right," he said.

There was copper at his elbow already: a stack of plates, cut square and clean, as many as he'd want for a day's work and probably more. He hadn't brought them. Nobody had come in. They'd simply been there when he sat down, as they were always simply there now, on his desk, on tables, by his chair; he'd stopped wondering about it some time ago, the way you stop wondering about the bread being on the table in the morning, in a house where somebody bakes. There was ink, too, and a stylus, and a board.

He wrote *1.* and stopped, and thought.

*1. Left-handed rig (for Varley).*

That was easy. He'd promised. And it mattered, when you thought about it, more than it sounded. He'd been thinking about it since the dinner, on and off, in the bath and in bed and once, regrettably, while Connie was talking to him. Because a rig you steered with your right hand was a rig for going somewhere, and that was fine; but a soldier wanted his right hand for the sword. A soldier with a rig in his left hand could fight in the air. And that would matter, if it ever came to it, because the Austrans flew. Kolb had said so. Kolb had said a good deal about the Austrans, mostly in the practice yard, mostly while Tor was lying on his back in the sand; but the thing Tor remembered, the thing that had stayed with him, was that their machines flew so fast they *roared*. A great roaring, Kolb said, like a fire going up a chimney, so you heard them before you saw them, and then you didn't see them for long.

Tor had tried to imagine going that fast. He'd been as fast as he dared, on his own rig, over the sheep field, and he hadn't roared. He'd only gone a bit deaf.

He looked at the paper.

"Two," he said, and wrote *2.*, and then sat with the pen over it, because the trouble with a left-handed rig was that you couldn't just turn it round.

You had to move where the field listened. That was the whole job, really; take the bit of the plate that heard the hand, and move it, and re-draw the lines that ran from it, and there you were. Only if he was re-drawing the lines anyway, it seemed silly to draw them the same as before. They'd been the first lines he'd ever drawn for a thing that flew, in a week when he hadn't been sleeping, and he could see now, looking at them in his head, that they went round the long way. Like a path across a field that somebody had made by walking it badly once, and everybody else had followed. If he took them straight, the rig would answer quicker. It would go faster, too, with the same push behind it.

And the turning. The turning had always been horrible. Everybody said so. Trice could do it, but Trice could do anything; Rolph did it by swearing. You had to lean, and tilt the hand, and wait, and then it swung round all at once like a gate in a wind. It would be easy to make it gentler, now, while the lines were all up anyway. Only a little more drawing.

And—

He put the pen down, and put his hand up, flat, the way he held a rig, and moved it slowly forward through the warm air of the orangery, and frowned at it.

The last time he'd flown fast, really fast, over the river in the sky with the wind pulling the breath out of him, he'd felt the shield get warm. Not much. Only a little, on his chest, through his shirt, like a hand laid there. He hadn't thought about it at the time. He'd thought about it since. A shield that was warm was a shield that was working, and a shield that was working hard all the way across a county, just to keep the air off your face, was a shield that wouldn't have much left over if somebody threw something. Better if the rig did some of that. A sort of—parting, in front, so the air went round you instead of into you. Not too much, or you'd have nothing to breathe. Just enough to keep it fresh and moving and soft, the way air comes in at a window.

"That's three things," Tor told his hand. "Four, with the left."

He looked at the paper. It said *Things to do*, and *1. Left-handed rig (for Varley)*, and *2.*

He drew a small square in the corner of the page, absently, and put a line through it, and pulled the copper board toward him, and the stylus, and the ink.

When he looked up, the square of shadow from the boarded pane had gone all the way across the floor and up the opposite wall, and there wasn't any shadow, because there wasn't any light. Someone had lit a lamp and put it at his elbow. He hadn't heard the bells. His neck had set like mortar. The ink in the pot had gone to a black skin, and when he poked it with the stylus it cracked.

On the board in front of him lay a template, finished: the belt plate and the handheld control side by side, the control drawn for the left hand. He looked at it for a while, with the lamp on it. The lines were straight. He was pleased with the lines.

He got up, which took several stages, and found he was hungry enough to eat the lemons, and found there was a tray on the end of the table under a cloth, which he ate, all of it, standing up on one leg. Then he limped out into the corridor, where it was night, and Hale was there, and said "Sir," in a way that might have meant anything.

"Sorry," said Tor. "Was I long?"

"Since the second bell yesterday, sir."

Tor thought about that.

"Oh," he said. "Sorry."

He meant, after that, to go on down the list. He sat down the next morning to do it. He had the paper there in front of him, and the pen, and he wrote *2.*, again, under the first *2.*, because he'd lost track of the first one.

And then he thought about what *2.* might be, and *3.*, and what came after; and somewhere under all of it, the way you feel a draught before you find the window, he thought about a cream-colored card with a ring of knotted rope in the corner, lying on a yellow tablecloth.

He put the pen down.

"I'll just do a few," he said. "Of the rigs. To see they come out."

He did a few. They came out. He tested one in the orangery, standing on his good leg with his bad one held up behind him like a heron, and rose very gently a foot off the floor and turned, left-handed, smoothly, a whole circle, with the air going cool and soft round his face, and came down, and laughed out loud; and then he looked round at the empty room and the lemons, a little embarrassed, and sat down and did a few more.

He did a few more after that.

Copying was the dull part. It always had been. You laid the template down, and laid the blank square over it, and went into it just far enough, and let the lines come through, the way water comes through a cloth, and then you took it off and did the next. It didn't need anything of you, except that you kept on. You could think about anything you liked while you did it. Or you could think about nothing at all, and that was better. You could just count.

"Thirty-one," said Tor. "Thirty-two."

He'd been working since breakfast. He would work until dinner. That was all right. Nobody could say he was ignoring anybody, if he was working. Could they. He was making rigs for the kingdom. That was the opposite of ignoring people. That was practically being polite.

"Thirty-three," he told the lemon tree.

The card had said *Meredith Anne Sorvey*, in a lovely hand. He didn't think about it. He thought instead, quite firmly, that it was a very good thing it had said Sorvey, right there on the front, in ink, where he could see it. Because imagine if it hadn't. Imagine if she'd been a cousin, say, on her mother's side, with some perfectly ordinary name, Smith, or Barrow, or something, and nobody had mentioned the family. He might have said yes. He'd been very tired, and his head had hurt, and everyone was being so kind about it. He might have said yes, and gone to dinner, and sat down, and looked across the table, and there would have been Dorgal, passing the salt.

Tor shut his eyes, briefly, at the thought.

No. Whatever else you could say about it, it was honest of them, putting the name on the card. Nothing good could possibly come of being related to Dorgal. Not to anybody. Not in any way. He was glad, really, that people's names told you who they were.

"Thirty-four," he said, much more cheerfully, and laid the next square down.

By the evening of the third day there were a hundred of them.

They lay in stacks along the far table, belt plates in one row and controls in the next, all of them warm still from his hands, all of them the same. He counted them twice, from habit. Then he sat looking at them for a long moment with his chin in his hand, aware in a distant way that this was quite a lot of rigs, and that he hadn't decided what they were for, and that he'd have to, at some point, decide.

He took a clean sheet of paper and wrote *Cargo floats* at the top of it.

He'd been thinking about those, too. He'd been thinking about them since Mercy's lunch, since the pears that wanted getting off the trees and to somewhere before they went to cider in the cart. A rig was no good for that. A rig wanted to go *up*; it was a person standing on air, and a person could balance. A cart full of pears couldn't balance. It needed to sit on something that was always there, the way a boat sits on the water, without anybody holding it up, and stay level whatever you put in it, and go where you pushed it and stop when you stopped.

It took longer than the rig had. It was new all the way through, and it fought him. Once, he made a float that sat beautifully level a hand's breadth off the floor until he put a pear on it, at which point it went gently and inexorably up to the glass roof and stayed there, among the iron ribs, with the pear on it, like a small sad moon. He had to get it down with a rake out of one of the drawers. Once he made one that went where he pushed it, and kept going, and went through the tub with the dead orange tree in it, and Hale came in.

"Sorry," said Tor, from the floor. "Sorry. It's fine. It's meant to."

Half a week. There were a hundred of those, too, at the end of it, stacked under the table where there wasn't room on it, and the orangery smelled of hot copper over the lemons, and the dead orange tree had been put back in its tub, more or less.

He hadn't seen Rolph all week. That was all right; Tor didn't mind. Rolph had come by the first morning, and stood in the doorway, and looked at the lemons and the copper and Tor, and said, "Right. I'll see you when you come out," and gone off whistling to the Debris. Sara was there. Rolph had been at the Debris, as far as Tor could tell, from breakfast every day to well after dark. Tor couldn't blame him. Sara was wonderful. If he'd had a choice between the Debri house and an old orangery with a man copying squares in it, he knew which he'd have picked; and he'd have picked it every day, too, and come back late, and whistled.

He got down off the stool, very slowly, and found his stick, and stood holding on to the edge of the table, looking along it at the stacks. They looked like the stacks in the copying room at school, at the end of term. They looked like work. He found he was very tired, all at once, and very pleased, and that there was an apple on the windowsill which he hadn't put there.

He was sitting on the end of the table eating it, with his leg out along the boards, wondering vaguely what came next, when the door opened, and Rolph came in.

* * *

"Well?" said Rolph. "Is it saved?"

The boy looked up from his apple. He was sitting on the end of a potting table with his leg stuck out along it in its splint and its black lacing, like a heron on a fence, with the evening light coming down through the glass on him, and copper all round him in heaps. He had a smear of ink across one cheekbone and the expression of a man who'd been in a mine and come up into the day and wasn't sure yet which day.

"Is what saved?"

"The kingdom." Rolph came down the room between the tubs, taking it in. Stacks along the far table, neat as bricks. Stacks underneath it. A thing like a tray, a hand's breadth off the floor, quite still, with nothing holding it there and a pear sitting on it. Lemons. "I heard you'd taken it on. By yourself. All of it, at once. I thought I'd come and see how far you'd got."

"Oh." The boy looked round, too, as if he were seeing it for the first time. "No. Sorry. It's just rigs. And the floats, for Mercy's fruit. I haven't done anything really."

"How many rigs?"

"A hundred."

Rolph had picked up one of the controls from the nearest stack, to look at. He put it down again, carefully, back on the stack, the way you'd put down somebody else's baby.

"A *hundred*," he said.

"They're left-handed," said the boy, as if that explained it.

Rolph stood for a moment looking at the stacks, and the stacks under the table, and doing a sum he'd been brought up to do, and arriving at a number, and putting the number away somewhere, because it was no good to him tonight and it would only frighten the boy. Down the length of the room, the floating tray turned very slowly round on the air, like a leaf in a pond, and the pear went with it.

"Right," said Rolph. "Well. That'll do for the kingdom." He sat down on the table beside the boy, which creaked, and took the apple out of his hand, and bit it, and gave it back. "Now you can stop."

"I wasn't—"

"Little Tor. You've been in a greenhouse for a week. Hale says you sleep in it. Hale *talks* about you. Hale's never talked about anybody." He looked down at the boy, sidelong. "There are people who want to see you, you know. People you could talk to. Who aren't lemons."

"I talk to Hale."

"People who talk *back*."

The boy considered this, chewing.

"Like who?"

Rolph had been waiting for that. He'd been waiting for it all the way along the corridor, and down the length of the room, and he took his time. He'd had the breakfast twice: once from Varley, at the gallop, with gestures, and the go-between done in a voice, and once from Karina, more slowly, correcting her; and then, last night, a third time, without any of the good parts, from his mother, who had looked at him over her reading glasses at the end and said, "Mercy's asked me. You'll see to it, Alphie," in the voice that meant she'd seen to everything else already, and he was only to carry it.

"Well," he said. "Like your potential in-laws, for instance."

The boy went scarlet. It was a thing Rolph had seen many times and never got tired of: it started at the collar and went up like wine into a glass, steadily, all the way, to the roots of his hair. His mouth opened. Rolph watched him almost ask *how did you know*, and then decide, quite visibly, that he didn't want to know, not ever, and shut it.

"The Morgans," Rolph went on comfortably, "would like you to dinner. Tomorrow. Ninth bell. Just family. They seem," he said, and let it sit, "*very* happy with you."

"Do they?" said the boy faintly.

"Over the moon. Uncle Eric's flown into the stable roof twice. It's going to be a good dinner, Tor. Nobody's going to throw anyone. Nobody's going to hit you forty times. No dukes." He considered. "One duke. But he's on your side."

There'd been a question, of course. There always was a question, with the boy, now: the king wanted him in, and seen to be in, and calm, and nobody knew yet what Winchester's people would do, or the Sorveys', or anybody's, if he went out through a gate in the evening in a carriage. Rolph had been in his mother's sitting room for most of a bell about it, and his father had come in at the end and said a few things, and in the end it had been settled: the Morgans' street, and back, and two of the purple following at a distance where they couldn't be seen, and Rolph in the carriage the whole way. The boy didn't need to know any of that. He'd only fret, and then he'd offer not to go, and then Rolph would have to go back to his mother's sitting room.

The boy was looking at his own knee.

"Maybe," he said. "Maybe that would be a good idea. I could—yes. I suppose it would be rude not to."

"Very rude."

"Then I'll go." He nodded, slowly, as if agreeing to have a tooth out; and then his face changed, all at once, the way it did, and his head came up. "Oh. Oh, the *dryers*."

"The what?"

"The drying fields. For the fruit. I said I'd do Mercy food dryers, at lunch, I said, and I've done the floats, but I haven't started the dryers, because I haven't got the template, it's at the Debris. All three went." He'd half got off the table. "If I'm going to dinner, I ought to bring them. I could do a hundred tomorrow, easily, from the template. If I had it." He looked up at Rolph. "Would you mind? Going and getting it for me? I'd go myself, only—"

"Only?"

"Only your mother asked me to stay in." The boy said it quite simply. "The queen. She said. So I'd better."

Rolph looked at him. He thought about the sitting room, and the bell of talk about the Morgans' street, and the two of the purple following at a distance.

"Yes," he said gravely. "You'd better."

"So would you? Only if it's not any trouble."

It wasn't any trouble. It was, in fact, Rolph thought, the best idea anyone had had in a week, and he'd had several. He'd been at the Debris since breakfast. He'd only left because Heather Debri had begun to look at him over the teapot in a particular way, kindly, the way you look at a nice dog that's been in your house all day. He could go back now with a reason in his hand, and an errand, a real one, from Tor; and Sara would have to find it, the template, which would mean going up to her father's strong room, which was at the top of the house, by the long way, past the window with the seat in it.

The good mood lasted all the way up him like warmth from a fire. It reached the place underneath, the place where the sums were kept: Sara's father was a merchant, and nobles married within three steps, and a prince married within one. It reached it, and sat there for a moment, and didn't change anything. Then he put it away with the other number. That was for some other evening, too.

"No trouble at all, Baker," he said, getting up. "I'll go now."

"Thank you. Really. It shouldn't take long. It's only a template."

"Oh, not long." Rolph was at the door already. He turned, and caught the boy's eye, and winked. "No more than four bells. Five, if the traffic's bad."

"*Four*—" said the boy, behind him, but the door had shut.

* * *

The prince came back before dinner.

Hale was on the door, because Hale was always on the door; he'd given up pretending to himself that he'd be relieved, and had begun to take his supper on a stool. He heard the prince before he saw him. He was whistling, coming up the private stair, something from the taverns, not all of it in tune; and when he came round the corner he had a flat parcel in brown paper under his arm, and his collar undone, and a look about him that Hale had seen on a good many young men coming home at the end of a long afternoon, a sort of sleepy satisfaction, as if the world had turned out to be exactly the right size.

It had been a bell and a half after the third when he'd gone. It was going on for the seventh now. The Debri house was on the other side of the Hill. Hale had walked it, in his time, in less than half a bell, in boots.

"Evening, Hale," said the prince.

"Highness."

The boy had been working in his own room since the afternoon, with the floats moved over from the orangery and stacked against the wall by the window; he'd said he liked to see them. He looked up from the floor as the prince came in, and his whole face lit, and then, almost at once, fell into a sort of sympathetic dismay.

"Oh, Rolph. Oh, I'm sorry. Was it awful?"

The prince stopped.

"Was what awful?"

"The streets. You've been ages. Was it the carriages? It's the carriages, isn't it." The boy took the parcel and held it, without opening it, as if Rolph had carried it across a desert. "You said four bells. I thought you were joking. I didn't think it could really be that bad."

Hale looked at the wall opposite. It was a good wall. He had looked at it for thirty years.

"Shocking," said the prince. His face was perfectly straight. Hale had to give him that. It was as straight as anything Hale had seen on the parade ground. "Wall to wall, Tor. Carts. A dray turned over by the fish market. A man with geese."

"*Geese*."

"Dozens."

"Oh, that's *awful*." The boy put the parcel down on the bed. He was shaking his head. "Someone ought to do something about it. Really. All the people in this city, and the only way to get about is to sit in a box behind a horse and wait for the man in front. It's ridiculous. It's—" He'd stopped, and was looking at the window, at the roofs going down the hill in the last of the light, and the haze of dust and chimney smoke over them, and the sound that came up from them, that you stopped hearing after a week in the palace, of wheels and hooves and shouting. His lips moved. "Something better than carriages," he said, under his breath. "There's got to be. Not a rig. Not for everyone. Something you could just—get on—"

The prince looked across the room at Hale, over the boy's head. Hale looked at the wall. The prince went out, whistling.

By the next evening there were fifty more of the floats against the wall, and a hundred of the fruit dryers on the desk in stacks of ten, and Hale had stopped trying to count anything.

The boy had done the dryers in the morning, from the prince's template, fast. He'd done them talking. He didn't seem to mind that sort of work so much when he was talking. Most of it was to the floats, about pears; some of it, around the fourth bell, was to the rig on the end of the bed, about string.

"It's hemp," Hale heard him say. "That's the trouble. It's just hemp. It's what I had. It's fine for me, I don't mind, but it rubs, and it stretches, and if a duke's going to wear it he ought to have something better than a bit of string off a parcel. Something with a buckle. Leather. A proper strap." A pause. "I wish I knew where you got straps."

Hale didn't hear anybody come. He'd stopped expecting to. But when he looked round the door at the fifth bell with the boy's tea, there were straps on the end of the bed: a great pile of them, new leather, oiled and supple and dark, each one with a brass buckle and a brass ring stitched in at the side where a plate could hang, and a smell coming off them like a saddler's shop. There were fifty. Hale counted them, because he couldn't help it.

And there was the count, standing at the foot of the bed with his hands folded, in his dark coat, exactly as if he'd been standing there all afternoon.

"Will those answer, sir?"

"*Burks*." The boy had one in his hands already, bending it, testing the buckle with his thumb. "These are perfect. These are better than perfect. Where did you *find* them?"

"There's a man in the lower town, sir."

"With fifty?"

"He had a good many, sir," said the count, which wasn't an answer, and Hale saw the boy accept it as one.

They threaded the rigs on together, the boy and the count, on the floor, the boy talking and the count saying "Sir," at intervals, and Hale stood in the doorway with the tea going cold on its tray and watched Count Lairdgren, who had been old when Hale was a recruit, kneel on a carpet and buckle copper plates onto belts, fifty times, with the patient, even hands of a man who has done every sort of work there is and doesn't mind any of it.

"And a chest," the boy said, near the end, sitting back on his heels. "Burks. I'm sorry. I'm always asking. Only I can't take it all in my arms, there's two hundred, I'll need something to put it in, and it ought to be something nice, it's for the Morgans—"

"I'll see, sir."

Hale didn't see him go. He went out with the tray himself, to get more tea, because the boy had drunk none of the first lot; and when he came back the count had gone, and in the middle of the floor, where there'd been nothing, there was a chest.

It came up past Hale's knee. It was long, and deep, of some dark wood with a red light in it, and every face of it was carved: flowers, a whole bank of them, roses and something like lilies and small round daisy-things, crowding up along the sides; and in among them, and along the lid, birds. Wrens, Hale thought. Finches. A heron on the lid, standing on one leg in the reeds, so finely done that he could see the barbs of the feathers. The handles at the ends were brass, and someone had made them into branches.

The boy was on his knees in front of it with both hands flat on the lid, not quite touching, the way Hale had seen countrymen stand in front of an altar.

"Hale," he said. "*Look* at it."

"Sir."

"Somebody must have spent their *life* on this." He ran a fingertip, very lightly, down the heron's leg. "And he just—it was just *lying* somewhere, I suppose. In a cellar. I'm going to have to give it back. I can't put copper in this. It's a treasure."

He put copper in it. He did it carefully, kneeling, with a folded blanket from the bed in the bottom: the fifty rigs on their new straps, coiled round on themselves, belt and control together, and then the fifty floats on top of them, and then the dryers, a hundred, in their tens, packed in round the edges and into the corners so nothing would shift. Two hundred. He counted them going in, under his breath, the way he counted everything. Then he shut the lid, and the heron came down over all of it, standing in its reeds.

Then he sat back on his heels, and looked round the room, at what was left.

The other hundred floats stood against the wall by the window, where they'd stood all day; he hadn't said what they were for. And there were the rigs.

There were fifty of them. They were on the bed, mostly, in the heap they'd been in since the orangery, with the old hemp string still on some of them; and on the chair with the roses, and two on the floor by the hearth, where they'd slid off, and one on the windowsill, for no reason Hale could see. Fifty. Fifty rigs, with their straight new lines and their gentle turning and the controls drawn for the left hand. The army had begged for those. Colonels were on a list for them. Lady Westry had looked at the duchess all through the soup, the footmen said, as if she wanted to eat her, for *two*.

"What about those, sir?" said Hale. He hadn't meant to say anything.

The boy looked at the bed.

"Oh," he said. "I don't know yet. I haven't decided." He frowned at them, mildly, and at the floats by the window, as at some washing he'd been meaning to fold. "Somebody'll want them, I expect. I'll think about it after dinner."

He got up, by stages, and went to wash his face. The rigs lay on the bed. Hale looked at them for a while.

Then he went out, and shut the door behind him, all the way, which he hadn't done all week; and stood in front of it, and found he'd put his hand on his sword hilt, and took it off.

The prince came at the seventh bell, in his good blue coat, with his hair wet and combed and his collar done up all the way.

The chest had already gone down. Hale hadn't seen that either, exactly; he'd seen the count come back, and another man with him, a big quiet man in a leather apron that Hale had never seen in the palace in his life, with forearms like hams, who took one end of it without being asked. They'd carried it out between them, down the private stair, the count at the front end, walking backward, steady as a table, not a breath out of him. When Hale and the boy got down to the yard, it was already roped onto the back of the carriage, and the man in the apron was gone.

The prince had stepped back to let the count go by, in the yard. Hale saw that. He stepped back, and half bowed, as if he didn't know he'd done it; and the count, passing, didn't look at him at all.

"Is it on?" said the boy anxiously. "Is it on properly? It's very heavy. I'm sorry. How are we going to get it off at the other end?"

"I'll get it off," said the prince.

The boy looked up at him, all the long way up, and was visibly reassured; which was fair, Hale thought, because the prince was the size of a door, these days, and growing.

"Oh. Yes. Of course." He let Hale help him up into the carriage, the stick first, the leg, all of it, and settled himself, and leaned out of the window at once. "Thank you, Hale. Really. Don't wait up."

"Sir," said Hale; and stood in the yard and watched the carriage go out under the arch, with the chest roped on the back and the heron on its lid going away into the dusk, and two of the purple coming out of the guardhouse a moment later on horseback and going after it, quite slowly, as if by chance.

He went back up to the door. There were fifty rigs behind it. Nobody had told him to stay. He stayed.

* * *

The city at evening was everything Rolph had promised it was, which was a surprise to him.

They came down off the Hill into it and it closed round them like water. Every cart in the capital seemed to have been told to be somewhere at the seventh bell. They stood in a crush at the bottom of Mercer Street for longer than it took to say the Litany, wedged between a dray of barrels and a gentleman's chaise whose gentleman was standing up in it and shouting; they went at a walk behind a hay wagon for the whole length of the Long Walk, and watched the hay go down the street ahead of them a stalk at a time. A boy was lighting lamps. He was going faster than they were.

Tor sat with his face at the window and his leg along the seat, and watched it all with an expression of growing, scholarly outrage.

"It's every night," he said. "Isn't it. It's like this every night."

"Mostly."

"You poor thing." He was looking at a woman on the pavement with a basket, who was walking, and who passed them, and went round the corner, and was gone. "Four bells. I'd have *walked* it." He looked down at his leg. "Well. You would."

"I did think of it," said Rolph.

Tor didn't hear him. He was looking at the carts, and the gaps between the carts, and above the carts, where there was nothing, all the way up to the first-floor windows and the signboards and the roofs. His lips were moving. His left hand had come up off his knee and lay open on the air, and tilted, very slightly, and Rolph watched it the way he'd watched Tor's hands for two years, over desks and dinner tables and the edges of beds, and knew there would be something, now, that hadn't been in the world at dinner yesterday, and that it would be his fault, and Sara's, and the hay wagon's.

He put his head back on the cushions and grinned at the roof of the carriage.

The Morgans lived on the far side of the Hill, in Gilder's Row, which had been very good indeed about a hundred and fifty years ago and had been coming down gently ever since. The carriage turned into it from the bright street, and the noise fell away behind them, and the lamps were farther apart, and older. The houses were tall and narrow and close together, of a gray stone gone dark with soot in the cracks, with steps up to the doors and iron railings, some of them leaning. The Morgans' was the fourth along. It was three windows wide. It had a fanlight over the door with one pane of colored glass missing, and a lamp lit on either side, and a brass knocker in the shape of a pear, polished till it shone.

Rolph had been here a hundred times, as a child, with his nurse. He hadn't been since he was fourteen. He'd forgotten how narrow it was. Trice had never once let them collect her from it; she met everyone at gates. He knew why, without being told, the way he knew most things about the Hill; and he looked at the pear knocker, and at the missing pane, and up at the three windows with their curtains drawn and their lamps lit behind them, and said nothing at all, and would go on saying it for the rest of his life.

Tor got out backward, stick first, and stood on the pavement and looked up at the house.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, it's *lovely*."

He meant it. He was looking at it the way he'd looked at the chest: at the stone, and the iron, and the fanlight, and the steps, which had been worn hollow in the middle by two hundred years of feet. You could have fitted his father's bakery into the front room, Rolph thought, and the bakery's yard, and the oven.

"Isn't it," said Rolph.

He got the chest off the back of the carriage with the driver's help, which mostly meant the driver watching, and carried it up the steps in his arms like a sleeping child. It was heavier than it looked. It was a great deal heavier than it looked; the count had come down the stair with one end of it as if it were a box of hats. Rolph's arms were beginning to have opinions about that by the top step, and he filed it away with the others, and said nothing about that either.

The door opened before they knocked.

The butler was the same butler. He was smaller than Rolph remembered, and grayer, and his coat was very good and very old and had been brushed until the nap was a matter of faith, and he looked at Rolph, and at the chest in Rolph's arms, and at the boy on the step with the stick and the black lacing, and his face did nothing whatever, which was what a good butler's face was for.

"Your Highness," he said. "Master Tor. You're expected."

The hall was narrow and the stair went up out of it steep, with a red carpet on it worn down to the threads on the edge of every tread, where feet had come down for a century. There were pictures on the walls of orchards, a great many orchards, in different seasons; and over the hall table, a fine old map of the barony with every tree on it. It smelled of beeswax and roast meat and, faintly, of apples, as if they kept them somewhere in the house and the house had got used to them.

The butler opened a door on the left.

The sitting room ran the depth of the house, front to back, with a fire laid but not lit and the long windows open on the garden behind, where the evening came in cool and smelled of grass. There were good old chairs, and a good old carpet with a path worn across it from the door to the hearth, and a great many candles. Uncle Eric was standing by the fireplace with his hands behind his back, enormous, fair, going gray at the temples, with his collar too tight, as it always was, and his face lit up like a boy's at the sight of the chest. Aunt Mercy was sitting in the chair by the window with a glass in her hand, and didn't get up, and was watching the door with her head a little on one side.

Rolph took two steps in and set the chest down on the carpet, and straightened, and felt his back thank him.

Then he saw Trice.

She'd been at the far end of the room, by the garden door, in a gown of dark green, with her hair down. Down. He had time to notice that. He had time to notice that she'd seen them, and that she'd put down whatever she was holding, without looking where, and that she'd started to move; and he knew that movement. He'd seen it in the practice yard at school, on the sand, from the bench by the water butt, a dozen times, and every time it had ended with someone on their back. She came across the room the way she came across the yard: low, fast, not hurrying and not stopping, straight down the worn path in the carpet, faster than anyone has a right to cross a sitting room in a gown.

Tor was looking at the chest. He was turning to say something to Uncle Eric about the straps.

Rolph put out his hand, not to stop her, because there was no stopping her, but to be where the stick would be. It was. Trice hit Tor square, and Tor's stick went sideways out of his hand into Rolph's, and Tor went backward half a step on his good leg and would have gone farther if she hadn't had both arms round him by then and held him up; and she kissed him.

It wasn't a long kiss. It was a quick hard one, on the mouth, like a stamp coming down on wax.

"I love you," said Trice. She said it straight into his face, from about three inches away, quite clearly, the way she said everything. "This is so much *better*."

Her ears had gone pink. Rolph saw it. All the way up, to the tips, under the dark hair. He'd known her since she bit him in a nursery, and he could count on one hand the times he'd seen that happen; and he'd never been able to tell, any of them, what it meant, and he couldn't now.

Tor said nothing.

He couldn't. Rolph could see that he couldn't. He stood in Trice's arms with his own arms held out a little from his sides, stiff, as if somebody had handed him something very hot and very valuable and he didn't dare put it down, and his mouth open, and his face gone through scarlet and out the other side into a sort of shining blankness. He looked at Trice. He looked, past her shoulder, quite helplessly, at the chest on the carpet, with the heron on its lid; and Rolph watched him work it out, or think he did. *Better.* Better than two rigs in a box. Better than he'd promised. The straps, maybe. The floats. His eyes went back to Trice with something like relief in them, and something like bewilderment, as if he'd found the answer at the back of the book and couldn't see how anyone had got it from the sum.

Trice wasn't looking at the chest. She hadn't looked at it once.

Rolph laughed. He didn't mean to. He did it quietly, politely, into his collar, the way you laugh at the theater when you're near the front; but he couldn't help it, any more than he could have helped catching the stick. He'd seen a great many things happen to Tor in two years. He'd never seen this.

By the window, Aunt Mercy hadn't moved. She was watching them over the rim of her glass, all three of them, with her head on one side, exactly like her daughter, and Rolph had no idea in the world what sum she was doing.

Uncle Eric cleared his throat.

He did it gently, for him, which meant the windows only rattled a little. He came across the room, and put one large hand on his daughter's shoulder, and one, after a moment's thought, on Tor's, and moved them apart about a foot, carefully, as if separating two halves of a cake.

"Patricia," he said. "Let the man have his stick back."

Rolph gave it to him. Tor took it, and held on to it with both hands, and looked at it as if he'd never seen a stick before.

"Master Tor," said the Duke of Morgan. He was smiling down at him, a broad, warm, entirely uncomplicated smile, the smile of a man who had flown into his own stable roof twice that week and would do it again in the morning. "You're very welcome in this house. Alphie." He nodded. "Cook will give notice if it waits any longer, and I can't afford another. Will you come in to dinner?"
