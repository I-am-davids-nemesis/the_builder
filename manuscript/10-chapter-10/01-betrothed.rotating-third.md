"Trice!"

He didn't shout it. Collette would remember that afterward, because she'd expected him to: a boy on a stick, in a room that had gone quiet for him once already tonight, watching the girl he'd just greeted walk away from him as if he were a chair. Anyone else would have shouted, or let her go. He did neither. He said her name the way you'd say it across a kitchen table, only louder, and with something under it that Collette couldn't put a name to, and the Ducharina Patricia Morgan stopped dead three yards short of the terrace doors.

She didn't turn round. She only stood, with her glass in her hand and her back very straight.

The boy went after her. Collette went after him, without at all meaning to; she found she'd simply started walking, the way you follow a dropped glove, and Count Thompson came a step behind her because he'd been standing beside her and there seemed to be nothing else to do. The stick went out, and the good foot, and the bad one, three times, quickly and not well, and the boy put out his free hand and took hold of the Ducharina's sleeve. Just the edge of it, between finger and thumb, the way a child takes hold of a grown-up in a market.

She turned then. Her face was annoyed, the cold, finished, aristocratic annoyance of a girl who has been touched by someone who had no business touching her; and then, all at once, it wasn't. It changed while Collette watched. It was as if she'd been looking at him from a long way off and had come back, suddenly, the whole distance, and found him standing there.

"Tor," she said.

"What's the matter?" He let go of the sleeve. "What's happened? You looked—"

"Nothing's the matter."

"You walked straight past me."

"I didn't see you." It was very smooth. It was so smooth that Collette, who'd heard a great many smooth things said in her mother's sitting room, knew at once it wasn't true, and also that it had been said in exactly the voice the Ducharina would use to tell a tradesman his account was settled. "It's nothing. It's not a conversation for here. Later."

"But—"

"*Later*, Master Baker."

Collette had leaned in. She hadn't meant to do that either. But the boy had his back to her, and the Ducharina's voice had dropped, and a girl from a very small family does not get to be a larger one by standing at a polite distance from the most interesting thing in the room; so she leaned, a little, to hear, and found her shoulder against the boy's arm and her chin somewhere near his ear.

He felt it. She saw him feel it. He went pink again, from the collar up, all the way, the same slow thorough pink as before, and edged sideways on his stick, away from her, by about the width of a hand, with an expression of tremendous apology.

And the Ducharina's eyes went from his face to Collette's, and to the pink, and to the little sideways step; and her face fell.

It wasn't much. Collette had been brought up to read a fallen face across a ballroom and would have missed this one at three feet if she hadn't been looking. The Ducharina's mouth didn't move. Her chin didn't drop. Only something went out of her, quietly, the way the light goes out of a window when someone inside carries the lamp away.

"Oh—no," said the boy.

He said it before the Ducharina had opened her mouth. That was the odd part. He said it so fast it fell over itself, as if he'd been answering a question, and Collette looked at the Ducharina to see what the question had been and found that she hadn't asked one.

"No, no. This is Collette. Baronetta Collette Coltress. She's Tovey's. I mean, she's here with Tovey. She's Tovey's guest. For the evening." He turned, nearly overbalanced, recovered, and indicated Count Thompson behind them with the whole of his free arm, like a man showing someone a view. "Tovey's."

"Ducharina," said Collette, and dipped, low, and came up again, thinking: *how did he know?*

The Ducharina looked at her. Then she looked at Count Thompson, who inclined his head a precise inch, and then back at the boy; and Collette watched her face come back on, the way it had gone off, as if someone had carried the lamp back in again and set it down, rather hard, on the sill.

"Of course," said the Ducharina. "How do you do, Baronetta. Cousin."

"Cousin," said Count Thompson.

And then something else came into the Ducharina's face. Collette saw it arrive. It was a small thing, and quick, and it began somewhere behind the eyes; it was the look her own mother had at the market when she saw a bolt of good cloth at the bottom of a pile and the stallholder hadn't. The Ducharina's eyes went over the boy. Not the way you look at a friend. Down, and up again: the stick, the laced trouser leg, the dark coat, the flattened hair, the face. She looked him over like a horse she was thinking of buying, and was going to buy, and was already working out how to get home.

The boy looked back at her. He looked, Collette thought, faintly alarmed, as though she'd said something; and she hadn't said anything at all.

"Trice?" he said. "What?"

"Nothing," said the Ducharina, and smiled at him, suddenly and brilliantly, and it was the first true thing she'd done since she'd turned round.

The boy's eyes went down.

Collette saw them go, and followed them, and her heart turned over.

The Ducharina's gown was dark blue and very plain, high at the neck, no lace at all, the sort of plain that costs more than lace; and on it, on a chain of silver so fine it looked like a scratch of light, lay a small flat disc of something pale. It wasn't pretty. It wasn't even jewelry, not really: it was a rough little thing, with lines cut into it, the sort of thing you'd find in a drawer and not know what it was for. On anyone else Collette wouldn't have looked at it twice. On the Ducharina's dark blue gown, in the long room, under a hundred candles, it was the most remarkable thing she'd ever seen. It made the Ducharina's eyes look bluer. They were very blue already; cornflower, Collette's mother would have said, and then sighed.

The boy wasn't looking at the Ducharina's eyes. He was looking at the disc, with his head a little on one side, the way her father looked at a horse's hoof.

"Is that—" Collette heard her own voice go up, and couldn't stop it. "Ducharina, is that one of the wizard Tor's? A *shield*? One of the amulets?"

The Ducharina looked down at it, and up, and her mouth twitched.

"It is."

"Oh." Collette put her hand to her own throat, where there was nothing but her grandmother's garnets. "Oh, I *wanted* one. Mother wrote. Twice. The second time Father put in gold, which is a great deal for us, I don't mind saying it, and they wrote back and said there weren't any. Not for anyone. Lady Arden doesn't have one. The *Harrows* don't. My cousin says even the city guard only have the—" She stopped. "How did you get one?"

The Ducharina turned her glass a little in her fingers. She looked, for a moment, exactly like a cat that has been asked, politely, where the cream went.

"He gave it to me," she said, and pointed, with one finger round the stem of the glass, at the boy.

Collette looked at him. He looked at the floor.

"He gave me the first one months ago," said the Ducharina, "before the army had any, when nobody knew what they were. This is the new one. The fourth." She touched it with one fingertip. "It's better than the ones the army has. He won't tell you that, so I will."

"It's only a bit better," said the boy, to the floor. "It's mostly the same. It's a bit faster, that's all, and I fixed the—it's mostly the same."

Collette looked from one of them to the other. A shield, a *fourth* shield, better than the army's, from his own hands, on a silver chain, on a duke's daughter, *months* ago—

"Oh," she said. "Oh, are you two—"

The Ducharina laughed. It was a real laugh, short and surprised, the kind you can't make on purpose.

"No," she said. "Heavens. We're not friends like *that*."

The boy's head came up. He looked at her, quickly, and then away, at a candle sconce, with an expression of tremendous interest in candle sconces.

"Scholar Baker is generous with his friends," said Count Thompson.

They all turned. He hadn't spoken since *cousin*. He spoke now the way he did everything, Collette was learning: plainly, at the end of a pause long enough to make you wonder if he'd heard.

"He gave me a shield," said the count. "And a flying rig. I didn't ask for either. He would give you his coat, Baronetta, if you were cold and he noticed." He paused again. "But that isn't the important thing about him."

Collette waited. The boy waited too; he looked, if anything, more worried than she was.

"The important thing," said Count Thompson, "is that when I needed it, he came at once to defend my honor. He didn't stop to ask whether it was his business. He didn't stop to ask anything." The count's face didn't change, but it seemed to Collette that it got a little stiffer, the way a door does when you lean on it from the other side. "I don't forget that. I won't."

"He did," said the Ducharina quietly. "I was there."

Collette looked at the boy. Whatever she'd expected him to do, a duke's daughter and a count on either side of him saying this kind of thing in the long room where anyone might hear, it wasn't what he did. He frowned. He looked from the count to the Ducharina and back, like a man who has been thanked for a present he can't remember giving.

"But I only hit you with a—" he said.

"Yes," said Count Thompson.

The boy opened his mouth, and shut it, and looked at the Ducharina, who looked back at him with an expression Collette couldn't read at all; and then, very slowly, he said, "Oh. Well. That's—you're welcome," in the tone of a person agreeing to something in a language he doesn't speak.

Collette didn't ask what he'd hit the count with. It was the hardest thing she'd ever not done.

Then the room changed.

It started at the far end. Collette felt it before she understood it: a stir, a turning, a soft falling-off of talk that went down the long room from the welcoming table like a hand passed over a field of barley. Heads turned. Glasses came down. All along the room a hundred people, without seeming to have decided to, faced the same way.

The boy turned with the rest. Then he went very still, and his eyes narrowed, and he looked, not at the welcoming table, but round about him, at the walls, at the ceiling, at the people nearest; searching, with an expression of keen professional interest.

"That's clever," he murmured. "I didn't feel a thing."

Collette followed his eyes round the room and back, and couldn't see what he was looking for. Then she looked at the welcoming table, and saw. The king had stood up. That was all. The king had stood up, and the room had done what rooms do when a king stands up.

She opened her mouth to tell him, and closed it again, because the herald had filled his chest.

"*His Grace Duke Eric Morgan,*" rolled the voice from the bottom of the barrel, "*and the Sitting Baroness, Her Grace Merciful Thorgood Morgan.*"

Both titles. Collette noted it, as she'd been brought up to note everything. A herald who gives a woman both her titles has been told to.

The Duke and Duchess came round from their places at the king's elbow and stood before the welcoming table: a big fair man with the shoulders of a wrestler, a little gray, and the queen's sister, who was famous for never simply coming into a room, and who stood close at his side now looking, for once, Collette thought, like someone who has been called to the front of a schoolroom and does not know why. And the king took a box from the guard behind him. A carved box, honey-colored, tied up in purple silk.

Collette's breath went in. Beside her the boy said, "Oh, good," softly, with enormous relief, as if he'd been worried it had got lost.

The king made a speech.

It was a good speech. Collette had heard a great many speeches from the far side of rooms and this was a good one; it went up and came down in the right places, and had *the kingdom* in it, and *the gratitude of the crown*, and *in these dry years*. The king untied the purple silk himself and opened the lid, and lifted out what was inside, one at a time, and held each up: two flying rigs, which Collette knew by sight now, everyone did, and two small pale discs on cords, exactly like the one on the Ducharina's dark blue gown.

"*From the Master Wizard Tor*," said the king, "*with his regard.*"

And the long room, which had been quiet, made a sound Collette had never heard it make. It wasn't applause, at first. It was a kind of murmur that went up, and up, until somebody near the front began to clap, and then everybody did.

The boy looked round at the clapping with polite surprise, as if a wedding party had gone by in the street.

"They're only spares," he said, to nobody. "I had extra."

Around them, the talk had already started. Collette heard it from all sides, low and fast. *What did they do? What can they have done?* A man behind her said *both*, as if it were an obscenity. A woman to her left said *Mercy* in a voice of pure and terrible friendship, the way you'd say it of someone you meant to call on tomorrow morning, early. *Two shields. From his hand. From the king's hand. What would it take—what would one have to—*

Collette knew what it meant, a gift like that, given like that. Anyone who'd learned the great houses by heart knew. It wasn't a present. It was a door, opened, in front of the whole court, and everyone in the room was looking at it and wondering how to get through.

She turned to say so, and saw the Ducharina's face.

The Ducharina wasn't clapping. She was looking at the front, at her mother and her father standing before the king with the rigs in their arms, and her face had gone very quiet and very intent, and her lips were moving. Count Thompson had turned to the front with everyone else, to watch his aunt and uncle; he had his back to them. The boy was at the Ducharina's elbow. Collette was at the boy's. Nobody else was near enough.

"A title," said the Ducharina, under the clapping.

She wasn't talking to Collette. She wasn't, quite, talking to the boy either; it was the voice Collette's father used over the household books, adding a column. But she'd half turned to him, and it was to him that it went.

"That's first. A title. Something with land, or it costs nothing. Uncle Richard would, if the right people asked, and after tonight they'll be asking." Her eyes didn't leave the front. "Then Mother. Mother's the easier. Father will want it to be his own idea; that can be arranged. They'd have to believe you meant it. That you—" Her eyes came round to the boy then, quick and level, and went back. "That you cared. For me. They'd have to believe that. And then they'd have to let it be you, instead of—"

She stopped.

She stopped the way you'd stop at the top of a stair in the dark, with one foot out. Collette, who had stopped breathing, waited for the foot to come down, and it didn't. The Ducharina closed her mouth on the rest of it. She took a sip from her glass, very composedly, and looked at the king.

*Instead of*, thought Collette. *Instead of whom?*

She ran down the list in her head, the way she'd been taught, every unmarried heir of the right rank and the right age, and got to the bottom and started again, and couldn't make any of them fit. It didn't matter. It was the most valuable thing she'd ever overheard in her life, and she knew it, and she held very still and held her glass and didn't drink from it, and kept it.

Then she looked at the boy.

He hadn't moved. He was looking at the Ducharina's profile with an expression Collette had last seen on a boy at her brother's school who'd been told, at the end of a very long day, that the examination had a second half.

"Wylde took his whole life," he said.

He said it very quietly. Collette didn't think it was meant for anyone. The Ducharina didn't turn; the clapping was still going, and Collette was the only one near enough to hear.

*Wylde*. She knew the story. Everybody knew the story; you were told it in the nursery, the young man spurned, the long climb, the end of it. A whole life, to rise as far as that.

And then the boy went still.

Not frightened-still, the way he'd gone in the doorway. Something else. He straightened on his stick. He folded both hands over the top of it. His face settled into an expression of perfect, patient, careful courtesy, the face of a page standing at the back of a council chamber, and stayed there.

"We'll need to start with Uncle Richard," the Ducharina was saying. She'd turned to him now, brisk, low, rapid, her glass in one hand and the fingers of the other counting. "Not tonight. Soon. When he's in a good humor, and not when anyone's just asked him for money. You'll have to be there. You'll have to be *useful*, which you are, so that's no trouble. And we'll need Aunt Constance, she'll want to have thought of it first, so we'll let her—"

"Yes," said the boy.

"—and Mother will want to talk to you. Properly. Not about orchards. You'll have to let her."

"Of course."

"You'll have to *talk*, Tor. You can't just bow at her."

"I see."

"And you're not to give anything else away to anybody until we've decided who it goes to, do you understand? Nothing. Not a plate."

"Yes, Trice. Thank you."

Collette looked at his face. It was perfectly polite. It was the politest face she had ever seen. He was looking at the Ducharina, and answering her, and his eyes were open and quite steady and fixed on a point a little to the left of the Ducharina's ear; and it was a long moment before Collette understood what was wrong with them, and then she couldn't stop seeing it.

He'd stopped blinking.

The Ducharina went on talking. At the front of the long room the clapping was dying away, and the king had sat down, and the duke and duchess were going back to their places with their arms full, and the boy stood with his hands folded on his stick, saying *yes*, and *of course*, and *thank you*, very quietly, in a voice like a closed door.
