Varley had been put beside him on purpose. She knew it the moment she found her card.

It was a small card, cream, with her name on it in the round black hand of her mother's secretary, and it sat at the twelfth place down on the left side of the long table, which was nowhere: too far from the king for honor and too near for disgrace, among the counts' wives and the marcher lords and a bishop who ate with his eyes shut. And on the card beside it, in the same black hand, it said only *Tor*.

Mama, thought Varley, and sat down, and arranged her napkin, and did not look up the table at her mother, because her mother would be expecting her to.

He came late. Everyone else was seated by then, and the first wine had gone round, and the long room had got back the roar it had had before the king stood up; and then a footman was drawing out the chair beside her, and the boy came down into it the way you'd lower a sack of flour onto a cart, carefully, all at once at the end. He put his stick against the table's edge. It slid. The footman caught it and took it away somewhere, with a face like a man carrying off a sword.

"Thank you," said the boy, to the footman's back. "Sorry."

Then he turned and saw her, and his face cleared, and he smiled. He knew her at once; she saw that. She'd been in his room only the other day, red to the ears, promising him ice from the cellars and the good pillows, and he'd thanked her for both four times. But then he went on looking, a beat longer than he should have, with a small frown between his brows, as if something in her face had put him in mind of something else and he couldn't think what.

Shy, thought Varley. She felt her ears begin to go, and stopped them. She was sixteen, and this was a dinner, and at dinners she knew exactly what to do.

"I don't believe," she said, "that we've been introduced."

He blinked at her. Then something went across his face that she liked very much: he understood that it was a game, and that she'd asked him to play it, and he put down his fork, which he hadn't used yet, and sat up straight on his chair.

"No," he said gravely. "I don't believe we have."

"Veronica Cordes." She inclined her head, exactly as far as was proper to someone with no title at all, which was not far.

"Torrance Baker." He inclined his head back, a little too far, the way people did who had learned it that week. "My friends call me Tor."

"Then you'd better call me Varley," said Varley. "Everyone does who's allowed to. Veronica's for cards and for being told off." She shook out her napkin a second time, for something to do with her hands. "I've been wanting to thank you. Properly. Not in a sickroom with Karina sitting on your knee."

"She wasn't on my—"

"Nearly. For the plates." She lowered her voice, though there was no need; the bishop's eyes were shut. "It's the hottest summer anyone can remember, and I'm the only girl on the Hill who sleeps. I sleep all night. I wake up *cold*. I have to pull the blanket up. You can't think what that's like, in this city, in the dry; Marguerite Harrow cried when I told her." She considered. "Of course, I shouldn't have told her."

"Oh," said the boy. "Good. I'm glad. You've got them on the wall?"

"With the nails. Four nails each." She leaned a little nearer. "I'll tell you something. Karina nearly didn't give them to me."

"Didn't she?"

"She had all four of them on her bed for a whole day. She lay among them like a dragon. I could hear her thinking through the wall." Varley picked up her glass, and put it down again, because it was wine and she was allowed one. "And then she worked out that they were so good, nobody needs more than two, and that four would be *greedy*. And greedy shows. So I got mine." She looked at him sideways. "You should be careful of her, you know. Karina. She'll want things. She'll always want the next one, before anyone else has the first. She'll use you for every trinket you've got."

He looked at her with an expression of mild surprise, as though she'd told him her sister was tall.

The soup came. It was a clear cold soup, the color of weak tea, with green things floating in it, and the footman who served Varley gave her half a ladle, because she was sixteen, and the one who served the boy gave him a full one, because he was a guest; and the boy looked down at his bowl, and then at her, and then he did something odd. He laid his spoon beside the bowl. He turned on his chair, the whole of him, toward her, so that his shoulder was to the bishop and his face was to her; and he took a breath, as if he were about to lift something.

He looked, Varley thought, like a man remembering his lessons.

"Do you go to school?" he asked.

Nobody had asked her that since she was nine. "Here," she said. "In the palace. They bring them in. The masters. Geometry on Tuesday, and Master Abelard for history, who smells of cloves, and a woman for the harp, which I'm hopeless at, and the theory man comes on Fridays and talks about fields until I could scream." She stopped. "I mean, I'm sure *your* theory classes are very interesting."

"No," said the boy, with feeling. "They're dreadful. Ours sits on the desk and swings his feet." He seemed pleased to have found a thing they had in common. "What would you study, if you could choose?"

"Flying," said Varley at once.

He laughed. It was a small, surprised laugh, mostly breath, but it was the first time she'd heard it, and she found she'd sat up straighter.

"I mean it," she said. "Not to *study*. To do. I want to go up. Papa's not allowed; the council voted, did you know? Lord Harrow and the rest. They say the king can't risk his neck on a harness, so he has to stand on the terrace and watch the guards go over like everybody else, and he sulked for a week. But nobody's forbidden *me*. I'm not anyone. I'm the third child." She said it without bitterness; it was a fact, like the soup. "Only there aren't any. Rigs. The army had them all, and then the city guard, and there's a list as long as this table, and everyone on it is a colonel. Nobody else is getting one. Not for any money." She tipped her head toward the top of the table, where her aunt and uncle Morgan sat with their heads together. "That's why everyone's in such a state about the box. That's why Lady Westry has been looking at Aunt Mercy all through the soup as if she wanted to eat her. Two rigs. And two shields. From your own hand, through Papa's." She let a little pause go by, the length of one breath. Karina could do it with the chin, the shy little dip. Varley had practiced it in the glass and never got it right, so she did it anyway, badly. "I don't suppose you have one about you."

"A rig?"

"In your pocket." She looked at him through her lashes, as she'd seen it done. "A very small one."

"No," said the boy. "Sorry."

He didn't notice the lashes at all. She saw him not notice them. He was looking past her, at nothing, with his eyes gone narrow and thoughtful; and then his hands came up off the cloth.

"I haven't got any left," he said. "I gave the last spares to your uncle. But I've been meaning to make new ones anyway. Different ones. Because the trouble with the ones I've got is they're all steered with the *right*." His right hand went up, flat, and tilted, and swooped, and nearly took the bishop's glass. "Like that, you see, and you lean, and you go. Which is fine if you're only going somewhere. But if you're a soldier, you want your right hand for the sword. Or a pike. Or whatever it is they use." His left hand came up and did the swoop instead, clumsily, and the right made a fist and jabbed at the air beside the salt cellar, at an invisible enemy. "So it ought to go on the left. All of it. Up and down on the left, and the turning, and you'd keep your right free and you could fight while you flew." He frowned at his left hand, which was not swooping well. "It's only a matter of moving where the field listens. It isn't hard. I just haven't done it."

Varley became aware that the table had gone quiet round them.

Not the whole table. Their bit of it. The bishop had opened his eyes. The marcher lord across from her, a big gray man with a scar through one eyebrow who had been eating his soup as if it had insulted him, had stopped with his spoon halfway, and was watching the boy's left hand. Further down, somebody said, low and quick, "*—left-handed, did you hear—*" and somebody else said "*Hush.*"

The boy didn't see any of it. He put his hands down on the cloth again.

"Anyway," he said. "When I've made them. If you'd like one."

"I'll pay for it," said Varley.

It came out before she could stop it, much too eagerly, the way things did. She went on anyway. "I've got my own money, you know. An allowance. And Grandmama left me a—I can *pay*. I don't want you to think I'm asking for it."

He looked at her as if she'd suggested he charge Rolph for breakfast.

"You're Rolph's sister," he said.

"Yes, but—"

"I'm not taking money off Rolph's *sister*." He seemed to find the idea faintly comic. "I'll put one by. The first one. With your name on, so nobody gets it by mistake." Then he thought of something else, and his face went serious. "And you'll want a shield. If you're going to fly. You mustn't go up without one; I didn't once, and I nearly—well. You mustn't. I've got a few of those still, I'll find you one. And one for Karina, or she'll mind."

Varley sat very still, so as not to do anything stupid with her face. A rig, the first one, with her name on. A shield, like the one on Trice's dress. Out loud. At table. In front of a marcher lord and a bishop and half the twelfth place down.

"Thank you," she said, and was pleased that it came out quite level.

* * *

After that it was easy. After that she could have talked to him all night.

She moved her chair, a little, under cover of the fish, so that she wouldn't have to lean across the bishop's elbow to be heard; and she told him things. She told him that the Whitcombes had come up from the country for the first time in eleven years, and that the duchess had brought her own pillows. She told him that the old Count of Salt had married his first wife's cousin, and that the first wife's family had never forgiven either of them, and that they were seated tonight at opposite ends of the room on purpose, and that it was her mother's doing, and that it was a kindness. She told him that Lady Arden collected heraldry, real heraldry, the old kind, from before the wall, and had a book of it in her morning room as thick as a doorstep, and would show it to you if you weren't careful. He listened to all of it with his head bent toward her, nodding, as if each thing were a piece of something he was going to have to build later, and asked the most extraordinary questions. "Why would you marry your wife's cousin?" he said. "Wouldn't it be awkward at harvest?"

"Why at harvest?"

"Well, that's when everybody comes over."

She laughed so suddenly that the marcher lord looked up.

He was not, she thought, at all what Karina had said. Karina had said he was sweet and simple and could be had for a smile, and Karina, as usual, had been wrong in the most useful direction. He wasn't simple. He was only attending to something else most of the time, and when he attended to you it was like standing in front of a fire.

"She doesn't use me, you know," he said, a little later, out of nothing. He was looking at his fish. "Karina."

"Doesn't she?"

"No." He said it half to himself, considering, the way you'd turn a stone over to look at the underneath. "She asks for things. Quite a lot. She *tells* you she wants them, and then she has them. But that's not the same as using somebody." He nodded, satisfied. "That's just asking."

Varley didn't know what to say to that. It seemed to her the kindest wrong thing she'd ever heard anyone say about her sister.

Then he looked up from his fish, at her, and his face was suddenly a little anxious.

"You won't start doing that, will you?" he said. "Asking like that. I'd be—" He searched for the word. "I'd be disappointed. I like talking to you."

Varley thought about the rig. The first one, with her name on. She felt her ears go, all at once, both of them, the whole way to the tips, and there was nothing at all she could do about it.

"No," she said faintly. "Of course not."

"Good," said the boy, and went back to his fish.

* * *

The courses kept coming. There was the fish, and a pie of something with walnuts, and lamb, and a bird stuffed with a smaller bird, and between them little cold things on spoons that you were meant to eat at one go; and Varley, as she always did, ate a corner of each and let the rest go back. She was sixteen and not yet the size she would be, and you couldn't eat at the king's table the way her father did, as if it were a duty to the crown. The boy did the same. He ate a mouthful, two, and looked at the rest with a sort of apologetic dismay, and laid down his knife. The footmen kept bringing him a man's portion, the way they'd bring one to anyone, and he kept looking at it as if he were going to have to carry it home.

"You can leave it," she told him, behind her hand.

"It seems a waste."

"It goes down to the kitchens. They eat it."

His face lightened. "Oh. Well, that's all right, then," he said, and left the bird with a clear conscience.

She was working the table now. It was what you did. Mama had taught her at nine, before the masters, before anything: you don't sit with the person you like best and talk to them all night, darling, you go round. Left, and right, and across, and the one past the one on the left. You find a thing to say to each. You leave none of them out. So she turned to the bishop and asked after his garden, and she turned to the marcher lord with the scar and asked after his horses, and she turned across the table to a little round woman in mauve and asked whether it had rained in the north, though she knew it hadn't, so the woman could have the pleasure of telling her.

And every time she turned back, somebody was talking to the boy.

She hadn't expected that. She'd thought he'd sit quiet, the way he had at their own small dinner the night he came, and she'd have to bring him out. But they came to him. They leaned round her. They leaned across the bishop. The marcher lord wanted to know whether a field could keep a well from going salt, because his had, all along the coast, since the dry; and the woman in mauve wanted to know whether it was true, about the river, and whether the water was really pure enough to drink, and then whether it would do anything for her husband's chest. And a lot of them, Varley noticed, when they had got him to themselves, wanted to tell him about something that was wrong.

It was the dowager who surprised her.

She'd been sitting the whole meal across and two down, very upright, in a black gown that had been good once and a cap with a long lappet; the dowager from the fen country, whom nobody had seen in town since the spring. Varley knew her by sight, as you knew everyone by sight. She'd heard the whispers when the old woman went away, which had been something about a house by the sea that was good for the lungs; and she'd heard her mother say *poor woman*, once, in the tone she used for things she wasn't going to explain. Now the dowager leaned forward, past the woman in mauve, and said to the boy, in a dry, careful, well-bred voice that had plainly not been used much lately:

"Young man. I don't suppose you know anything about damp."

The boy turned to her as if she'd offered him a cake.

"Damp?"

"My house," said the dowager, "is very low. It stands on very wet ground, close to the sea. It's very healthy, I'm told." Her mouth went thin. "The linen goes green in the press. There's a black bloom on the walls of the bedchamber that comes back the day after it's scrubbed. My books are spotting. My maid coughs." She sat back a little. "I only wondered. Everyone says you do water."

"Oh, but that's *easy*," said the boy.

Several people looked round.

"I mean—sorry—it's not easy for you, it's dreadful for you. But it's easy to *fix*." His hands had come up again; Varley moved the salt. "It's only water in the air, you see. A little. Much less than the river. Much less than the dryers. You'd only have to take it out and put it somewhere, the way you'd wring out a cloth. Over a drain, or out through the wall." He was looking at the dowager's cap, not seeing it. "A plate, I think. On the wall, like the cooling ones. In each room that's bad. And you'd want it to stop when the air's dry enough, or you'd crack the furniture, that's the only clever bit—"

"I wasn't asking you to—" the dowager began.

"I can't do it now," said the boy, apologetically, coming back to her. "I haven't got anything with me. And I'd want to think about the stopping. But I'll make you one. Some. I'll send them."

"Oh, really, there's no need. I only mentioned it. It's of no consequence."

"It is to your maid," said the boy.

The dowager stopped.

"You'll have to write it down for me," the boy went on, earnestly. "Your house, and which rooms, and how to get things to you. Please. Send a note. Here, or to the school; Rolph will know where. Because if you don't, I'll forget, I always forget, and then I'll remember in a year in the middle of something else and feel terrible, and your books will have got worse." He looked at her anxiously. "Will you? Send a note?"

The dowager looked at him for a long moment. Varley couldn't read her face. It was a face that had been arranged a long time ago and had not been rearranged since.

"Yes," she said at last. "Very well. I'll send a note."

"Thank you," said the boy, as if she'd done him a favor.

* * *

Not all of them wanted to tell him about damp.

It began with the lady on the bishop's other side, who was perhaps forty and wore a great many pearls and had a husband three places up who was deep in the lamb, and who leaned round the bishop during the bird to tell the boy that he had *very* clever hands. The boy thanked her, and looked at his hands, puzzled, as if to check. Then there was a widow in green across the table who wanted to know whether he danced, and whether he would, and when; and then a younger one, married that spring, by the ring, who laughed at everything he said whether it was funny or not, and touched his sleeve each time she did it.

The boy went red. He went red slowly, from the collar, the way he did, and kept going, and he edged back on his chair from the sleeve-touching until he was nearly in Varley's lap, and answered everyone with a courtesy so desperate it was painful to listen to.

Varley took pity on him.

"You'll have to write to Mama, you know," she said, quite loudly, to nobody in particular, in the voice she used for telling the dogs to get off the furniture. "If you want to ask him anything. Anything at all. Everyone does. My brother made it a rule."

The widow in green looked at her.

"Alphie made it a rule," said Varley, enjoying herself now, "while they were with the Debris, and then he wrote and told Mama, and Mama said she'd be *delighted*. So it all goes to her. Every word. And she's very particular." She took a sip of water. "She's already said no to Karina."

The younger lady stopped touching the sleeve.

"To Karina?" said the boy.

"At breakfast." Varley couldn't help it; she giggled. "Karina asked if she might have you. She was half joking. Only half. And Mama didn't even look up from her letters. She said, *Certainly not, darling, pass the marmalade*." She giggled again. "Karina was furious. She didn't speak all morning."

The boy looked at her. He was frowning. Varley watched him take it in, and turn it over, and arrive somewhere; she saw the moment he arrived, because his face cleared.

"She'll have wanted more plates," he said.

Varley opened her mouth, and shut it.

"Well," said the boy, and turned with great dignity to the widow in green and the lady in pearls and the young bride, who were all three looking at him, "if anybody wants anything, then. You'll have to write to Her Majesty." He said it entirely seriously, as though it were the most natural arrangement in the world, as though baker's sons from Two Rivers had always had their invitations to dance passed through the queen of Noram, and nodded to them, and went back to his bird. "That's the rule."

* * *

Dessert came on silver.

It came in shallow silver dishes, one to each place, and at first Varley thought it was sugar: a little heap of something white and fine and glittering, piled up like new snow on a step. Then the cold of it reached her. It came up off the dish into her face, actual cold, in this room, in this summer, with a hundred people and two hundred candles and the windows shut against the dust; and a little white smoke came off the heap and slid over the rim of the dish and down onto the cloth, and lay there.

It was ice. Shaved ice, shaved so fine it was nearly powder, with something dark and sweet poured over the top that had frozen where it touched. It wasn't melting. In this room it should have been water before it reached the table, and it sat there on its dish, holding its shape, smoking very faintly.

All down the table people stopped talking.

Varley put a little on her spoon and put it in her mouth, and it was gone. It didn't melt so much as vanish; there was cold, and the dark sweet taste of cherries, and then nothing at all, and her teeth ached, and she wanted more at once.

Beside her the boy had tasted his too. He was looking at the dish with his professional face on, the narrow one.

"That's very good," he said. "That's *very* cold. Somebody's got a lot of ice." He glanced round, as though he might see who.

Varley looked up the table, to her mother.

Her mother was looking straight down it. Not at Varley. At the boy. She had her own spoon in her hand, and as Varley watched she lifted her glass, a very little, half an inch off the cloth, and inclined her head toward him, and set it down again.

The boy looked over his shoulder. There was only a footman behind him.

"I think," he said doubtfully, turning back, "your mother's thanking somebody."

"Yes," said Varley.

"I didn't *make* it." He looked down at the dish. "I mean, I've never been in the kitchens. I suppose that box might have—I did put a plate in one, this afternoon. A cold one. Burks took it. But that's only keeping things cold, it's not making the ice, somebody had to make the—" He stopped, and frowned at his spoon. "It might have helped a bit," he admitted.

"Cook's been impossible all afternoon," said Varley. "One of the maids told me. She's been going round the kitchens with her hands on her hips, saying *now let them see*." She ate another spoonful, and it vanished. "Let who see, nobody knows."

* * *

The shouting started while the dishes were going out.

It came from further up, on the other side, four or five places short of the king's end: two men's voices, raised. That wasn't so strange. It was late in a long dinner and the wine had been round six times and there was a drought on, and men's voices went up at dinners; Varley had heard her father's go up at this table, once, about wheat. She turned to look, as everyone did, and saw them. One she knew at once, because everyone did: the Duke of Winchester, a great broad red-gold man with a beard like a doormat, who had a seat on the council and twenty thousand men in his county and the reputation of never losing his temper at all. The other was smaller and darker. Rochester. A baron. His wife sat beside him, fair and slight, in pale yellow, with her hand on his arm.

"—has *always* been the line," the duke was saying. "Always. My grandfather's grandfather set the stones."

"Your grandfather's grandfather set them on the wrong side of the mill race, and you know it, and so did he." Rochester's voice was thin and very clear. "The water moved. The line goes with the water. That's the law, Winchester. It's been the law since—"

"The *mill* is mine."

"The mill is on *my side of the water*."

The boy, beside her, was craning. "What are they saying? I can't hear."

"It's about a county line," said Varley. "Where it is. Whose mill. It's very boring, really, they've been at it for—"

The duke's chair went over backward.

That was the first thing. The second was the sparks.

They came off him all at once, the way a fire catches in dry bracken: off his shoulders and his hair and the backs of his hands, red, a deep, dull, angry red like the inside of a forge, crackling. Varley had seen a rage before. Everyone had; that was the dreadful thing about this summer, you'd all seen one. But she'd never been this near. The heat came down the table like an oven door opening. It came across her face, and her bare arms, and the cloth in front of her seemed to shimmer; the candle nearest the duke bent its flame flat and went out.

And he reached across the table, past Rochester, and took Rochester's wife by the throat.

Somebody screamed. Several people. The table emptied. It emptied the way a pond empties when you throw a stone into the middle, outward, all at once, chairs going over, glasses, a woman's skirts dragging a cloth half off and the silver with it. Varley found she was on her feet, and then that she'd stepped back, two steps, three; she hadn't decided to. Her legs had decided. It was the heat, and something under the heat, a pressure, a weight on the back of the neck that said *away*, *away from that*, the way your hand comes off a stove before you know it's hot. All round the room people were doing the same. They were backing to the walls. The guards had come off the walls and made a ring, a wide one, the width of the room, with their hands on their swords and their faces set; and they were not going in. They weren't going to. You didn't, with a rage. Nobody did. You held the ring, and kept the room out of it, and waited for it to burn down, and afterward you counted.

In the middle of the ring the duke held the Baroness Rochester up off her feet by the neck, with one hand, as if she were a coat. Her husband was hanging off his arm. The duke didn't seem to know he was there.

Her face was going a color Varley would not forget.

Varley looked round for her father. For Alphie. For anybody. The king was on his feet at the head of the table with two guards in front of him, and her mother's hand was on his arm, very tight. Alphie was nowhere. The heat came and came.

The boy was still sitting down.

He was the only one at the table who was. He was sitting there among the overturned chairs with his napkin in his lap, looking up the room at the duke and the woman, and his face was white, and puzzled, and appalled.

"Why isn't anybody *doing* anything?" he said.

"You can't," said Varley. Her voice came out high. "It's a rage. Tor. You can't go near a rage, nobody can, you just have to—"

He wasn't listening. She saw him not listen. He looked at the guards, the ring of them, the hands on the swords; at the people pressed to the walls; at the woman's feet, kicking, slower now. Something went across his face, a sort of terrible plain arithmetic, the face of someone adding up a column and not liking the sum.

He put his hand to his throat, where the cords were, and touched the left-hand one. Then he got up.

"Tor—"

He went. He didn't have his stick. He went up the long table toward the duke the way he'd come in to dinner, on the good leg and the bad, hopping and catching himself on chair backs, and the heat pressed on him and he didn't seem to feel it. Halfway, he put his hand to his throat again, quickly, checking, the way you pat your pocket for a key; Varley saw his fingers close on the cord and do something to the little disc on it, a turn, a press, and let go. Whether he'd turned it on or off she couldn't have said. Then he was through the ring. One of the guards put out an arm. The boy ducked under it as if it weren't there.

He got to the duke. The duke didn't see him. Varley didn't think the duke could see anything.

The boy looked up at him, all that way, the great red-gold back crackling with sparks; and then he did a thing so absurd that a woman near Varley actually laughed, one awful bark, and put her hand over her mouth.

He hit the duke in the kidney.

He did it properly, too. Alphie's fencing master would have been proud of it: feet set, weight behind it, a short hard punch up under the ribs from behind. It would have dropped a footman.

The duke didn't notice.

The boy looked at his fist. He shook it out. He said something; she was too far to hear, but she saw his lips go, and she'd have sworn it was *they'll hang me for this*. And then he set his feet again and hit the duke a second time, in the same place, harder.

Nothing.

The baroness had stopped kicking.

The boy stepped back. He looked at the duke's back for a moment the way he'd looked at the left-handed rig, with his head on one side, as if it were a problem in a book; and then he turned himself round on his good leg, and swung the splinted one.

It came up stiff as a gatepost, laced to the hip in black ribbon, with the whole of his weight behind it and no knee to stop it, and it took the Duke of Winchester across the backside with a crack like a door slamming.

The duke let go of the baroness.

She dropped. Rochester caught her, more or less, and went down with her, and dragged her backward along the floor away from the heat with her yellow skirts all bunched, and somebody ran in from the ring, bent double, and got her other arm. Varley saw her chest move. She saw it move again.

The duke turned round.

He looked down at the boy. And the boy, who came up to about the second button of his waistcoat, and had no stick, and had one leg he couldn't bend, looked back up at him, and said something Varley did hear, clear across the room, in a voice that shook from top to bottom:

"Over here. Me. Not her. *Here*."

The duke hit him.

It was a blow that would have killed him. Varley knew that as she watched it; anyone did. It came round in a great flat red arc, with the sparks streaming off the fist, and it caught the boy on the side of the head and he went across the floor like a thrown cushion, and fetched up against the leg of the table, and lay there.

Then he got up.

He got up the way he'd sat down to dinner, in stages, holding onto the table's edge; and he looked round, and found the duke, and said, "No, *here*, I'm over *here*," and limped back toward him into the heat.

That was when Varley saw the gold.

It was coming off the boy. Not off the front of him; off his sides, and his back, from the air just round him, a hand's breadth out from his coat, where he couldn't have seen it if he'd tried. Sparks. Pale, pale gold sparks, the color of straw in sunlight or of very old coins, not crackling like the duke's but fizzing, fine and bright, streaming off him behind as he went, and going out a long way, a long way out into the room before they died. And with them came a pressure. Not the duke's heat. Something else. It went through the soles of Varley's slippers. It was in the floor. It was like standing beside a mill wheel, a great one, turning, and feeling it through the boards; and her ears filled, the way they did on the tower stairs, and popped.

All along the wall people were stepping back again. Further, this time. From both of them.

The duke hit him again. And again. The boy went down, and got up, and went down, and got up, and each time he got up he said *here*, and each time the duke turned to the voice and came at him. Sometimes the boy got out of the way. He went left, every time, Varley noticed; he always went left. Sometimes he didn't, and the duke's fist came down on him in a gout of red sparks and gold, and he went sliding across the floor in the spilt wine, and lay still long enough that Varley's heart stopped, and then he'd put his hand flat on the boards and push, and get up, and say *here*.

"That's the old gold," said somebody beside Varley, under their breath.

Varley turned. It was Lady Arden, in her shawl. She'd backed against the wall with the rest and was standing there with her old hands clutched together in the fringe of it, and she wasn't looking at the duke. She was looking at the boy, at the pale fizzing gold streaming off his back, and her face was rapt and white and frightened, the face of a woman who has opened a book she knows by heart and found a page in it she's never seen.

"Nobody's thrown that," said Lady Arden, to nobody, "not since the—not since the first king's line ended. Not in my lifetime. Not in my *mother's*."

The old gold. Varley knew that. She half knew it. Master Abelard had said it once, on a Tuesday, in the long dull summer of the year she was twelve, with the clove smell coming off him and a page of colored shields open on the table; he'd tapped one of them, a plain gold one at the very top of the page, and said *and this, of course, is the*—and she'd been looking out of the window. She'd been looking at the swifts. She tried now to get the rest of the sentence back, and it wouldn't come, and the boy went down again in the wine, and she lost it altogether.

It went on and on.

She didn't know how long. The palace bell rang the hour, somewhere, very far off, and she didn't hear it start, only stop, and couldn't have said which hour it was. Nobody moved. The ring of guards stood with their hands on their swords. The king stood at the head of the table with her mother's hand on his arm. And in the middle of the room the duke went at the boy, and the boy got up, and the boy got up, and the boy got up, until it seemed to Varley that she'd been standing against that wall her whole life and would stand against it until she died, watching him get up.

And then the duke stopped.

It was quite sudden. He'd raised his fist, and it hung there; and the red sparks went thin on his shoulders, and thinner, and went out, like a fire when you put the lid on the pot. The heat went out of the room in a long breath. The duke's fist came down, slowly, by itself, as though it had got heavy. He swayed.

He looked down at the boy, who was on one knee in the wine and the broken glass, holding onto a chair.

"I say," said the Duke of Winchester, in a perfectly ordinary voice, a mild, puzzled, rather deep voice, the voice of a man who has been woken from a nap in a chair. "Are you all right, young man?"

The boy looked up at him.

"I was talking to Rochester," said the duke slowly. He put his hand to his forehead. "About the mill race. And the next thing I—" He looked at his own hand, at the knuckles, and then at the boy. "Was I *hitting* you?"

* * *

Rolph got there in time to hear the joke.

He'd been coming the whole time. He'd been coming since the chair went over, from the far end of the room, through a wall of the largest people in the kingdom, all of whom had been backing away from the heat and straight into him; and then, when the gold started, through the same people backing harder. He'd felt it before he saw it. He'd felt it the way he'd felt it in the tanners' yard, through the floor, through his shoes, like someone opening the oven door under the boards, and every hair on his arms had stood straight up, and he had said, aloud, to a marcher count's back, "Oh, *no*, not again, Baker, not *here*," and shoved.

So he came through the last of them and out into the open just as the duke said *hitting*, and found Tor on one knee in the wine, with his coat torn and a cut over one eye and the whole of his face gone the color of candle wax. Tor was looking up at the Duke of Winchester. He was shaking. Rolph could see it from ten feet: not trembling, shaking, all over, as if he were standing in a cold river.

And Tor smiled.

It was the worst smile Rolph had ever seen on him. It went on crooked and stayed there by force.

"We had a disagreement," said Tor, in a high, shaking, perfectly courteous voice, "about a lady, Your Grace." He got hold of the chair back and pulled himself up on it, one stage at a time. "And I'm afraid she's going home with me."

The duke looked at him.

He looked for a long time, with his hand still at his forehead. He had the look of a man who can feel a headache coming over the hill toward him like weather, and is going to have to stand in it for a day and a half. Rolph knew that look. He'd worn it.

"I beg your pardon?" said the duke.

But Tor had seen Rolph. His eyes came round and found him, and something happened to his face; the dreadful smile went, and what came instead was worse, because it was relief. Plain, tired relief, and resignation, the face of a man at the end of a long walk who has seen the gallows at the top of the hill and is glad, at least, that the walk is over.

He sighed. He reached up, with fingers that shook so badly it took him three tries, and lifted the cord over his head, the one with the shield on it. And he held it out to Rolph.

Rolph took it, because it was held out. He stood there in the middle of the long room with a hundred people against the walls looking at him and the ring of guards still standing and the Duke of Winchester swaying gently on his feet, and he looked down at Tor's shield amulet lying in his palm, and had absolutely no idea what he was meant to do with it.

Nothing happened.

Rolph waited for something to. So, plainly, did Tor; he stood holding his chair back with his chin up and his eyes fixed somewhere past Rolph's shoulder, waiting, with the whole of himself, for a hand on his collar. A minute went by. Somebody near the wall began, cautiously, to talk. The duke sat down, heavily, on the nearest chair that was still upright, and put his face in his hands. Somebody brought Tor his stick. Another minute.

Tor's eyes came back from past Rolph's shoulder, slowly.

"Oh," he said, under his breath. "Not in front of the guests."

Then he turned, on his stick, and limped away down the side of the room, toward the terrace doors; and Rolph, with the amulet in his fist, went after him, because there was nothing else whatever to do.

* * *

The garden was dark and smelled of hot box hedge and the dry dust that got into everything that summer. There were lamps along the gravel path, small ones, with moths. Behind them the long room glowed through its glass like a lantern, and the noise of it came out muffled, rising, a hundred people all beginning to talk at once.

Tor went as far as the first lamp and stopped. He stood there with both hands on his stick and his back to the house.

Two of the royal guard came out of the doors behind them. They came quietly, and stopped at a little distance on the gravel, and stood there. They were both big men, sergeant and corporal, purple and black, and Rolph had known the sergeant since he was eight; and neither of them, Rolph saw with a small cold shock, quite liked being here. The sergeant's hand wasn't on his sword. It was hanging very carefully beside it.

"Do you need anything?" said Rolph to Tor's back. He heard how stupid it was. He was still holding the amulet. "Water? A chair? I could get a—"

"Master Tor," said the sergeant.

Tor turned round.

"Begging your pardon, sir." The sergeant cleared his throat. "I have to ask. Do you intend to pursue this further?"

Tor looked at him.

"Pursue it?" he said. "I—" He looked at Rolph, and back. "No. No. I'll come quietly. I won't make any trouble. I'm sorry about the—I know I shouldn't have—I'll come with you now, if you like, only my leg's a bit—"

The sergeant's face did something complicated.

"Sir," he said, "I need to know if you're satisfied. With the duke. Or whether there's going to be further—" He looked for a word and found a careful one. "Further conflict. Between yourself and His Grace. The king's men will need to know how to stand."

Tor stared at him.

Rolph saw it then: all of it at once, the whole shape of the thing, the way you see a room when someone opens the shutters. The sergeant wasn't here to take anybody anywhere. He was here to find out whether there was going to be a war.

He stepped forward, beside Tor, and put on the voice.

He didn't like the voice. He never had. It was his father's at audiences and his grandfather's on the coins, and he'd spent three years at a school on the edge of nowhere teaching himself to forget it; but it was there when he wanted it, the way your arm is there, and he wanted it now.

"Sergeant," he said. "I'll answer for Master Tor, if he permits."

Tor, who looked as if he'd have permitted anything at all, nodded.

"Master Tor considers the matter closed," said Rolph. "It was never a personal quarrel on his side. He saw the Baroness Rochester attacked and went to her defense, and he did nothing more than that, and wanted nothing more. I don't believe His Grace of Winchester bears him any ill will either, nor the baroness. The room came within a breath of a death tonight, and didn't have one, and there's an end. It happens, in a summer like this." He let a little pause go by. "If His Grace should feel otherwise, in the morning, when he's himself, you'll let us know. I'd advise against it; I wouldn't care to be the duke, going that road against Master Tor. But that's a matter for His Grace's own honor, and he's free to choose it. Still, I've known His Grace all my life for a level man and a fair one. I expect you'll find this is finished, and finished in earnest."

"Sir," said the sergeant. Something went out of his shoulders. "Thank you, sir. Highness." He touched his brow, and the corporal did, and they went back up the gravel, quickly, the way men go who have something to report.

Rolph let his breath out. He held out the amulet to Tor, and when Tor only looked at it, he pushed it into his hand and closed his fingers round it and pointed at his neck.

"Put it on," he said, in his own voice. "Go on. Put it *on*, Baker."

Tor put it on. It took him a while. His hands were shaking so hard the cord kept missing his head.

* * *

His mother came out a little after that.

She came alone, which was a thing the queen of Noram almost never did, with her skirts held up off the dust and no lady behind her, and she came straight down the path to Tor without a word and put her arms round him. Tor went rigid. He stood in her arms with his stick in one hand and the other held out to the side, not touching her, like a man who's been handed a baby; and then, after a moment, something in him gave, and he put his forehead down on her shoulder, briefly, and took it away again.

"Priscilla Rochester asked me to thank you," said Rolph's mother. She held Tor off and looked at him, at the cut over his eye. "She can't, yet. She can't speak above a whisper; they've taken her home. But she asked me to say it, and I'm saying it. Her own shield came up. Not much, not enough, but enough to keep him from closing his hand all the way, until you came and gave him something else to think about." Her voice was quite steady. "It was very brave, Tor. It was the bravest thing I have seen anyone do in that room in twenty years, and I've seen some things in that room."

Tor looked at her. He looked at Rolph.

"Why haven't I been arrested?" he said.

Rolph thought for a moment that he'd misheard.

"*Arrested*?" he said. "Are you *joking*?"

"I hit a duke," said Tor. "I hit him three times. I *kicked* him. In front of everybody. Striking a noble's death, Rolph, Wensa said so, the first week, she said it's treason, and I didn't just strike him, I—" He stopped. "I kicked him in the—"

"Baker." Rolph took him by the shoulders, gently, because he looked as if he'd fall down otherwise. "Look down."

Tor looked down.

On the breast of his coat, on the deep blue nap, over his heart, there was a falcon. Silver on purple, wings spread, a shield in its talons, the size of a child's palm. It had a little wine on it.

Tor stared at it as if it had just landed there.

"That's *Father's*," said Rolph. "That's the king's own. Burks must have put it on you. While you've got that on, you didn't hit the duke. As far as anybody in that room's concerned, you didn't do anything at all. *He* did. The king. Everything you did in there, the king did it, with your hands." He let go of Tor's shoulders. "That's why the sergeant came out asking. He wasn't asking if you'd go quietly. He was asking if the crown was going to war with Winchester."

Tor's mouth opened.

"It's a very good thing," said Rolph, "that you don't hold grudges."

"Rolph."

"No, listen. Do you understand what they saw? All of them? You went in there and you went into a rage, Baker. A real one. Your own. They all saw it. I *felt* it, from the far end, through the floor, like the midden, worse than the midden. And the first couple of times that happens to somebody, sometimes they don't come out. Ever. They just stay in it until it kills them or someone kills them. That's why nobody would go near you. They weren't just keeping clear of him." He swallowed. "And then you came out, and you made a *joke*. And Winchester's got twenty thousand men, and a good county, and a seat on the council, and he hasn't got one single thing he could set against you. Not one. Not with what you can do, and what you could give Father's army if you wanted to. They all know it. If you'd said, out there, that you weren't satisfied—"

He didn't finish. He didn't need to.

"And you took your shield off," said his mother quietly. "Why did you do that?"

Tor looked at her blankly. "I didn't."

"Darling, you did. Everybody saw. You turned it off as you went in." She glanced at Rolph. "It was quite plain. Without it, there was nothing holding it in."

"I didn't take it off. I *checked* it. Twice." Tor put his hand to the disc at his throat, now, and turned it over, frowning. "And I had it. I could feel it working. It was just the same as always, it was doing what it does, the force came in and went round me and went down into the floor, you can feel it go, it goes down through your feet like water down a drain, I *felt* it—" He looked from one of them to the other. "I had a shield on."

Rolph looked at his mother. His mother looked at Rolph. Neither of them said anything at all.

"I'd like to go to my room," said Tor, into the silence. "Please. If I'm still—if I'm still allowed in the house."

His mother's face changed. Rolph had seen it change like that perhaps twice in his life.

"Of course you're still welcome in the house," she said. "Don't be absurd. You're welcome in this house as long as there's a house." She put out her hand and straightened the falcon on his breast, and brushed the wine off it with her thumb. "But not yet, I'm afraid. You have to go back in."

"Back—"

"Back in. Now. And smile, and talk to people, and let them see you." She smoothed his lapel. "They've just watched you go into a rage at the king's table, Tor, and they don't know if you've come out. You have to show them you have. That you're perfectly yourself, and perfectly calm, and nothing's happened." She smiled at him, a small, serene, terrible smile. "It will frighten exactly the people who ought to be frightened. The ones with any sense. The rest won't notice."

Tor looked at the lit glass of the long room. Rolph watched him look at it, the way he'd look at a cliff.

Then Tor got his stick under him, and straightened his coat, and went.

* * *

The room went quiet when he came in.

It went quieter than it had when the herald said his name. Rolph, coming in behind him, felt it go: a hundred conversations stopping, one after another, like doors shutting down a corridor. Tor stood just inside the glass with his stick in his hand and his coat torn and the falcon on his breast, and smiled, the crooked smile, and held it there.

And three people came at him at once.

Rolph saw them start. The Duke of Winchester, from his chair, getting up. Tovey, from the wall, where he'd been standing with his date with his face perfectly still. And from the other side of the room a man Rolph knew by sight and had never spoken to: a vast man, broad as a wagon, a head taller even than Rolph's father, with a face burned brown by the sun and hands like spades. They all came, and all of them were faster than Tor, who had started forward to meet them on his stick and covered about a yard.

The duke got there first. He stopped in front of Tor.

And went down on one knee.

There was a sound in the room, a sort of soft gasp, a hundred people breathing in. The Duke of Winchester knelt on the boards in the wine, in front of a boy in a torn coat who came up to his waistcoat button, and bowed his great red-gold head.

"Master Tor," he said. His voice was low and rough. "I ask your forgiveness. For the rage, and for what I did in it. For laying hands on you. I don't remember it; that's no excuse. I've no excuse. I ask your pardon."

Tor looked down at him. Rolph, at his shoulder, saw his face, and saw that he was more frightened now than he had been in the middle of the fight; that the kneeling duke frightened him far more than the hitting one had.

Then Tor did something Rolph had seen him do once before, at school, to Tovey. He bowed. Properly, from the waist, as low as the stick would let him.

"Please get up, Your Grace," he said. "It was nothing. Really. It ought to be nothing." He seemed to be feeling for the words. "I mean, there's nothing to forgive. Not between us. We might be friends, mightn't we, after? I'd like that. And there's no debts between friends. Not where I'm from." He hesitated. "Only—you'll have asked the baroness, too? It's her you ought to ask. She's the one that was hurt."

The duke looked up at him for a long moment.

"I'll go to her tonight," he said. "Now." He got up, slowly, a big man getting up off the floor; and he put out his hand, and Tor shook it, and the duke held on to it for a moment longer than he needed to, and looked at Tor with an expression Rolph had never seen on Winchester's face. "Thank you," he said. And he turned, and went, straight down the room and out, and the crowd parted for him.

Tovey had arrived. He didn't say anything. He took up a place at Tor's left shoulder, a little behind, where you'd stand if you thought something might come back; and stood there, with his hands behind him and his face giving nothing, and stayed.

The vast man had stopped a pace off, to let the duke go by. Now he came on. He looked down at Tor from his great height, and something in his sunburned face relaxed, satisfied, as if he'd seen what he'd come to see. He held out his hand.

Tor looked at it, and put his own into it, and it vanished.

"Ford," said the man. "Count Ford."

"Oh," said Tor. "Oh—my lord. How do you do."

"I've wanted to shake your hand for ten days." Count Ford had a slow, deep, country voice, with the vowels of the south in it. "Since the water came over the gorge. My steward sent a rider, and the rider went straight past my door and down to the river, to see it, and didn't come back up for an hour; I had to go down myself and fetch him. He was standing in it to his knees." He went on holding Tor's hand. "And the queen tells me, tonight, that there's to be food as well. That you've a plan to make up what we've lost of the crops, with your own work, given away; and that you never thought to ask a single thing of me for any of it." He shook his head, slowly. "If there's anything I can do for you, Master Tor, or any of my people, ever, you have only to say. I'm at your service. All of Ford is."

Tor had gone red again, all the way up.

"It's the king's river," he said. "Really. His Majesty asked for it, and it was his falcons, and his idea to—I only did the fields. A bit of it. I'm just glad I could help. A small part."

Count Ford looked at him for a moment longer. Then he laughed, a short low laugh like a cart going over a bridge, and let go of his hand, and clapped him on the shoulder so that he staggered, and Tovey, without a word, put out a hand and steadied him.

* * *

It went on for two more hours.

Trice knew it would. She'd known it the moment her aunt caught her eye across the room, after the duke went out, and moved one finger, half an inch, toward the boy: *go*. So she'd gone, and taken her place on his right, where Tovey wasn't, and stayed there.

They came to him in a line. They didn't call it a line. Nobody would have dreamed of calling it a line; it was only that everybody in the long room seemed to find, one after another, that they had been meaning to cross it, and that their way lay past Tor. Counts and their wives. A bishop. The ambassador with the beard. Two of the marcher lords together, who wanted to talk about the left hand and went away thoughtful. Old Lady Arden, in her shawl, who held Tor's hand in both of hers for a long while and looked into his face as if she were trying to read small print in it, and said nothing at all but *thank you, young man*, and went away. Trice watched her go and filed it.

She said every name twice. Once to Tor, under her breath, before they arrived, and once aloud, as she presented them. *Lord Pelham, his wife's the tall one, they have a daughter at the academy. Lord Pelham, may I present Master Tor.* It was a thing she was good at. She'd been doing it for her father since she was eleven. Tor said, "How do you do," and "Thank you," and "No, really, it was nothing," and bowed, and forgot every one of them the moment they'd gone, she could see him forget, it went across his face like a cloud; and she didn't mind. That was what she was there for.

She minded the rest of it more than she'd expected.

She minded that his hand was shaking, still, an hour in; that he kept it on top of his stick so nobody would see, and she saw. She minded the cut over his eye, which nobody had seen to, and the way he shifted his weight off the splinted leg when he thought no one was looking, and back onto it when he thought someone was. She minded that he didn't know, even now, what he'd done; that every time someone thanked him he looked faintly surprised and then faintly guilty, as if he'd been given someone else's coat.

And under the minding, the other thing, cool and level, adding its column: that she was standing at his side in front of the whole court, on the night he'd done it. That everyone in this room would remember who'd been at his elbow. That her mother, across the room with Aunt Constance, had seen it, and would think about it all night. That none of this was wasted.

Both things at once. She didn't see why they shouldn't be.

"You're doing very well," she told him, in the half-minute between a countess and a marshal.

"Am I?" He didn't look at her. "I've no idea who any of them are."

"That's what I'm for."

"Trice." He did look at her, then, quickly. "Thank you. Really."

She found she had nothing brisk to say to that, and was glad the marshal arrived.

It was past the first bell of the morning when her aunt finally caught Tor's eye from the head of the room and inclined her head, the same half inch she'd used over the ice: *enough. You may go.* Trice saw him see it. She saw the whole of him let go, a little, the way a rope does when the load's set down.

She walked him to the door. It was a long way, the length of the room, and the stick went slowly, and people moved aside for them as they went, the way you'd move aside for something carried. At the door, in the shadow of the great herald, who had gone off duty and stood there with his tabard over his arm like an ordinary man, she stopped him with a hand on his sleeve.

"I haven't forgotten, you know," she said, low. "What we talked about. Earlier."

He went still. Not the dreadful stillness from before dinner; a smaller one.

"Tonight helps," said Trice. "Tonight helps a great deal. Half that room would vote you a barony this minute if anyone handed them the pen." She looked at him levelly. "It's only your station, Tor. That's all. We just have to improve your station."

He went red. She watched it start at his collar and go up. But he didn't look at the floor this time, which she'd been sure he would; he didn't look at the sconces. He looked at her for a moment with an odd, steady, serious expression, as if he'd made up his mind to something. And then he shifted his stick into his left hand, and took her right in his, and bent over it, slowly, because of the leg, and very carefully, like a man who'd seen it done once and was determined to get it right, and kissed the back of her hand.

His mouth was dry and warm and it was over in a moment.

He straightened. "Good night, Trice," he said. "Thank you for the names."

Trice opened her mouth, and found that there was nothing in it.

She could feel her own face. She could feel it going, the whole of it, hot, all at once, up from her collar, exactly as his did; and she couldn't stop it, and she couldn't say anything, and the herald was looking at the ceiling with great tact. So she took her hand back, and turned, and walked away from him down the long room, quickly, not looking back, with her face burning; and left him standing in the door on his own.

* * *

Hale had the door again that night. He heard them coming a long way off: the stick, and a soft step beside it that was no step at all, and the stick again.

The count had his arm. Hale stood straight. The count didn't look at him, and Hale didn't look at the count; that had been settled, between them, without anybody saying anything, some days ago. The boy looked at Hale. He had a cut over one eye and a bruise coming up along the jaw, plum-colored, and his good coat was torn at the shoulder and stiff down one side with something dark, and he gave Hale a little nod as he went by, polite, as he always did.

"Evening, Hale," he said. "Sorry. It's late."

"Sir," said Hale.

He didn't mean to watch. But the door stood open the width of a hand, as it always did, and the lamp was lit inside, and he could see the corner of the bed and the rose chair, and the boy sitting down on the bed's edge, slowly, all at once at the end. The count knelt in front of him. He undid the black ribbon down the side of the trouser leg, the long lacing he'd done himself that afternoon, eyelet by eyelet, without hurry. Then the shoes. Then he stood and eased the torn coat off the boy's shoulders, one arm and then the other, and held it up to the lamp a moment, and turned it, and Hale saw the falcon on its breast catch the light.

"I'm sorry about the coat," said the boy. His voice was very small and very slurred. "It was somebody's."

"It was, sir," said the count. He folded it over his arm with the falcon outward. "It's done its work."

"I hit a duke, Burks."

"So I hear, sir."

"I *kicked* him."

"Yes, sir. Arms up."

The boy put his arms up, like a child, and the shirt came off over his head; and when it had come off, his arms didn't come down again, quite. They stayed up a moment, wavering. Then he toppled over sideways onto the pillows, very slowly, the way a tree goes, and lay there, and was asleep. He hadn't even got his feet up. He didn't stir when the count lifted them, one and then the splinted one, and laid them straight, and drew the sheet up to his chin, and stood a while looking down at him by the lamp.

Hale looked at the far wall of the corridor, and kept looking at it, and in a little while the lamp went down to nothing and the count came out past him without a sound and was gone, and Hale never saw which way.
