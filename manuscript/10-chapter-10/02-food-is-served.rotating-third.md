Varley had been put beside him on purpose. She knew it the moment she found her card.

It was a small card, cream, with her name on it in the round black hand of her mother's secretary, and it sat at the twelfth place down on the left side of the long table, which was nowhere: too far from the king for honor and too near for disgrace, among the counts' wives and the marcher lords and a bishop who ate with his eyes shut. And on the card beside it, in the same black hand, it said only *Tor*.

Mama, thought Varley, and sat down, and arranged her napkin, and did not look up the table at her mother, because her mother would be expecting her to.

He came late. Everyone else was seated by then, and the first wine had gone round, and the long room had got back the roar it had had before the king stood up; and then a footman was drawing out the chair beside her, and the boy came down into it the way you'd lower a sack of flour onto a cart, carefully, all at once at the end. He put his stick against the table's edge. It slid. The footman caught it and took it away somewhere, with a face like a man carrying off a sword.

"Thank you," said the boy, to the footman's back. "Sorry."

Then he turned and saw her, and his face cleared, and he smiled. He knew her at once; she saw that. She'd been in his room only the other day, red to the ears, promising him ice from the cellars and the good pillows, and he'd thanked her for both four times. But then he went on looking, a beat longer than he should have, with a small frown between his brows, as if something in her face had put him in mind of something else and he couldn't think what.

Shy, thought Varley. She felt her ears begin to go, and stopped them. She was sixteen, and this was a dinner, and at dinners she knew exactly what to do.

"I don't believe," she said, "that we've been introduced."

He blinked at her. Then something went across his face that she liked very much: he understood that it was a game, and that she'd asked him to play it, and he put down his fork, which he hadn't used yet, and sat up straight on his chair.

"No," he said gravely. "I don't believe we have."

"Veronica Cordes." She inclined her head, exactly as far as was proper to someone with no title at all, which was not far.

"Torrance Baker." He inclined his head back, a little too far, the way people did who had learned it that week. "My friends call me Tor."

"Then you'd better call me Varley," said Varley. "Everyone does who's allowed to. Veronica's for cards and for being told off." She shook out her napkin a second time, for something to do with her hands. "I've been wanting to thank you. Properly. Not in a sickroom with Karina sitting on your knee."

"She wasn't on my—"

"Nearly. For the plates." She lowered her voice, though there was no need; the bishop's eyes were shut. "It's the hottest summer anyone can remember, and I'm the only girl on the Hill who sleeps. I sleep all night. I wake up *cold*. I have to pull the blanket up. You can't think what that's like, in this city, in the dry; Marguerite Harrow cried when I told her." She considered. "Of course, I shouldn't have told her."

"Oh," said the boy. "Good. I'm glad. You've got them on the wall?"

"With the nails. Four nails each." She leaned a little nearer. "I'll tell you something. Karina nearly didn't give them to me."

"Didn't she?"

"She had all four of them on her bed for a whole day. She lay among them like a dragon. I could hear her thinking through the wall." Varley picked up her glass, and put it down again, because it was wine and she was allowed one. "And then she worked out that they were so good, nobody needs more than two, and that four would be *greedy*. And greedy shows. So I got mine." She looked at him sideways. "You should be careful of her, you know. Karina. She'll want things. She'll always want the next one, before anyone else has the first. She'll use you for every trinket you've got."

He looked at her with an expression of mild surprise, as though she'd told him her sister was tall.

The soup came. It was a clear cold soup, the color of weak tea, with green things floating in it, and the footman who served Varley gave her half a ladle, because she was sixteen, and the one who served the boy gave him a full one, because he was a guest; and the boy looked down at his bowl, and then at her, and then he did something odd. He laid his spoon beside the bowl. He turned on his chair, the whole of him, toward her, so that his shoulder was to the bishop and his face was to her; and he took a breath, as if he were about to lift something.

He looked, Varley thought, like a man remembering his lessons.

"Do you go to school?" he asked.

Nobody had asked her that since she was nine. "Here," she said. "In the palace. They bring them in. The masters. Geometry on Tuesday, and Master Abelard for history, who smells of cloves, and a woman for the harp, which I'm hopeless at, and the theory man comes on Fridays and talks about fields until I could scream." She stopped. "I mean, I'm sure *your* theory classes are very interesting."

"No," said the boy, with feeling. "They're dreadful. Ours sits on the desk and swings his feet." He seemed pleased to have found a thing they had in common. "What would you study, if you could choose?"

"Flying," said Varley at once.

He laughed. It was a small, surprised laugh, mostly breath, but it was the first time she'd heard it, and she found she'd sat up straighter.

"I mean it," she said. "Not to *study*. To do. I want to go up. Papa's not allowed; the council voted, did you know? Lord Harrow and the rest. They say the king can't risk his neck on a harness, so he has to stand on the terrace and watch the guards go over like everybody else, and he sulked for a week. But nobody's forbidden *me*. I'm not anyone. I'm the third child." She said it without bitterness; it was a fact, like the soup. "Only there aren't any. Rigs. The army had them all, and then the city guard, and there's a list as long as this table, and everyone on it is a colonel. Nobody else is getting one. Not for any money." She tipped her head toward the top of the table, where her aunt and uncle Morgan sat with their heads together. "That's why everyone's in such a state about the box. That's why Lady Westry has been looking at Aunt Mercy all through the soup as if she wanted to eat her. Two rigs. And two shields. From your own hand, through Papa's." She let a little pause go by, the length of one breath. Karina could do it with the chin, the shy little dip. Varley had practiced it in the glass and never got it right, so she did it anyway, badly. "I don't suppose you have one about you."

"A rig?"

"In your pocket." She looked at him through her lashes, as she'd seen it done. "A very small one."

"No," said the boy. "Sorry."

He didn't notice the lashes at all. She saw him not notice them. He was looking past her, at nothing, with his eyes gone narrow and thoughtful; and then his hands came up off the cloth.

"I haven't got any left," he said. "I gave the last spares to your uncle. But I've been meaning to make new ones anyway. Different ones. Because the trouble with the ones I've got is they're all steered with the *right*." His right hand went up, flat, and tilted, and swooped, and nearly took the bishop's glass. "Like that, you see, and you lean, and you go. Which is fine if you're only going somewhere. But if you're a soldier, you want your right hand for the sword. Or a pike. Or whatever it is they use." His left hand came up and did the swoop instead, clumsily, and the right made a fist and jabbed at the air beside the salt cellar, at an invisible enemy. "So it ought to go on the left. All of it. Up and down on the left, and the turning, and you'd keep your right free and you could fight while you flew." He frowned at his left hand, which was not swooping well. "It's only a matter of moving where the field listens. It isn't hard. I just haven't done it."

Varley became aware that the table had gone quiet round them.

Not the whole table. Their bit of it. The bishop had opened his eyes. The marcher lord across from her, a big gray man with a scar through one eyebrow who had been eating his soup as if it had insulted him, had stopped with his spoon halfway, and was watching the boy's left hand. Further down, somebody said, low and quick, "*—left-handed, did you hear—*" and somebody else said "*Hush.*"

The boy didn't see any of it. He put his hands down on the cloth again.

"Anyway," he said. "When I've made them. If you'd like one."

"I'll pay for it," said Varley.

It came out before she could stop it, much too eagerly, the way things did. She went on anyway. "I've got my own money, you know. An allowance. And Grandmama left me a—I can *pay*. I don't want you to think I'm asking for it."

He looked at her as if she'd suggested he charge Rolph for breakfast.

"You're Rolph's sister," he said.

"Yes, but—"

"I'm not taking money off Rolph's *sister*." He seemed to find the idea faintly comic. "I'll put one by. The first one. With your name on, so nobody gets it by mistake." Then he thought of something else, and his face went serious. "And you'll want a shield. If you're going to fly. You mustn't go up without one; I didn't once, and I nearly—well. You mustn't. I've got a few of those still, I'll find you one. And one for Karina, or she'll mind."

Varley sat very still, so as not to do anything stupid with her face. A rig, the first one, with her name on. A shield, like the one on Trice's dress. Out loud. At table. In front of a marcher lord and a bishop and half the twelfth place down.

"Thank you," she said, and was pleased that it came out quite level.

* * *

After that it was easy. After that she could have talked to him all night.

She moved her chair, a little, under cover of the fish, so that she wouldn't have to lean across the bishop's elbow to be heard; and she told him things. She told him that the Whitcombes had come up from the country for the first time in eleven years, and that the duchess had brought her own pillows. She told him that the old Count of Salt had married his first wife's cousin, and that the first wife's family had never forgiven either of them, and that they were seated tonight at opposite ends of the room on purpose, and that it was her mother's doing, and that it was a kindness. She told him that Lady Arden collected heraldry, real heraldry, the old kind, from before the wall, and had a book of it in her morning room as thick as a doorstep, and would show it to you if you weren't careful. He listened to all of it with his head bent toward her, nodding, as if each thing were a piece of something he was going to have to build later, and asked the most extraordinary questions. "Why would you marry your wife's cousin?" he said. "Wouldn't it be awkward at harvest?"

"Why at harvest?"

"Well, that's when everybody comes over."

She laughed so suddenly that the marcher lord looked up.

He was not, she thought, at all what Karina had said. Karina had said he was sweet and simple and could be had for a smile, and Karina, as usual, had been wrong in the most useful direction. He wasn't simple. He was only attending to something else most of the time, and when he attended to you it was like standing in front of a fire.

"She doesn't use me, you know," he said, a little later, out of nothing. He was looking at his fish. "Karina."

"Doesn't she?"

"No." He said it half to himself, considering, the way you'd turn a stone over to look at the underneath. "She asks for things. Quite a lot. She *tells* you she wants them, and then she has them. But that's not the same as using somebody." He nodded, satisfied. "That's just asking."

Varley didn't know what to say to that. It seemed to her the kindest wrong thing she'd ever heard anyone say about her sister.

Then he looked up from his fish, at her, and his face was suddenly a little anxious.

"You won't start doing that, will you?" he said. "Asking like that. I'd be—" He searched for the word. "I'd be disappointed. I like talking to you."

Varley thought about the rig. The first one, with her name on. She felt her ears go, all at once, both of them, the whole way to the tips, and there was nothing at all she could do about it.

"No," she said faintly. "Of course not."

"Good," said the boy, and went back to his fish.

* * *

The courses kept coming. There was the fish, and a pie of something with walnuts, and lamb, and a bird stuffed with a smaller bird, and between them little cold things on spoons that you were meant to eat at one go; and Varley, as she always did, ate a corner of each and let the rest go back. She was sixteen and not yet the size she would be, and you couldn't eat at the king's table the way her father did, as if it were a duty to the crown. The boy did the same. He ate a mouthful, two, and looked at the rest with a sort of apologetic dismay, and laid down his knife. The footmen kept bringing him a man's portion, the way they'd bring one to anyone, and he kept looking at it as if he were going to have to carry it home.

"You can leave it," she told him, behind her hand.

"It seems a waste."

"It goes down to the kitchens. They eat it."

His face lightened. "Oh. Well, that's all right, then," he said, and left the bird with a clear conscience.

She was working the table now. It was what you did. Mama had taught her at nine, before the masters, before anything: you don't sit with the person you like best and talk to them all night, darling, you go round. Left, and right, and across, and the one past the one on the left. You find a thing to say to each. You leave none of them out. So she turned to the bishop and asked after his garden, and she turned to the marcher lord with the scar and asked after his horses, and she turned across the table to a little round woman in mauve and asked whether it had rained in the north, though she knew it hadn't, so the woman could have the pleasure of telling her.

And every time she turned back, somebody was talking to the boy.

She hadn't expected that. She'd thought he'd sit quiet, the way he had at their own small dinner the night he came, and she'd have to bring him out. But they came to him. They leaned round her. They leaned across the bishop. The marcher lord wanted to know whether a field could keep a well from going salt, because his had, all along the coast, since the dry; and the woman in mauve wanted to know whether it was true, about the river, and whether the water was really pure enough to drink, and then whether it would do anything for her husband's chest. And a lot of them, Varley noticed, when they had got him to themselves, wanted to tell him about something that was wrong.

It was the dowager who surprised her.

She'd been sitting the whole meal across and two down, very upright, in a black gown that had been good once and a cap with a long lappet; the dowager from the fen country, whom nobody had seen in town since the spring. Varley knew her by sight, as you knew everyone by sight. She'd heard the whispers when the old woman went away, which had been something about a house by the sea that was good for the lungs; and she'd heard her mother say *poor woman*, once, in the tone she used for things she wasn't going to explain. Now the dowager leaned forward, past the woman in mauve, and said to the boy, in a dry, careful, well-bred voice that had plainly not been used much lately:

"Young man. I don't suppose you know anything about damp."

The boy turned to her as if she'd offered him a cake.

"Damp?"

"My house," said the dowager, "is very low. It stands on very wet ground, close to the sea. It's very healthy, I'm told." Her mouth went thin. "The linen goes green in the press. There's a black bloom on the walls of the bedchamber that comes back the day after it's scrubbed. My books are spotting. My maid coughs." She sat back a little. "I only wondered. Everyone says you do water."

"Oh, but that's *easy*," said the boy.

Several people looked round.

"I mean—sorry—it's not easy for you, it's dreadful for you. But it's easy to *fix*." His hands had come up again; Varley moved the salt. "It's only water in the air, you see. A little. Much less than the river. Much less than the dryers. You'd only have to take it out and put it somewhere, the way you'd wring out a cloth. Over a drain, or out through the wall." He was looking at the dowager's cap, not seeing it. "A plate, I think. On the wall, like the cooling ones. In each room that's bad. And you'd want it to stop when the air's dry enough, or you'd crack the furniture, that's the only clever bit—"

"I wasn't asking you to—" the dowager began.

"I can't do it now," said the boy, apologetically, coming back to her. "I haven't got anything with me. And I'd want to think about the stopping. But I'll make you one. Some. I'll send them."

"Oh, really, there's no need. I only mentioned it. It's of no consequence."

"It is to your maid," said the boy.

The dowager stopped.

"You'll have to write it down for me," the boy went on, earnestly. "Your house, and which rooms, and how to get things to you. Please. Send a note. Here, or to the school; Rolph will know where. Because if you don't, I'll forget, I always forget, and then I'll remember in a year in the middle of something else and feel terrible, and your books will have got worse." He looked at her anxiously. "Will you? Send a note?"

The dowager looked at him for a long moment. Varley couldn't read her face. It was a face that had been arranged a long time ago and had not been rearranged since.

"Yes," she said at last. "Very well. I'll send a note."

"Thank you," said the boy, as if she'd done him a favor.

* * *

Not all of them wanted to tell him about damp.

It began with the lady on the bishop's other side, who was perhaps forty and wore a great many pearls and had a husband three places up who was deep in the lamb, and who leaned round the bishop during the bird to tell the boy that he had *very* clever hands. The boy thanked her, and looked at his hands, puzzled, as if to check. Then there was a widow in green across the table who wanted to know whether he danced, and whether he would, and when; and then a younger one, married that spring, by the ring, who laughed at everything he said whether it was funny or not, and touched his sleeve each time she did it.

The boy went red. He went red slowly, from the collar, the way he did, and kept going, and he edged back on his chair from the sleeve-touching until he was nearly in Varley's lap, and answered everyone with a courtesy so desperate it was painful to listen to.

Varley took pity on him.

"You'll have to write to Mama, you know," she said, quite loudly, to nobody in particular, in the voice she used for telling the dogs to get off the furniture. "If you want to ask him anything. Anything at all. Everyone does. My brother made it a rule."

The widow in green looked at her.

"Alphie made it a rule," said Varley, enjoying herself now, "while they were with the Debris, and then he wrote and told Mama, and Mama said she'd be *delighted*. So it all goes to her. Every word. And she's very particular." She took a sip of water. "She's already said no to Karina."

The younger lady stopped touching the sleeve.

"To Karina?" said the boy.

"At breakfast." Varley couldn't help it; she giggled. "Karina asked if she might have you. She was half joking. Only half. And Mama didn't even look up from her letters. She said, *Certainly not, darling, pass the marmalade*." She giggled again. "Karina was furious. She didn't speak all morning."

The boy looked at her. He was frowning. Varley watched him take it in, and turn it over, and arrive somewhere; she saw the moment he arrived, because his face cleared.

"She'll have wanted more plates," he said.

Varley opened her mouth, and shut it.

"Well," said the boy, and turned with great dignity to the widow in green and the lady in pearls and the young bride, who were all three looking at him, "if anybody wants anything, then. You'll have to write to Her Majesty." He said it entirely seriously, as though it were the most natural arrangement in the world, as though baker's sons from Two Rivers had always had their invitations to dance passed through the queen of Noram, and nodded to them, and went back to his bird. "That's the rule."

* * *

Dessert came on silver.

It came in shallow silver dishes, one to each place, and at first Varley thought it was sugar: a little heap of something white and fine and glittering, piled up like new snow on a step. Then the cold of it reached her. It came up off the dish into her face, actual cold, in this room, in this summer, with a hundred people and two hundred candles and the windows shut against the dust; and a little white smoke came off the heap and slid over the rim of the dish and down onto the cloth, and lay there.

It was ice. Shaved ice, shaved so fine it was nearly powder, with something dark and sweet poured over the top that had frozen where it touched. It wasn't melting. In this room it should have been water before it reached the table, and it sat there on its dish, holding its shape, smoking very faintly.

All down the table people stopped talking.

Varley put a little on her spoon and put it in her mouth, and it was gone. It didn't melt so much as vanish; there was cold, and the dark sweet taste of cherries, and then nothing at all, and her teeth ached, and she wanted more at once.

Beside her the boy had tasted his too. He was looking at the dish with his professional face on, the narrow one.

"That's very good," he said. "That's *very* cold. Somebody's got a lot of ice." He glanced round, as though he might see who.

Varley looked up the table, to her mother.

Her mother was looking straight down it. Not at Varley. At the boy. She had her own spoon in her hand, and as Varley watched she lifted her glass, a very little, half an inch off the cloth, and inclined her head toward him, and set it down again.

The boy looked over his shoulder. There was only a footman behind him.

"I think," he said doubtfully, turning back, "your mother's thanking somebody."

"Yes," said Varley.

"I didn't *make* it." He looked down at the dish. "I mean, I've never been in the kitchens. I suppose that box might have—I did put a plate in one, this afternoon. A cold one. Burks took it. But that's only keeping things cold, it's not making the ice, somebody had to make the—" He stopped, and frowned at his spoon. "It might have helped a bit," he admitted.

"Cook's been impossible all afternoon," said Varley. "One of the maids told me. She's been going round the kitchens with her hands on her hips, saying *now let them see*." She ate another spoonful, and it vanished. "Let who see, nobody knows."
