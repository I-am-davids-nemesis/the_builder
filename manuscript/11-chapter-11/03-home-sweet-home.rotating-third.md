They came down on the green because there was nowhere else to come down, and they hadn't finished doing it when the door of the house by the pump banged open and a man came out at a run.

Trice had counted the village from the air, because she counted things. Twenty-six roofs, if you allowed the two sheds; one chapel, square, of gray stone gone green at the footings, with a bell in an open frame on top instead of a spire; one pump; one green, cropped close; one cow, which had looked up at seven people and ten trunks descending out of a clear noon sky and gone back to eating, and which Trice found she respected. The houses were timber, mostly, silvered with weather, on footings of river stone, and their roofs were shingle. Gardens behind every one: beans on poles, cabbages, a row of hollyhocks taller than a man. The two rivers came in from either side of the gap in the forest, one brown and quick, one slow and green, and met below the chapel with a long soft sound like somebody letting out a breath. Over all of it, the smell of the bakery chimney, which had been coming up to meet them for the last half mile: bread, and woodsmoke, and something sweet and dark that had gone just slightly too far at the edges.

She was a foot off the grass, setting herself down gently, the way you'd set down a full glass, when the running man arrived.

He was long and lean and brown, in shirtsleeves and a leather waistcoat, with a cooper's mallet in one hand. He saw the mallet at the same moment Trice did, and put it behind his back, and then didn't know what to do with the hand that was behind his back, and bowed. He did it in sections, the way a man takes down a ladder: first the head, then the shoulders, then, after some thought, the rest. Duke Eric, who had come down hard and was still standing on one leg trying to remember how the other one worked, bowed back. The cow watched.

Then the man spoke.

Trice had been brought up to hear four accents of Noram and be polite in all of them. She had the Hill, and the court, which was the Hill with its collar done up; the river cant Sara had taught her at fourteen, in the dormitory after the lamps were out; and the flat, careful speech of the northern counties, which her mother's stewards used, and which she had learned so as to know when they were lying. She had expected Two Rivers to be a fifth, and had spent some part of the flight getting ready for it.

It wasn't. It came out of the man in a long warm run, all hills and hollows, and the vowels went where they liked. She caught *lords* in it, near the start, or something with *lords* inside it. She caught *ladies*, or nearly. Then it went away from her entirely, like a fish turning in a stream, and she was left with the shape of it and nothing in her hands.

"He says welcome," said Tor, beside her. He'd come down sitting, as he'd gone up, with his splinted leg out in front of him and his stick through his belt, and he was struggling upright on it now with his face gone pink and bright. "Welcome, Lords and Ladies, to Two Rivers. They're very happy to have you." He paused. "That's the polite part. There was some more about the cow. He's sorry about the cow."

"Tell him the cow's lovely," said Rolph.

But the man wasn't looking at Rolph. He was looking at Tor, and as Trice watched, the look changed. It went from a man looking at a stranger in silk, a small one, with a stick and fine leather trousers, to a man looking at a stranger who reminded him of somebody, to a man whose whole long face had dropped open like a cellar door.

He said something. It was one word, and it was *Tor*.

Then a great deal more, and this time Trice noticed what she should have noticed the first time: that it wasn't the same speech. It wasn't the same language at all, or not quite. The words he'd used for the duke had stood up straight and kept their hats on. These ran, and leaned on each other, and laughed in the middle, and the vowels came down flat and warm, as if he'd put his arm round Tor's neck with his voice. It had changed between one breath and the next because the person he was talking to had changed. Trice, who had spent ten years learning to alter a sentence by a hair for a countess as against a baroness, stood in her silk on the village green and felt, quite distinctly, like a beginner.

Tor laughed. It was the laugh he had when something surprised him into it, short and helpless, and he answered the man in the same tongue, and it came out of him as if it had been waiting behind his teeth all summer.

"He wants to know," said Tor, turning back to them, wiping his eyes, "if I'm your baker. If you've taken me on. He thinks you've hired me to do your bread, and that's why I'm here with you. Dressed up." He looked down at his silk shirt, as if it had betrayed him. "He says it's a good place. He says he always knew I'd do well."

"Could we?" said Duke Eric, interested. "Hire him? For the bread?"

"Eric," said his wife.

"I only asked."

Tor had turned back to the man and was talking again, and now he was gesturing at them, one after another, with the hand that wasn't on his stick. Trice heard their names go by like stones dropped in a stream, the only solid things in it. *Morgan. Morgan.* A long phrase after her father's name and a shorter one after her mother's, and then *Barony*, she was almost sure, which meant Tor had remembered. *Tovey*, and something after it that made the man bow again, all the way down in a single piece this time. *Rolph*, with nothing after it but a short warm word. *Sara*, and a word Trice thought might be *trade*. And then Tor stopped, and turned, and held his hand out toward her, and said her name.

*Patricia.*

He said it differently from the others. Slower. As if he'd been saving it.

And after it, a word she didn't know, and the man's eyebrows went up to his hair, and he looked at Trice, all of her, from her boots to her pinned-up hair and down again, and then at Tor, and then at Trice, and then at Tor; and Trice, who did not know the word, knew perfectly well what it was. She felt her ears go hot, and looked at the chapel tower, which had a crack running up it like a hair in a teacup.

"I told him who everybody was," said Tor, to the party at large, rather fast. "Your Grace, and the Baroness, I said Baroness, Trice, I remembered. And Count Tovey. And Sara, Sara's a merchant, I said, from the city, which is true. And my roommate, Rolph."

Rolph turned his head slowly.

"Your what?"

"Roommate." Tor looked worried. "Shouldn't I have? I couldn't think how to say the other thing. There isn't a word for it here. Or there is, but it's the one for the king in the hymns, and I didn't want him to think—it would have been a lot, Rolph, all at once, on top of the cow. Sorry. I can go back and—"

"No," said Rolph. "No. Don't you dare." His whole face had opened up, like a window thrown wide on a warm day. He strode across the grass to the man with the mallet and took his free hand and shook it, enormously, up and down. "Rolph," he said. "Just Rolph. Roommate. Delighted."

The man looked up at him, a long way up, and said something kind and baffled.

"He says you're very big," said Tor.

"I am," said Rolph, who had never in his life sounded so happy about anything.

"And me?" said Trice. She hadn't meant to ask it. It came out anyway, in a voice she didn't usually let out of the house.

Tor went pink again. "I told him last," he said. "On purpose." And then, as if that might not have been clear: "Because you were the best part."

Sara, at Trice's elbow, made a very small noise, and Trice didn't look at her.

The rest of it went quickly. Tor said something more to the man, with the word for *father* in it and the word for *mother*, Trice was nearly sure, and pointed across the green; and the man pointed too, at the house with the smoking chimney, as if Tor might have forgotten where it was, and clapped Tor on the shoulder, gently, the way you'd pat a loaf to see if it was done. Then he bowed to all of them again, in sections, and remembered the mallet behind his back, and turned and ran off across the green, the way he'd come, shouting. He shouted at the first house, and a woman came out of it with a baby on her hip. He shouted at the second, and two men came out of a shed. By the time he reached the chapel there were faces in every window round the green, and a small boy had climbed onto the pump.

"That's Tom," said Tor. "He's the mayor. He's gone to tell everybody."

"Everybody?" said Sara.

"There's not that many." Tor considered. "He'll have done it by supper. Come on. It's this way." He set off across the green at his fastest hop, the stick going down and the good leg after it, and the luggage plates lifted off the grass behind him one by one and followed in a long line, trunks and all, at the height of a table, so that the cow at last stopped eating and watched them go with her jaw still. Trice followed, with her parents behind her, and Tovey, who walked through Two Rivers the way he walked through everything, as if he'd been sent to inspect it and found it adequate.

The bakery was the biggest house on the green, and it wasn't big. Long and low, timber on a footing of round river stones, with a shingle roof gone silver and soft-looking as a cat; the chimney was stone, and broader than the house seemed to want, and the smoke went up out of it straight and white. The door was green, and had been painted green a great many times, and stood a little open, and out of it came the smell that had met them in the air: bread, and hot sugar, and fruit cooked down to something nearly black. There was a bench by the door, worn into two hollows by people sitting on it. There was a window full of loaves. To one side, under a walnut tree, stood a long table of planks with a bench down either side, and beyond it a yard, and a woodpile, and a well with a bucket on a chain, and a small shed with a moon cut in its door.

Trice looked at the moon in the door, and thought of the house in Gilder's Row, three windows wide, with its worn stair carpet, which she had been ashamed to let her friends call at; and for a moment she had to look at the woodpile instead.

The plates set the trunks down in a heap by the bench, one on another, ten deep, and settled to the grass beneath them, and were still.

Tor reached for the door.

It burst open before he got there, and three children came out of it together, as if the house had sneezed them.

They hit Tor all at once. Two of them were the same size, a boy and a girl, eleven or twelve, both brown-haired, both with flour on them, and they took him round the middle from either side; and the third was a girl of six or so, with a smear of something purple down her front, who went straight for his good leg and wrapped herself round it like ivy round a post. Tor said, "*Oof*," and went backward, stick and all, and the splinted leg went out sideways, and for one long moment he hung there over the doorstep at an angle no builder would have allowed; and then Rolph lunged, trod on Trice's foot, said "Sorry, sorry," grabbed a great fistful of the back of Tor's silk shirt, and hauled. Tor came upright like a bucket out of a well, with the shirt pulled half out of his trousers and up round his ears, and Rolph, overbalancing the other way, sat down on the bench by the door, hard, still holding on, so that for a moment the two of them were joined by a long taut stretch of silk like a pair of washerwomen folding a sheet.

"Got him," said Rolph.

The children hadn't noticed any of it. They were all talking at once, in the same tongue Tom had used, only faster and higher and with no hats on at all, and Tor was talking back, laughing, with his face buried in the top of the little one's head; and the twins—they were twins, Trice saw now, and she saw something else, that they came up to Tor's shoulder already, both of them, at twelve, and would be past him by next summer—the twins were saying his name over and over, *Tor, Tor*, the way you'd knock on a door you'd been told was locked.

Then the girl looked up past Tor's ear and saw Duke Eric.

It went through the three of them like wind through barley. The little one let go of Tor's leg and got behind it instead, so that only one eye and a lot of hair showed. The twins took a step back together, without looking at each other, and the girl put her hand on her brother's sleeve, and they stood shoulder to shoulder in the doorway and looked at the seven strangers on their step with the flat, careful look of village children who have been told never to go near a horse they don't know. Their eyes went from Eric's size to Tovey's black, to Rolph's height, to the pile of trunks, to the luggage plates lying on the grass under them, to Trice; and stopped there, and stayed.

"These are Terry and Tara," said Tor, a little breathless, with his hand on the back of the little one's head. "And this is Taman. Tam. She's the youngest. Say hello, Tam."

Tam didn't say hello. Tam got further behind the leg.

"They're my friends," said Tor, in the village tongue, and then, Trice guessed, more or less the same again in more words, crouching as far as the splint would let him. "From school. Honestly. And this is—this one's—"

"Tor?"

A man had come into the doorway behind the children.

He filled it, nearly. He was broad and thick through the chest and not very tall, and he wore a long apron that had been white that morning, over a shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbow; his forearms were floured to the elbow, and there was flour in his eyebrows and in the short brown beard going gray along the jaw, and one long white handprint on his hip where he'd wiped his hand without thinking. He had a slow, wide, weathered face, the kind that takes a long time to decide on an expression and then keeps it. The one it decided on, looking down at Tor, was so plain and so whole that Trice, who had a long list of things to say to Tor's father and had been keeping it in order since the capital, found that she'd forgotten every item on it.

"Father," said Tor.

The man came down the step and put his arms round him, children and all, the stick caught somewhere in the middle, and held on.

Then he let go, and looked at the strangers, and Trice saw him take them in the way the twins had: the size, the silk, the trunks. He wiped his hand on his apron, and looked at it, and wiped it again.

Tor did the introductions a second time. He did them better. He'd had practice. Duke Eric, Duke of Morgan, and the Sitting Baroness, Mercy, Duchess, his wife—he got tangled there and came out the other side—and Count Tovey, and Sara, and Rolph, his roommate; and last, again, *Patricia*, with the same slowness in it, and the same word after.

Tor's father looked at her for a moment. Then he held out his hand to her father, floured as it was, and thought better of it halfway, and began to take it back; and Duke Eric reached out and took it anyway and shook it, flour and all, with every sign of enjoying it, and left a white print on his own silk cuff and didn't look at it once. Trice loved him very much, briefly, and filed it.

Her mother shook hands too. She did it the way she did everything, as if she'd been doing it all her life, and she held on a moment longer than she had to, and looked into the baker's face as if she were reading a ledger she meant to buy.

The baker said something to Tor, in the tongue. It was low and thick and slow, all the corners worn off it, and Trice didn't catch so much as *lords*.

"He says Mother's got the pies in," said Tor. "Blackberry. She can't come out yet, she's sorry, or they'll burn; they're nearly done. And he says would you like cider, and come and sit down, out of the sun." He pointed at the long table under the walnut. "There. It's nicer than inside. Inside's mostly oven."

"Cider," said Duke Eric, with deep feeling.

They went to the table. It was old and gray and splintery at the edges, scrubbed pale down the middle, and the benches either side had been mended so often with odd pieces of wood that they looked like a patchwork. Eric sat on the far side of it, facing the bakery, and the bench groaned under him and held; he patted it, as if to thank it. Trice's mother took the near side, across from him, with her back to the bakery door and her face to the green, and put her hands flat on the old wood and looked down the length of it with an expression Trice couldn't read. Tovey folded himself down at the end. Rolph sat astride the bench like a man on a horse. Sara sat next to Trice and leaned against her, very slightly, the way she used to in the dormitory, and said nothing at all, and Trice was grateful.

Tor's father was going round the side of the house. Tor went after him, hop and stick, and stopped by the trunks, and touched the edge of one of the luggage plates with his foot.

It rose off the grass, and slid itself out from under Trice's biggest trunk without upsetting anything, and followed him round the corner of the house like a dog.

* * *

The cellar door was round the side, in the angle where the kitchen met the house, a pair of slanting boards on a frame, and Alan Baker hauled them up and propped them and went down the steps into the cool without looking back, because he'd heard the boy behind him and knew he'd follow.

He always had. Ever since he could walk. Down to the cellar with his father, to the woodpile, the well, the oven, under your feet the whole day long and asking what everything was for.

It was cold down there, as it always was in summer, and smelled of earth and apples and the butter crocks along the north wall under their wet cloths. The cider was on the rack at the back, three casks, last autumn's. Alan took two jugs off the hook and went to the near one, and knocked the bung, and listened to it, and set the first jug under the tap.

Behind him, the boy was coming down the steps on his backside, one at a time, the bad leg stuck out in front of him in its black lacing, the stick across his knees. Above him, in the square of light, the flat copper plate he'd brought round the house was hanging in the air, quite still, at the height of the third step, waiting. As though it had been told to.

Alan looked at it for a moment. Then he turned back to the tap.

"Glasses are on the shelf," he said. "The good ones. Top."

"I know where the glasses are, Father."

"I know you know."

The boy got to the bottom step and stayed on it, and reached up, and took the glasses down off the shelf one at a time, the good ones with the green in the foot, eight, and set them on the plate, which had come down two steps to meet him without his saying anything that Alan could hear. They didn't rattle. The plate didn't so much as rock. The boy counted them, with his lips moving, and moved one an inch to the left, and was satisfied.

Alan watched the cider come up in the jug.

"Well," he said. "A merchant girl."

The boy's head came up.

"From the city," said Alan. "With a trunk with her letters on it, in brass. Very nice. I always said, I said to your mother, he'll go to that school and come back with something, and it won't be learning."

"She's not a merchant."

"The tall one."

"No, Father, *Sara's* the merchant. Sara's the little one. Trice isn't—she's a ducharina." He said it carefully, as a word from another place, and then, seeing Alan's face: "It means a duke's daughter. The big man's her father. The duke. And the lady's her mother. She's a duchess and a baroness both, I'll explain later, it's complicated, it's to do with who was born first."

Alan turned the tap off. He took the full jug out, and set the second under, and turned it on again.

"A duke's daughter," he said.

"Yes."

"Marrying you."

"Yes." A pause. "Father. Please don't—when you're up there. Don't tell her she oughtn't. Or them. That she shouldn't marry a, you know. Somebody from here. A country boy. With nothing. I know you'll think it, I thought it, I *still* think it, most days, but they've thought of it already, they must have, they're very clever, all of them, and they've decided anyway, and if somebody says it out loud at the table—" He stopped. "I don't want it said out loud. That's all."

Alan began to laugh.

It came up out of him slowly, the way his laughs did, from somewhere under the apron, and it went on, and he put his hand on the cask to hold himself up and laughed into the cool dark among the butter crocks until the boy, sitting on the bottom step with his leg out and his face gone red, started to laugh too, without knowing why.

"I'll not tell her that," said Alan at last, and wiped his eyes with the back of a floury wrist. "That's not mine to tell her." He turned off the tap. "Do you love her?"

The boy stopped laughing.

He sat on the bottom step with his hands on the stick across his knees, and he thought about it, honestly, the way he'd thought about everything since he was three years old and asked why bread rose; and Alan waited, and let him.

"Yes," the boy said.

"Well, then."

"Her name's Trice. Patricia, but they call her Trice." He looked up, and there was something in his face that made him look, for a moment, about seven. "It's like *trees*. When you say it the way we do. I thought that, the first time I heard it. I thought, that's a name that would do all right at home."

"Trice," said Alan, trying it. In his mouth it came out *trees*. "So it would."

"She's my friend. She was my friend first, before any of it. And I love her, enough to marry her. More than enough." He turned the stick over in his hands. "It was her idea. The marrying. Not mine. I'd never have thought of it. I'd never have *dared*. But she did, and she asked, and now it seems like the best idea anyone's ever had, and I keep waiting for somebody to come and tell me it was a mistake."

Alan picked up both jugs, one in each hand. They were heavy and cold and sweating already in the cellar air.

"Love makes odd matches," he said. "Always has. Look at me." He went past the boy to the steps, and set his foot on the first one, and looked up at the square of daylight and the walnut leaves moving in it. "Married your mother, didn't I."

"*Father*."

"Up you get." He went up a step. "Your mother'll want to see them."

He heard the boy come up behind him, stick and bottom and good leg, the glasses following on their plate without a sound; and he didn't look back, because he knew he'd follow.

* * *

The cider was cold and sharp and cloudy, and it was the best thing Trice had drunk all year, and she was trying to decide whether that was the flight or the cider or the walnut shade, and suspected the answer, when the bakery door opened again.

Tor was standing between the table and the house. He'd been pouring, one-handed, leaning on the stick, refusing all help with the jug and spilling only a little; and then he'd seen that Tam had come out from wherever she'd gone and was watching Rolph from round the corner of the woodpile, and he'd stepped back toward the house to say something to her. So he was the first thing his mother saw when she came out of the door, and Trice, who was watching him, saw his face see her.

She was a middling sort of woman, Trice thought: middling height, middling years, fifty or near it. She had on a gray dress and over it a long apron stained purple in a great flowering patch down the front, and a kerchief tied over her hair, and the hair that showed at the edges of it was going gray. Her face was brown and creased at the eyes from squinting into an oven, and her arms were floured to the elbow, the way her husband's were, and she walked with a little forward lean from the waist, a carrying stoop, as if there were always a tray in her hands even when there wasn't. She came out wiping her hands on the apron, saying something over her shoulder to somebody inside; and then she saw Tor.

She didn't say anything. She just came across the yard, not fast, and put her arms round him, stick and all, and held him; and he put his face down on her shoulder, which he could just reach, and stayed there. Trice heard her say something into his hair, short and low. She didn't know a single word of it, and didn't need to. She'd have known it in any tongue on earth. *We've missed you.*

Trice looked down at her cider.

"Mother," said Tor, coming up out of it, wet-eyed, laughing at himself. He turned, with his hand still on his mother's arm, toward the table. "Mother, I want you to meet—these are my—this is—"

His mother had looked past him at the table.

Trice saw her see it. She saw the woman's eyes go along it, quickly, from the end: Tovey, Rolph, Sara, Trice herself, a glance, nothing. Her mother's back. Her father's face, over the top of his cup.

And stop.

His mother let go of Tor's arm. She stepped round him, gently, as you'd step round a chair, and began to walk toward the table.

She was untying her apron as she came. She did it without looking, both hands behind her, the way you'd do a thing you'd done ten thousand times, and pulled it off over her head, and folded it once, lengthwise, and once again, and held it out to her side; and Tor, behind her, took it, because it was held out to him, and stood there holding it. Then she put up one hand and pulled the kerchief off her hair.

It was the stoop that went first. Trice saw it go. Between one step and the next the woman straightened, from the waist and then from the shoulders, the way a hazel wand comes up when you take your foot off it; and then she wasn't walking like a baker's wife crossing her own yard. She was walking the way Trice's mother walked into a room full of counts. The way Aunt Connie came down a staircase. The way Trice herself had been taught to walk, at seven, with a book on her head and a governess counting, and had never quite managed. Her chin was up. Her hands were still at her sides. She came across the grass of a village bakery yard as though it had been laid down for her that morning.

And something else was going too, and Trice, who prided herself on seeing things, couldn't for a moment have said what.

She thought it was the shade. The woman had walked out of the sun and into the walnut's shadow, and shade is kind to faces; that was all. Except that the gray at her temples had been gray in the sun and wasn't now, quite. Except that the creases at her eyes, which Trice had seen clearly at twenty paces, she couldn't find at ten. She had put the woman at fifty in the doorway. She was a careful judge of ages; it was part of being a careful judge of everything. Halfway across the yard she'd have said forty. At the edge of the table she couldn't have said thirty with any confidence at all, and she found she was gripping her cup.

Nobody said anything. Sara had stopped leaning on her. Rolph's cup was halfway to his mouth and had stayed there. Tovey had gone still at the end of the bench, as still as he went before a fight.

Trice's mother, with her back to the bakery, was reaching for the jug, and hadn't seen any of it.

Trice's father had.

He'd seen it from the beginning, she thought. He'd been looking at the bakery door when it opened. He'd been looking at it since. And his face—Trice had known her father's face all her life, every weather of it, and she had never seen it do this: go slowly blank, and then slowly puzzled, the way a man's face goes when he reads a letter in a hand he knows and can't, for a long moment, think whose. He looked at the woman. He went on looking. The woman stood at the end of the table, quite still, with her hands at her sides, and let him. It went on so long that Tam came out from behind the woodpile to see, and so long that a walnut leaf came down, turning, and landed on the table by Trice's cup, and Trice noticed it.

Then her father put his cup down, and stood up. The bench groaned.

"Laurie?" he said.

Tor laughed. It was a small, polite, uncertain laugh, the one he used when he thought somebody had made a joke he hadn't understood, and he opened his mouth, with the folded apron in his hands, and Trice knew exactly what he was going to say: *No, that's my mother.* Because nobody called his mother that. Nobody had ever called his mother that.

He didn't get to say it.

"Hello, Eric," said the woman. "Mercy. How have you been?"

It wasn't in the tongue. It was the Hill. Pure, and cool, and easy, every vowel exactly where Trice's own governess had put hers, the speech of the long rooms and the good stairs, with a little lift at the end of *been* that Trice had only ever heard from her mother's oldest friends.

Tor's mouth was still open to translate. There was nothing to translate. Trice watched him find that out: watched it go over his face and leave it blank, the apron forgotten in his hands, his eyes going from his mother to the duke and back, as if the ground had moved an inch under his good foot and he was waiting to see if it would move again.

Trice's mother had gone rigid at the sound of her own name. She turned on the bench. She turned all the way round, slowly, the jug still in her hand, and looked up at the woman standing over her in the walnut shade; and the jug went over, and the cider ran across the old gray planks and dripped through the cracks onto the grass, and Mercy Morgan didn't so much as look at it.

"*Laurie*," she said.

And then she was up, over the bench in one movement, in her flying leathers and her pigeon-gray silk, the Sitting Baroness, who had not run anywhere in Trice's whole life; and she ran the three steps there were and threw her arms round the baker's wife and held on, hard, as if somebody might try to take her away.

"We all thought you were *dead*!"
