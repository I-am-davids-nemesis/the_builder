Nobody went in to dinner.

Mercy had known nobody would. She'd been married to Eric for twenty-four years, and in all that time she'd never once seen him walk past a closed box. He'd got as far as the door, with one large hand on the boy's shoulder to steer him; and then his eyes went back over his own arm to the chest on the carpet, with the heron on its lid, and stayed there, and his feet stopped of their own accord, like a horse's at a gate it knows.

"Cook," Mercy said, from her chair.

"Cook," agreed Eric. "Yes. Only, my love—one look. It's been sitting there. It's *carved*."

"It's for you," said the boy, as if that settled anything. "Both of you. And it isn't the carving, really, that's Burks, he found it, I don't know where. It's what's in it. Sorry. Should I—" He was already turning on his stick toward it, and Trice had already taken the stick out of his way and put her own arm where it had been, without seeming to notice she'd done either.

Mercy put her glass down and got up, because if she was going to be made to wait for her dinner she would at least see what for.

Eric knelt to it. He lifted the lid as if it might be asleep. Underneath lay a folded blanket, and under that, coiled round on themselves like sleeping snakes, belts: new leather, dark and supple, each with a brass buckle and a copper plate hanging from a ring at its side, and another, smaller, beside it. Eric drew one out and held it up in the candlelight, and the plate turned slowly on its ring and threw a little red light back at him.

"Is this—" he said.

"It's the new one," said the boy. "Left-handed. So you can have a sword in the other. The turning's better, too. It doesn't go round all at once like a gate any more. And there's a sort of—it keeps the air off you, a bit, so it's not so cold up high. You'll like it. Trice will tell you. Or—no, Trice hasn't tried it. Nobody has, really. Except me, in the orangery, about a foot up." He considered. "It went very well, about a foot up."

Eric looked at the belt in his hands, and then down into the chest, where the others lay coiled, and Mercy watched him count. He counted with his lips, like a farmhand; he always had. She'd stopped minding it the first winter.

"There's fifty," said the boy helpfully. "And fifty of the floats underneath. They're for the fruit. Like a cart, but they don't need a road; you push them and they go, and they stay level, whatever you put on them, mostly. And a hundred dryers round the sides, from the Debris' template, so they're the proper ones. You'll have to watch the floats near trees, they go *through* things if you push too hard. I'm sorry. I'll fix that."

Nobody said anything.

Mercy looked into the chest. She'd grown up on the account books of a barony; her father had sat her on a stool beside him at quarter day from the age of seven, and given her the column of pears to add, and boxed her ears when she was wrong. She couldn't help adding. She added now.

Fifty rigs. There were perhaps a hundred and twenty in the kingdom, counting the king's guard and the ones the army had, and every one of them spoken for before it was made. A family on the Hill would mortgage a farm for one. Fifty was not a present. Fifty was a regiment. And under them, the floats: carts that wanted no road and no horse and couldn't spill. She thought of the barony, of the valley road down to the river at Hollin, and the wagons on it every autumn with their axles screaming, and the river barges, and the men who ran them and what they charged; and she thought that there was no one, anywhere in Noram, who owned fifty carts like that, and that by tomorrow morning someone would. And then the dryers, a hundred, round the edges, packed in so they wouldn't shift. The Debri house had built its whole year on the dryers. Mercy had stood in their outer office in the spring, with her steward, and been told politely that the list was closed.

It wasn't a groom's gift. A groom brought a horse, or a ring, or a good bit of plate for the sideboard. A groom didn't bring three trades in a box and ask you to mind the trees.

She looked at the boy. He was watching Eric's face with real anxiety, the way a child watches its mother open a drawing.

They'd been ready to have him without any of it. That was what she kept coming back to. She'd decided it at breakfast, at Connie's table, three days ago, watching him tell a go-between and a queen that he meant to marry her daughter, red to the hair, with the pieces of the story in very nearly the right order. She'd gone home and told Eric, and Eric had said, "The little fellow with the leg? Good," and gone out to fly into the stable roof again. Title or no title. Connie would manage the title, or wouldn't, and they'd manage without it, and the Hill could say what it liked; the Hill had been saying things about Morgans since the Morgans came down from the hills. She had not expected to be paid for it.

"Tor," said Eric at last. He'd sat back on his heels. He looked, for the first time all week, like a duke. "This is a great deal of money."

"Is it?" The boy looked into the chest as though the money might be visible in it. "Oh. I suppose. Sara would know." And then, going pink, all at once, as if he'd remembered something shameful: "Only it's not much use, is it. I didn't think. It's no good to you just lying in a box. You'll need people. To fly them, and run the floats, and look after the dryers in the fields, and know how. I should have—I could have written something down. How to. I'm sorry. It's a bit useless, really, on its own."

"Useless," said Eric.

"Without people."

Eric put the belt back in the chest. He did it slowly, and coiled it as it had been coiled, and laid his big hand flat on top of it for a moment, as on the neck of a horse.

"I'll find the people," he said. "That's no trouble. There's a hundred men on the home farm would give their teeth to go up in one of these, and I know every one of them, and their fathers." He wasn't smiling, now. "But they'll swear to me. Every one. On the stone in the chapel, the old way. A man with one of these on his belt and a shield on his chest can go anywhere in the kingdom and nobody can stop him." He looked up at Mercy, and she saw that he'd worked it out quicker than she'd have given him credit for, and that it frightened him a little, and that he liked it. "I'll not hand that to anyone I couldn't hang."

"Eric," said Mercy. "Dinner."

"Dinner," said Eric, and shut the lid on the heron, and stood up, and the floor complained.

Trice hadn't said anything at all. She was standing with the boy's arm through hers and her face perfectly calm, and her ears, under the loose dark hair, pink to the tips; and she was looking at her mother, quite steadily, the way she'd looked at her across the nursery at four, over the wreck of something, waiting to see which way it would go.

Mercy gave her nothing. Not yet. She'd want something for this, later; it was only good business.

* * *

The dining room was at the back, over the garden, and it was too small for the table and always had been. The table had come down from the barony with Mercy's grandmother and had been built for a hall; it went from wall to wall, nearly, and the chairs at the ends had to be got into sideways. Mercy had stopped apologizing for it about the time she'd stopped apologizing for Eric.

She'd put them close, at one end. Eric at the head, herself at his right, the boy at his left with the leg on a footstool the butler had produced from somewhere, Trice next to him, Alphie across. Five, and two branches of candles, and the good cloth, which had a darn in it that the butler had set the salt over.

It was a plain dinner. She'd meant it to be. She'd sat at Connie's table often enough to know there was no beating it, and no sense trying, and she'd rather be plain on purpose than grand by halves. There was a soup of leeks. There was a trout each, from the barony, sent up in ice. There was a saddle of mutton that Cook had been threatening them with since the morning, with a sauce of red currants, and new potatoes, and the little sweet carrots, and bread she'd watched come out of the oven an hour since. There would be pears afterward, poached in something, and a cheese.

She watched the boy eat it.

She'd been told, by Connie and by Trice and by her own eyes at the palace, that he'd sat at the king's right hand and eaten off gold; and she'd expected, she supposed, a young man who'd got used to it. He hadn't. He looked at the soup as if he'd been given a present. He said "Thank you" to the butler every time the butler did anything at all, which unsettled the butler more than she'd seen anything unsettle him in thirty years; and when the mutton came in on its board, brown and spitting, he said "Oh," very softly, and then looked round the table to see if he'd said it out loud.

"Do you like mutton, Tor?" she said.

"I like *this*," he said. "I mean—yes. Sorry. We had it at home, sometimes, at the turn of the year. Not like this. Mum would boil it." He seemed to think that might sound disloyal. "She does it very well. Boiled."

"I'm sure she does."

"She'd want to know about the sauce."

Mercy thought that she'd rather like to meet a woman who'd want to know about the sauce, and was surprised to find she meant it.

The butler came round with the wine.

It was the barony's red, the good one, from the south slope; there was only the one cask of it left, in the cellar under the hall, and she'd had it brought up for tonight on purpose and not told Eric, because Eric would have argued about it. The butler poured for her, and for Eric, and for Alphie, and for Trice; and came to the boy, and the boy put his hand over his glass.

He did it gently, and quite quickly, as if he'd done it a great many times.

"No, thank you," he said. "I don't. Sorry."

Eric stopped with his fork in the air.

"Not at all?" he said. "Not even a little one? It's the south slope. Mercy's had it up specially, I can tell, she thinks I can't tell."

"I can't," said the boy. "Really. It's not that it isn't—I'm sure it's lovely. It's only that it makes my head go soft. Even a little. And I need it, my head, for building. It's the only thing I've got that's any good, so I'd better not spoil it." He went pink. "Sorry. That sounds silly."

"It doesn't," said Trice, not looking at him. "It sounds like you."

Across the table, Alphie lifted his own glass an inch, to the boy, and drank, and said nothing; which told Mercy that Alphie had heard it before, and had argued about it once, a long time ago, and lost.

The butler stood holding the bottle with an expression of mild bereavement. Mercy caught his eye.

"The apple," she said. "From the cold larder. The jug with the blue rim."

He went. He came back with it, beaded all over from the cold, and poured the boy a glass of something pale gold, a little cloudy, that smelled, all the way across the table, of an orchard in September with the sun on it.

The boy tasted it. Then he put the glass down and looked at it, and then at her, with an expression she'd last seen on Eric's face the first time he went up off the ground.

"What *is* that?" he said.

"Apple."

"It isn't. It isn't just apple. I've had apple." He drank some more, carefully, as if checking his work. "There's more than one in it. There's a sharp one, and a—something like a pear, nearly, underneath. And it doesn't go thin at the end. Apple goes thin."

He'd found her out, and she was absurdly pleased about it.

"It's four," she said. "Two sharps, from the top orchard, and a russet, and a little of the old yellow that nobody grows any more but my steward's father. We blend it the way we blend for cider. You taste each one, you see, and you put a little of this against a little of that, until it stands up on its own." She heard herself, and couldn't stop. "Only for cider you want the sweet ones, all the sugar you can get, because the sugar's what turns. For this you don't. There's nothing to turn. So you can go sharper, and keep the taste, and it doesn't cloy."

"So it's the same as the cider," said the boy slowly, "only you leave out the bit you need for the cider, and then you can put in the bits you couldn't have before." His eyes had gone a little distant. "That's very clever. That's like—" He stopped himself, visibly. "That's very clever."

"It's very nice, is what it is," said Trice, "and you're to drink it, Master Baker, not build one."

"I wasn't."

"You were."

He drank it. He drank three glasses, in the course of the mutton, and thanked the butler for each one; and Mercy, who had married into a family that measured a dinner by the cellar, and had sat through twenty-four years of men explaining wine to her, found that she had spent the whole of the fish talking about apples to a boy who wanted to know about the russets, and how long they kept, and whether you pressed them whole or cut, and that she'd enjoyed it more than any dinner since she'd come up to town.

Eric didn't mind. Eric talked to Alphie about the rigs, across her, with his hands, flying them over the potatoes. Twice he nearly had the candles.

* * *

They didn't talk business at table. Nobody did, on the Hill; it was the first thing you learned, before your letters. So it was only when the cloth was drawn, and the pears had come and gone, and the cheese, and the butler had put the decanter down by Eric and the jug with the blue rim by the boy and gone out, that Alphie sat back in his chair and put his hand inside his coat.

"Right," he said. "I've been sitting on this since the soup. It's been very bad for my digestion."

He brought out a long fold of heavy paper, cream-colored, tied with a purple cord, with a seal hanging off the cord on a ribbon. Mercy knew the seal before it had cleared his coat. She'd seen it at the bottom of her own marriage lines. She'd seen it, before that, on a letter she'd burned.

He didn't open it. He held it out across the table, not to the boy, but to Eric.

"You read it, Uncle Eric," he said. "If I do, I'll laugh, and then he'll think it's a joke."

"What is it?" said the boy. "Is it from Rich? Is something wrong?"

Eric had taken it, and untied the cord with his large careful fingers, and spread the paper on the cloth among the crumbs; and Mercy saw, upside down, the king's hand at the top, which she'd have known anywhere, and under it three signatures and three seals in a row along the bottom: two in red wax, and the last, on the end, in green.

Eric cleared his throat. It was his outdoor voice. The windows hummed.

"*Richard, by grace of the people and the old line, King of Noram, to all who shall see this writ, greeting,*" he read. "*Know that we have looked upon the deeds of Torrance Baker, of Two Rivers in the County of Lairdgren: that he did of his own will and without commission bring water to the County of Ford in the time of its drought, by a work never before made, which holds yet; that he did go down into the tanners' midden of our city and bring up out of it alive the children who had fallen there, at the hazard of his life; and that he has made, of his own invention and copied from no man, a harness for flight governed by the left hand, and a shield to be worn with it, so that a soldier of the realm may go up armed and come down whole, which is a work beyond the reach of any master now living.*" He paused, to find the next line, and his eyebrows went up at it. "*Wherefore we make him Knight Esquire of our realm, with all that belongs to it, and command that he be so called and so received. Witnessed by our hand, and by the hands and seals of the counts of our realm here set.*"

He looked up.

The boy hadn't moved. He was sitting with his glass of apple in his hand, quite still, the way people sit when a horse has gone past very close.

"That's three," said Trice, very quietly.

"What?"

"Three deeds. He's counted three. The river, the midden, and—" She put one finger, not quite touching, on the third line. "The rig. The new one, and the shield to go with it. Together. That's the masterwork." She sat back. "Not copied. New. Nobody on the Hill can say it isn't; half of them were begging for it at the dinner."

"Father said you'd say it was only a rig," said Alphie to the boy. "He said to tell you it's only a rig the way the sea's only water."

The boy put his glass down. He looked at the writ, and at Eric, and at Alphie, and then, last, at Trice, and his mouth opened, and what came out of it was:

"But I didn't *ask*."

"No," said Alphie. "That's rather the point of you."

* * *

They did it there, in the dining room, because there wasn't anywhere else and nobody wanted to wait.

Eric got his sword down off the wall over the sideboard. It was his grandfather's, and very long, and hadn't been out of its scabbard since their own wedding, and it came out with a noise like a door that hasn't been oiled. He gave it to Alphie, hilt first, with a little bow that wasn't a joke at all, and Alphie took it, and Mercy saw him feel the weight of it and remember, for a moment, whose son he was.

The boy was trying to get up. He'd got hold of the table edge. Trice had the stick.

"You needn't kneel, Tor," said Mercy. "Not with that leg. A chair will do. Old Sir Barnaby was made in a chair; he had the gout."

"I'd like to kneel," said the boy. "If that's all right. Only—I'll need some help with the getting down. And the getting up. Sorry."

It took all of them. Eric took one arm and Trice the other, and they let him down between them onto the good knee on the worn rug by the hearth, with the splinted leg stuck straight out sideways behind him in its black lacing like the oar of a boat, and Mercy, who was standing because she found she couldn't sit, took the footstool and slid it under the foot, so that at least the leg had somewhere to be. He knelt there, a little lopsided, in his good coat, with his head bowed and the candlelight in his hair, and the top of his head came up to about Alphie's belt.

Alphie looked down at him. He had the sword in both hands. For a moment he didn't seem to know what to do with his face.

"You know I've been one of these since I was thirteen," he said.

"Have you?" said the boy, to the rug.

"Since I was thirteen. Father did it on my birthday, after breakfast, in his study, between two other things. He does it to all of us. It's like getting your ears." The sword came down, very lightly, flat, on the boy's right shoulder. "I didn't do anything for it. I didn't do *one* thing." Over, and down, onto the left. "You did three. Four. I've lost count. Everybody has." He stopped. Mercy saw his throat move. "Get up, Sir Tor. Before Uncle Eric's knees go."

"*My* knees," said Eric indignantly; and then they were all getting him up, which was worse than getting him down, and somebody trod on the footstool, and the boy came up between Eric and Trice red to the ears and laughing, holding on to both of them, with his hair over his eyes.

"Sir Tor," said Eric, and shook him by the hand, so that he swung. "Sir Torrance. Ha! Mercy. Mercy, we've a knight in the house."

"We've had one all evening," said Mercy. "Alphie's been sitting in it."

"That's different," said Alphie.

Trice hadn't said anything. She had given the boy back his stick, and he'd taken it, and she was standing beside him with her hands folded in front of her, very upright, and her face had gone entirely calm, the way it went at cards; and Mercy, who had made that face and had given it to her, knew exactly how much it was costing.

She'd been ready to have him without it. She kept telling herself so. It was perfectly true. But now there was no need to be ready. There was a writ on her tablecloth with the king's hand on it and three counts' seals, and nobody on the Hill, not the Westrys, not the Winchesters, not that dreadful Sorvey woman with her go-between, could stand up in any room in the kingdom and say a Morgan had married beneath the rules. It was allowed. It had become allowed over the cheese.

"Who signed?" said the boy. "I don't—I can't read it from here. Who'd *sign* that? For me?"

"There was a queue," said Alphie, sitting down again and picking up his glass, his own voice back. "I'm not joking. Mother had to send people away. Half the Hill wanted their name on it, so they could say they'd known you first. Father said three, and said he'd choose." He counted on his fingers. "Tovey, first. Obviously. He'd have signed it in blood if they'd let him; he'd have *written* it, if they'd let him. He'd do anything to see you two—" He stopped, and looked from the boy to Trice and back, and grinned. "Anything at all. Then Ford. Count Ford came up to the palace in person, the morning after the dinner, and stood in Father's study and wouldn't sit down, and said that if any man in the kingdom had a better right to sign it he'd like to meet him, and then he signed it so hard he went through the paper. You can see. There."

The boy looked. There was a hole.

"And the last one," said Alphie. "That's the one, Baker. That's the one I'd have framed. That's yours."

"Mine?"

"Your count. Lairdgren. Two Rivers is his, isn't it? Your village, your woods, your mum's bakery, all of it. Count Lairdgren." He tapped the green wax. "He came in specially to sign. Nobody even knew he was in the city. He came in from the north, Father said, *flying*, if you please, the evening before last, landed in the yard, signed, had a glass of something with Father and went. I didn't know he had a rig. I didn't know he'd ever *seen* one. Somebody must have sold him one of yours." He considered. "You'd like him, I think. He's a little fellow. Not much bigger than you; a few inches. You'd hardly know he was a noble at all, standing next to one. And he looks like you. He does, a bit. Surprisingly. The face." He waved his glass vaguely at the boy's face. "Same sort of thing. I'll tell you about him some time. There's a whole story. It's a good one."

"I've never seen him," said the boy. "Nobody has, at home. He's never come. Mum says he's very old." He frowned at the seal. "I hope it was a good rig. If somebody sold him one. Some of the early ones weren't very—"

Mercy found she had been looking at the green seal for some time. She stopped.

Alphie was refilling his glass. The boy was looking at the green seal with his lips moving a little, as if he were reading something in the wax that wasn't written there.

* * *

"Well," said Eric, when they'd sat down again, all of them, and he'd poured for everybody who'd have it, and the boy had his apple. He put both hands flat on the cloth on either side of the writ and beamed down the table. "Well, now. Now we can do it properly. Now there's nothing in the world to stop it. The banns, and the chapel, and the—what is it. The other thing, Mercy."

"The settlement."

"The settlement. And the—"

"Oh," said the boy. He'd gone, quite suddenly, rather pale under the pink. "Oh. Yes. Of course. I should—I ought to ask. Properly. Shouldn't I. I haven't. I mean I said, at breakfast, to you, but that isn't asking, not *really*, and I haven't asked *Trice*, I've only—and then there's the banns, which I don't know how you do here, at home it's the Elder, and the chapel, which I suppose would have to be here, or—and the house, we'd have to have somewhere to—and Mum. Mum doesn't *know*. Nobody's told her anything. She thinks I'm at school. She thinks I'm doing my *accounts*." He took hold of the edge of the table. "And I'd have to write to her, and the post to Two Rivers takes a fortnight, and she'd want to come, and she can't leave the bakery, not before the harvest, and Dad can't do the bread on his own, not all of it, and there's Teira, and—sorry. Sorry. I'm sorry. There's a great deal of it, isn't there."

"Tor," said Mercy.

"I can make a list," said the boy, a little desperately. "I'm quite good at lists. I start them, anyway—"

"*Tor*."

He stopped.

Mercy set her glass down. She did it the way Connie did it, which she had taught Connie, a long time ago, on the stairs at home, with their mother's best crystal and a great deal of giggling; and the table went quiet for it, even Eric.

"You will not make a list," she said. "You'll make a great many things, I don't doubt, and the kingdom will be the better for every one of them, but you will not make a list for this. This is mine." She saw his face, and put a little softness into hers, and meant it. "Mine, dear, and your mother's. Nobody else's. Not Connie's, though she'll try. Not Eric's; he'd have it in a barn. And not yours. A wedding is the one thing in the world that isn't any business of the people getting married. You'll be told where to stand. You'll stand there. That's your part."

The boy looked at her. He looked, for a moment, as though she'd lifted a cart off him.

"But Mum doesn't know you," he said. "She doesn't know any of—"

"Then she'll have to meet me, won't she," said Mercy, "and I'll have to meet her. I've been wanting to all evening. I want to know about the sauce." She turned to Eric. "We'll go with them."

"Go where?"

"Back. When they go back to school. We'll go too, as far as Two Rivers, and stop, and I'll sit down with Mistress Baker over a pot of tea, and we'll settle it between us like sensible women, and the rest of you can go and look at the ovens." She looked across at Trice. "When do you go?"

"We'd meant the end of the week," said Trice. Her voice was entirely level, and she'd got hold of her napkin under the table and was folding it, Mercy could see by her elbow, into smaller and smaller squares. "We could go sooner. If we flew, it's a day. Less, with the new rigs. Two days from now would give Tovey time to come up, he'll want to; and we'd want the luggage plates loaded, and the trunks sent round, and someone to tell the school we're bringing two more, because they'll want rooms aired, and Mother—" She stopped. "Two days," she said. "Two days is enough."

"Two days," said Eric, delighted. "We'll fly! Mercy. Mercy, we'll fly to it."

"You'll fly," said Mercy. "I shall go in the carriage, like a grown woman, and you'll come down for me when you've finished hitting things."

"I don't hit things."

"The stable roof."

"Twice," said Eric, with dignity. "Only twice."

The boy laughed. It came out of him all at once, helplessly, the way she'd seen him laugh in the sitting room with her daughter's arms round him, and he put his hand over his mouth, and couldn't stop; and Alphie laughed at him laughing, and Eric, who never needed a reason, roared, so that the candle flames lay down flat; and under the table Trice's square of napkin was now about the size of a sugar lump, and her ears were pink, and she was smiling at her plate with her whole face, as if nobody could see her.

Mercy picked up her glass of south-slope red and drank it, all of it, and thought that it was a great pity there was only the one cask.

* * *

The fifty rigs went first. That was the first thing.

The boy had been back from the Morgans a bell, with the moon down, sitting on his bed in his good coat with his shoes still on, in a state Hale had seen before on young officers after their first proper action, all of it still going round inside them and no way out; and at the end of that bell he'd looked round at the heap of copper on his bed and his chair and the floor by the hearth, and said, quite suddenly, "Oh, *of course*. They can have these. They'll need them. It's a hundred men, Duke Eric said," and lain down on top of them and gone to sleep.

So in the morning Hale gathered them up.

He did it himself, because he wasn't going to have a footman do it. He went round the room with a basket, on his knees on the good carpet, and got the two by the hearth, and the one off the windowsill, and the ones down the side of the chair with the roses on it, where they'd slid, and counted every one into the basket as it went, which was a habit and a duty and a pleasure all at once. Fifty. He carried the basket down to the orangery on his own arm, with his other hand on his sword, not because he thought anyone would try, in the private wing, in daylight, but because he'd been looking at those rigs for three days with the door shut, and a man gets into the way of it.

He put the basket down on the end of the long table, by the lemon tree.

"Fifty, sir."

"Thank you, Hale," said the boy. "Really. You didn't have to."

Hale didn't say anything. He went back out to the door and stood at it; and for the first time in three days, he found, his shoulders came down out of his ears.

He'd thought that would be all. He'd thought the boy would rest. He'd heard the Duke of Morgan, through the dining-room door, roar so the panes rang; he'd heard the prince's voice go quiet in a way it didn't, and then the boy laughing; and when they'd come out to the carriage, the boy had had a fold of cream paper inside his coat that he kept putting his hand on, as if to make sure it was still there. Hale had been told about the paper by the prince in the yard, in three words, with a grin, and had said, "Sir," and had felt something go off inside his chest like a bell struck once, and had stood at his door all that night not thinking about it.

He'd thought the boy would rest. A knight, now. A knight could sit down.

The boy was at the long table by the second bell with copper all round him and a sheet of paper that said, at the top, *Morgans (more)*.

"They'll need shields," he told Hale, when Hale came in with his breakfast and found him there. He didn't look up. "That's the thing. I gave them fifty rigs, and now another fifty, that's a hundred, and only the two shields, the king's ones, and you can't, Hale, you *can't* put a man up there without a shield, it isn't fair on him. Duke Eric said so. Well, he didn't. But he would have."

"Sir."

"So that's a hundred shields." He wrote it. "And plates. Cooling ones, the temperature ones, for the house. Their house. It was ever so hot in the dining room, Hale, with all the candles, Mercy—the duchess—Lady Morgan—she was fanning herself with the bread basket when she thought nobody was looking, and Duke Eric's collar's too tight already without it being an oven. And one for his study, and one for wherever he likes. They're his. He can put them in the stable if he wants." He wrote that, too. "Twenty. To start."

"A hundred shields," said Hale, "and twenty plates."

"It's only two days," the boy said apologetically, as if Hale had complained it wasn't enough.

The copper was there. Hale had stopped asking himself about the copper. It had been there when he came in with the breakfast, in a stack higher than the lemon tree's tub, cut square, and a second stack, of a different color, thinner, for the plates; and a fresh pot of ink, and a new stylus; and nobody had passed his door all night, and he would have sworn to it on anything you like.

The boy didn't go under for the shields. He'd made shields before; he had them in his head, the way Hale had his drill. He copied them straight out of the air onto the copper, without a template, one after another, and talked while he did it: to the lemons, mostly, and once to Hale, about Two Rivers, about his mother, about how she'd fold her arms when Mercy came in at the door, and then unfold them, he thought, when she found out about the apples. By the sixth bell there were forty shields on the table in rows. By the dark there were sixty-three, and Hale had brought him two trays, and taken away two trays, the second one barely touched.

The plates were harder. Those he went under for, a little, on the second morning, the first one; Hale saw him go. He stopped talking all at once in the middle of a sentence about the bread basket and sat looking at the copper with his hands flat on either side of it and his face gone still and quiet and a long way off, and he stayed like that while the square of shadow from the boarded pane went from the lemon tree to the door. Hale stood inside the door the whole while, which he'd never done before, with his back to the wall. When the boy came back, he blinked, and looked at the first plate in front of him, finished, and touched it, and said "Oh, good," in an ordinary voice, and then nineteen more came off it like bread off a board.

He finished the last shield at the eleventh bell of the second night, by lamplight, with his head propped on his hand. A hundred. Hale counted them, after. The plates were twenty.

"I'd have done more," said the boy, looking along the table at it all, a little sadly. "If there'd been more time. I'd have liked to do the dryers again, a few, for the Ford farms. And I never got to the—" His left hand had come up off the table, open, and tilted; and he looked at it, and put it down again. "Never mind. Next time."

"Sir," said Hale.

The boy looked round at him. He was swaying slightly on the stool. He had ink on his chin.

"Hale," he said. "Was I long?"

"Two days, sir."

"Oh," said the boy. "That's all right, then." He thought about it. "We go tomorrow."

"Yes, sir."

"You'll come? To the yard? To see us off?"

Hale looked at the wall over the boy's head, which was glass, and black, with the lamp in it, and the two of them, small and gold, and the lemon tree.

"Yes, sir," he said.

The other hundred floats stayed in the boy's room, against the wall by the window, where they'd stood for a week. Nobody had decided anything about them. Hale shut the door on them that night, all the way, and stood in front of it, and didn't take his hand off his sword till morning.
