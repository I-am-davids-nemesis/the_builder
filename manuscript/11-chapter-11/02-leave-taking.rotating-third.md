Sara had priced the trousers before she was out of the Debri carriage.

She couldn't help it. She'd had her own made in two days, on the house account, by the woman in Tallow Lane who did riding leathers for the Hill and charged the Hill accordingly; and so she knew, standing in the palace yard in the early sun with her satchel on its strap and her one trunk being lifted down behind her, exactly what everybody else's had cost. Duke Eric's had been made for him, and recently, and by somebody who had not been told how much of him there would be; they creaked when he moved, like a new saddle. The duchess's were older and better, and fitted, which meant she'd had them for years, and Sara filed that away. Tovey's were black and plain and had cost more than the other two pairs together. Rolph's were the ones he'd worn to fly up from school in the spring, and had a mend at the knee.

Over them, the shirts. Silk, every one, because silk didn't catch the wind or hold the cold, and because you could fly in silk and land and walk into a duke's house in it without changing. Eric's was white. Mercy's was the gray-blue of a pigeon's neck. Trice's was black, which was a joke, and only Sara got it.

Nobody was in a dress. It wouldn't do, to fly in a dress. Sara had put that to her mother as the reason for the trousers, and her mother had looked at her over the counting-house ledger for a long moment and said, "Who's flying?" and Sara had said, "Everyone, Mother," and her mother had signed the chit without another word, which was how Sara knew she'd been heard.

The yard was full of it, the going. Two carriages stood at the foot of the steps with their doors open and their horses stamping. Trunks were piled beside them, a dozen, Trice's three among them with her initials in brass; and beside the trunks, stacked flat like plates on a dresser, the luggage plates, ten, a little scuffed now at the corners, waiting for somebody to tell them what to carry. Grooms went to and fro. A cat was asleep on one of the trunks and nobody had the heart.

Tor was at the bottom of the steps, on his stick, with his leg in its black lacing and a brown-paper parcel under his other arm, saying good morning to everybody.

"Sara!" He'd seen her. His whole face did it, the way it always had, since the laundry; it didn't matter that she'd seen him three days ago, or that he was a knight now, or that every person in this yard outranked her by a distance you could only measure in centuries. "Oh, good. You're here. I was afraid you'd—I don't know what. Been sold. Is that silk? It looks very nice. Is it cold, silk? I've never—"

"Tor," she said. "Good morning. What's in the parcel?"

He went pink, which meant it was a present.

She knew the shape before he'd answered. She'd been handling his work for two years, and she could have told the corners of it through a sack. A long flat thing, rigid, with a softer coil round it, which would be a rig on its strap. A square, thinner, behind it: a plate. A teardrop, smaller still, against his ribs, and something on a cord. He'd wrapped it all in plain brown paper and tied it with the white string he'd brought from home, which bakers use for cake boxes, crossed twice and knotted in a loop you could pull.

She did the sum. She didn't want to. The rig alone, this week, in this city, with a colonel's list in front of it and Lady Westry wanting to eat Mercy for two: she stopped at the rig. Her father had said there were sums you didn't finish aloud, because finishing them told the listener you'd never been near the thing. She'd never been near this one either.

"It's not for you," Tor said, apologetic. "Sorry. I'll make you something. It's for—oh, there he is."

He'd turned. Sara turned with him.

Along the shady side of the yard, under the arches, the household had come out.

She hadn't noticed them come. That was the first odd thing; she was trained to notice, and they were not few. There were thirty, perhaps, or more, standing in a long line in the shade with their backs to the wall: footmen and maids and grooms, the cook in a clean apron, a boy with a broom he'd forgotten to put down, and at the end, as straight as his own sword, Hale. They'd dressed for it. That was the second odd thing. Sara knew palace livery; she'd seen it at the dinner, purple and silver, pressed and fine. This was finer. Every button had been polished. The maids' caps had been starched until they could have stood up by themselves. The footmen's coats were new, or had been brushed until they might as well be, and somebody had put a flower in the cook's buttonhole, and the cook had let them.

For Tor, Sara supposed. It was a lot of starch for a guest, even a knight; but then he'd cooled their kitchens.

In front of the line, a little apart from it, stood a small neat man in a plain dark coat, with his hands folded in front of him.

He was hardly taller than Tor. That was the first thing Sara saw, and it put him outside every set she knew how to sort people into: too small for the Hill, too still for a servant, too plainly dressed for anything in between. He wasn't old, or not so you could say how old. He was simply there, the way a post is there.

"Burks!" said Tor, and went to him across the yard at the fastest hop he could manage, the stick going down and the good leg after it and the parcel clutched under his arm. Trice moved to go after him, and stopped herself, and didn't.

"Sir," said the man.

"I didn't know if you'd come. I hoped you would. I looked for you yesterday, only you weren't anywhere, and I didn't know where you'd be, I don't know where you *live*, really, isn't that awful, after all this time—" He stopped, a little out of breath, and held out the parcel in both hands, which meant he had to let go of the stick, and stood on one leg. "This is for you. It isn't much. Sorry. It's only things I make, but I didn't know what else, and you've done so much. The copper. The straps. The *chest*. The clothes, at the very beginning, I never thanked you properly for the clothes."

The man looked at the parcel. He didn't take it at once.

"There's a rig," Tor went on, because he never could leave a silence alone. "Because of all the stairs, you're always going up and down them, I've seen you, and in and out, and I thought you could go *over*, instead, if you wanted. It's a left-handed one, so you can carry things in the other. And a shield. You have to have a shield, if you fly, it isn't safe otherwise, Rolph will tell you. And a plate for your room, a cooling one, if you've got a room. You must have. And a new amulet." He went pinker. "For the heat. You've got one, I know, but it's one of the first ones, the lines are terrible, I didn't know what I was doing. This one's better. It'll last."

The man took the parcel.

He did it with both hands, the way Tor had held it out; and then he stood, holding it, and didn't say anything at all, for long enough that Sara saw the boy with the broom look sideways at the cook.

Sara had spent her whole childhood watching people's faces as they looked at things they wanted. She'd stood beside her father at the counter in the glass halls and watched the faces come in off the river: greedy faces, careful ones, faces pretending not to want, faces of men who'd come to buy a ring for a wife they were afraid of. She could read them like weather. She'd learned the face of a man who has wanted a thing a long time and finally can afford it, and the face of a man who's been given something he thinks is beneath him and is working out how to be gracious. She knew both of those well. She'd have known them across a market square.

This wasn't either of them.

His thumb had found the knot in the baker's string, and stopped there, on the loop, without pulling it. He was looking at the brown paper. Only at the paper. His face hardly moved; she'd have said, if she'd been asked to swear to it, that it didn't move at all. But something in it had gone very quiet and very far back, the way a room goes quiet when someone has opened a door onto snow; and she had the unaccountable feeling, standing there in the yard in her new trousers, that she was watching a man who had been given a great many things, over a great many years, and couldn't remember the last time one of them had been wrapped.

"Thank you, Tor," he said.

That was all. But he said it in a different voice. Not *sir*. Sara heard the difference as she'd have heard a coin ring false on a counter, or true; and she couldn't have said which this was, only that it rang.

"Oh, that's all right," said Tor, beaming, enormously relieved, and hopped half a step and nearly fell over, and the man put out one hand without looking and caught him by the elbow and set him straight, as you'd straighten a picture, and gave him back his stick.

"Have a safe journey, sir."

"I will. We will. It's only four bells, Trice says, with the new ones. Less, maybe. I'll write." He looked uncertain. "Can I write? Where would I—"

"It will reach me, sir."

"Oh, good."

He turned away, glowing, and came back toward the carriages. The man in the dark coat didn't move. He stood in front of the household with the parcel held against his chest in both hands, and the line of footmen and maids behind him stood exactly as they had, and nobody said anything; and Sara watched him a moment longer than she meant to.

Then the queen was coming down the steps, and it was over, and she had no idea what it had been.

* * *

The goodbyes took longer than the packing.

The queen kissed Tor on both cheeks and then, as if she'd decided something, on the forehead, and told him to write to her, and not to Alphie, because Alphie didn't write, and to eat. She stood well back from the luggage plates while she did it, the way people stand back from the edge of a quay. The king shook Tor's hand, and then Eric's, and then Tor's again, and said, "Sir Tor," in a voice that carried to the gate and made the cat get off the trunk; and Tor said, "Thank you, Rich," and then saw the household and went scarlet and said, "Your Majesty. Sorry. Your Majesty," and the king laughed so hard he had to hold on to his wife.

Hale came last. He came out of the line at the end of it, and stood in front of Tor, and didn't say anything; and Tor said, "Hale," and held out his hand; and Hale looked at it for a moment as if it were a sword he'd been handed hilt first, and then took it.

"Sir."

"Thank you for the door."

"Sir," said Hale again, and stepped back into the line, and looked at the wall over all their heads with a face like a closed gate.

They divided at the carriages, the way water divides at a stone, without anyone saying how. Mercy got into the first one, and Eric, after a short sharp exchange about whether he'd fit, and Tovey, folding himself in last, a joint at a time, like a carpenter's rule. Sara found herself in the second, with Trice handing her up, and Rolph behind, and Tor last of all, backward, stick first, the leg after, settling himself with a little grunt against the cushions. The door shut. The yard swung past the window, the steps, the queen with her hand up, the long line in the shade and the small dark figure in front of it, still holding the parcel.

Then the arch, and the street, and the city.

* * *

The city was quiet at that hour, or as quiet as it ever got. The carts were going in, not about; the shutters were coming down off the shops in the steep streets below the Hill, with a bang and a rattle each, like a long uneven drumroll going away downhill. Somebody was washing a step. The light came slantwise down the streets and caught in the awnings, blue and saffron and green, and made them glow like lanterns.

Tor had his face to the window. He watched a dray go by, and a chaise, and a woman with a barrow of turnips, and the carriage kept moving, smartly, at a trot, round the stone horse in its square and down the Long Walk with nothing in front of it at all.

"It's not bad at *all* this morning," he said, with faint disapproval, as if the traffic had let him down.

"No," said Rolph. "The geese have all gone home."

Sara looked at Rolph. Rolph's face was perfectly straight. He was looking out of his own window, at nothing, and his knee, which was beside hers on the seat because the seat wasn't wide enough for a prince the size of a door and anyone else, was very slightly shaking.

She'd had him in the house all week. Every day, from breakfast until her mother started looking at him over the teapot. She didn't know what he'd told Tor about where he went, and from the look of him she wasn't going to be told; but she could guess, and the guess made her look out of the window herself for a while, at the turnips.

"Trice," said Tor. "Can I ask something?"

"You can ask."

"It's only—I've been wondering. Since the dinner. And I didn't like to ask *her*." He hesitated. "Why is your mother a baroness? When your father's a duke. Shouldn't she be a duchess? Or is that rude. If it's rude, I don't need to know."

"It isn't rude." Trice had her hands folded in her lap and her face calm, and she gave the question the small pause she gave anything with a price on it. "She is a duchess. By marriage. It's the lesser claim. The barony's hers by birth: she's the eldest, so when my grandfather died it came to her, and she sits it in her own right, not her husband's. A thing you hold yourself is worth more than a thing you hold through somebody else." She glanced at Sara, as if Sara would know that, and Sara did. "So she uses the stronger one."

"The eldest," said Tor. He frowned, counting. "So Connie's the—the queen's younger? Than your mother?"

"Two years."

"Oh." He thought about it. "That's funny. I'd have thought, I don't know. That the eldest would be the one who—" He stopped, and went pink. "Sorry. That's none of my business."

Trice didn't answer at once. Sara knew her well enough, after nine years, to hear the not-answering.

"She was meant to," Trice said. "Mother. Marry him. Uncle Rich. It was settled when they were children; she was the elder, and a Thorgood, and the old king wanted the barony. And then it didn't happen. Aunt Connie married him instead, and Mother married Father, and everybody seems to have been perfectly happy about it ever since." She looked at her folded hands. "Nobody's ever told me why."

"Have you asked?"

"Twice." She said it lightly. "I'm not finished."

Tor looked at her, and opened his mouth, and Sara saw the next question come up into it, and then saw him put it back, carefully, the way he'd put down a field that wasn't ready. He looked out of the window.

"I expect it was complicated," he said.

"Oh, it was *very* complicated," said Rolph, turning round from his window with the whole of his face lit up, as if he'd been waiting at a door for someone to knock on it. "Do you want to know? I know. Trice doesn't, but I do. Nobody tells girls anything good. I got it from Varley's old nurse, who got it from Mother's maid, who was *there*." He settled back. The carriage creaked. "Right. So. They were all at school together. Lairdgren. Same as us. All of them. Mother and Aunt Mercy, and Father, and Uncle Eric, who was enormous even then, apparently, and kept breaking chairs—"

"He still breaks chairs," said Trice.

"—and a fellow called Serge Glost. And the count's daughter. Laurali." He held up a hand and counted them off on his big fingers, six. "And it had all been arranged, every bit of it, when they were in the nursery: who'd marry whom, for the land and the votes and the old king's peace of mind. And it was all perfectly sensible. And then they went off to school and spent three years together in the same six rooms, and by the end of it they'd fallen in love, every one of them. Only with the wrong people. Every pair had swapped."

"Every pair," said Sara.

"Every single one. It was a disgrace. Nurse said there was a ball, at the end of the last year, and there was very nearly a duel, and somebody was sick in a fountain, and nobody will say who, so it was Father." He grinned at the roof. "And the old king came down on them like a roof falling. Grandfather. He'd made promises. Other families had been promised things. He was going to have them married as written if he had to march them up the aisle in chains."

"What stopped him?" said Tor. He'd turned all the way round in his seat to listen, with his bad leg along the cushions and his good knee drawn up, like a boy at a fireside.

"Count Lairdgren."

Tor's face went still. Sara saw it; she was opposite him. It was the look he'd had in the laundry, two years ago, when the square of pine had started to warm under his hand and he hadn't known why.

"*My* count," he said. "From the writ."

"The very same. He came down to the capital, Nurse said, which he never does, and went in to Grandfather, alone, and shut the door. And they were in there a bell. And when the door opened again Grandfather came out and said that on reflection the arrangements were withdrawn, and the young people would please themselves, and that was all he said about it ever again, to anybody, for the rest of his life." Rolph spread his hands. "The count had told him it was a bad idea. That they ought to marry for love. And Grandfather *did what he said*."

"Why would the king do what a count said?" said Sara.

It came out of her plain, the way things did when she was really asking. Because it was the wrong shape. Her mother sold to counts; she'd sat in the counting house and watched her mother sell to them, and watched them haggle, and watched them go away with less than they'd come for and think they'd won. Counts were a long way up from a merchant's daughter, but they were somewhere you could see from where she stood. Kings weren't. Kings didn't go into a room with a count and come out with their minds changed.

"And Serge Glost," said Rolph, sailing on, "went home. Which is the other good bit. Because he was Austran. He was there on some sort of exchange, from before the trouble, and he went home, and now he's someone very important in the Austran government. Nobody's sure what. Something with a great many people under it. Father gets letters." He frowned. "I don't think Father answers them."

"An *Austran*," said Tor. "At Lairdgren."

"Of all places. I know."

"And Laurali?"

"Oh," said Rolph. "Well. That's another story." He waved it away, and leaned forward with his elbows on his knees, and lowered his voice, though there was nobody to hear it but the four of them and the back of the driver's head. "But the best part's the count. Because Grandfather did what he said, Sara, because he always does. Everybody does. Because the count isn't just a count." He looked from her to Tor and back, savoring it. "He's an Ancient."

He sat back, pleased with himself.

Nobody said anything.

Sara looked at Tor, and Tor looked at Sara, and she saw on his face exactly what she could feel on her own: a polite, attentive blankness, the face you make when someone has said the end of a joke in a language you only half know.

"An ancient what?" said Tor.

Rolph's face fell.

"Ancient," said Sara slowly. She was turning it over. She knew the word. Of course she knew it. In the glass halls, on the river, there was a back room behind a back room where her father had taken her once, when she was eleven; and on the shelves in there, on velvet, were things that weren't magic and weren't anything else either: a smooth black slab no bigger than her hand, with no seam in it anywhere; a little clear bead that had been cut, somehow, into a hundred faces; a green bottle that wasn't glass. *Ancient work*, the man in the back room had called them, and her father had said afterward, walking home along the river, that they were things from before, and nobody could price them, so nobody tried, and you never bought one, whatever they told you, because it was against the law to own them and because you couldn't sell them on. "It means old," she said. "Older than anyone can price. Things from before." She looked at Rolph. "You're saying he's *old*?"

"I'm saying—" Rolph stopped, and looked at Trice, and something passed between them that Sara had seen pass between them before, over Tor's head, a look that said *they don't know* and *of course they don't*. She'd seen it often enough. She'd never before been on the side of the table it was about.

"You tell it," said Rolph. "You had the governess with the voices."

Trice unfolded her hands, and folded them again the other way.

"It's not a secret," she said. "Not exactly. It's—everybody knows. On the Hill. You get it in the nursery, after your letters and before your sums, the way you get the Litany and Wylde and Doretta. It's only that it isn't told *down*." She glanced at Sara. "Not to merchants, much, or not the whole of it. Not in the villages at all. Nobody decided that. It's just how it went."

Sara said nothing. Her mother had read every letter that came into the Debri house for twenty years. The Debris had a man in every port from the Pacifico to the eastern sea and knew the price of wheat in towns whose lords didn't know they had wheat. And here was a thing everybody knew, on the Hill, in the nursery, between letters and sums, and nobody had told them.

"Long ago," said Trice, and her voice changed a little, went smoother, as if she'd stepped back onto a path she'd been walked down as a child, "before Noram, before any of it, there was one great country over the whole world, or nearly. Not magic. Things. Machines, like the Austrans have, only more, and bigger than you can think: they could talk across the sea, and fly higher than birds, and build cities taller than the palace, a thousand of them. They could do anything they liked to the land. So they did. If they wanted a river somewhere else, they moved it. If they wanted a hill gone, it went. They made the land into whatever they wanted that year, instead of looking after it, and they did it for a long time, and it all held, until all at once it didn't." She paused. "Nobody knows exactly what happened. Something broke. Everything broke. The governess said the sky went wrong for a hundred years. And when it was over, the great country was gone, and there was only what you see now, and the old things in the ground."

"The ruins," said Tor quietly. "The road at home. That goes under the river."

"Very likely."

The carriage went down a long hill. Sara put her hand out to the strap and didn't notice she'd done it.

"And the people who were left," Trice went on, "broke into pieces, and every piece took a different road, so that it wouldn't happen again. A different idea of how to live. Noram took magic. Fields and builders and sigils; the way we do it. Soam, in the south, over the mountains, took the other way altogether: living with the land, not over it, taking only what it gives. Austra went back to the machines. They thought the old country's only mistake was not being careful enough." Her mouth went thin for a moment. "And Antar—the cold country, across the sea, where the striped cloth comes from—took families. Lines. Who's born to whom, and what they carry in them, and building that up, generation on generation, the way you'd breed a horse."

"Breed *people*?" said Tor.

"That's what the governess said. I've never been." She went on before he could ask anything else. "And over each of them, to guide them, so they didn't go wrong again the way the old country had, there were people set. A few. They'd lived through the end, or been made to, and they didn't die, or not the way we do. Something kept them. Some trick of the old machines, or some magic, nobody's sure, and whatever it was, it's lost now." She held up her fingers, as Rolph had, and counted them off, cool and quick, as if they were names on an account. "They named themselves for colors. Nobody knows why. Red. Green. Brown. White. Gray. Black." She folded her hand away. "Those are the Ancients. And Green is the one for Noram. He's meant to rule it. He has done, in one way and another, for as long as there's been a Noram to rule."

"Rule it," said Sara. "The king rules it."

"The king," said Trice, "goes into a room with Count Lairdgren and comes out having changed his mind."

There was a short silence in the carriage.

Tor was looking at nothing, at the place between Sara's knee and Rolph's, with his lips moving a little, the way they did when he was doing a sum he didn't like. Then the carriage jolted, and he blinked, and said, "How long do they *live*, then? Ancients? If it's from before. That's—Trice, that's thousands of—"

"Oh, look," said Rolph. "The wall."

It was there. It filled the window on Sara's side, white and blank and thick as a house, with the shadow of the gate coming down over them like a cool hand, and the noise of the wheels went hollow and enormous under the arch, so that nobody could have heard anything anybody said; and then they were out the other side, into sun, and grass, and the smell of a field that had been cut, and the driver was calling to the horses, and the first carriage had already stopped ahead of them on the verge by a white milestone, and Duke Eric was getting out of it backward, with a sound like a bear coming out of a hollow tree.

Sara had six questions. She had them lined up, in order, the way she lined up the points of a contract before she sat down at the table. She looked at Trice, and Trice was already reaching for the door.

"Later," said Trice.

It wasn't *not yet*. Sara noticed that, and put it away with the rest.

* * *

They loaded on the verge, by the milestone that said *THE CITY — 1* in black letters cut in white stone, as if anyone could have missed it.

Tor did the plates. He did them sitting on Trice's biggest trunk with his leg straight out in front of him, handing out the copper buttons to the drivers, who held them as if they might go off; and the plates rose one by one off the grass where they'd been laid and slid themselves under the trunks, and lifted, and hung there a foot up, steady as tables, waiting. One of the horses wouldn't look at them. Sara didn't blame it.

The rigs were new.

He'd made them last night, he said, after the last of the shields, because he couldn't sleep and it was only copying; she hadn't known that until he handed hers to her on the verge, and she put her hand on the plate and felt the lines under her fingers, and they were different. Straighter. She couldn't have said how she knew, any more than she could have said how she knew a good sapphire from a good piece of glass; she just knew, the way her hand knew.

"Left-handed," said Tor, anxiously. "Sorry. I should have asked. Is that all right? You can have a right one, I've got the old one in the trunk—"

"It's all right, Tor."

"The turning's better. You'll see. It doesn't—you know. Swing. Lean less than you think. *Much* less. Everybody leans too much the first time."

She buckled it on. She put the shield on over her silk, and felt it settle against her breastbone, warm as a hand.

Behind her, a short sharp conversation was going on by the first carriage.

"You said," said Duke Eric, "you'd go in the carriage. Like a grown woman. You said it at *dinner*."

"I did." The duchess was buckling her own belt, briskly, on top of the pigeon-gray silk, without looking down at it, as if she'd done it every morning of her life. "And then I sat in a carriage for three bells yesterday going to the Westrys' and back, and I thought about four days of it to Two Rivers, on those roads, and you up there among the clouds, flying into things, and I thought: no." She pulled the strap tight. "A grown woman has trousers made."

"You never *said*."

"You never asked." She took the control from Tor, who was holding it out to her with both hands and a look of terror. "Thank you, dear. Which way is up?"

"It's—you just—you sort of *think* it, and tilt, only gently—"

The Sitting Baroness Merciful Thorgood Morgan thought it, and tilted, gently, and rose straight up off the grass of the verge to the height of the milestone, and stopped there, level, with her other hand on her hip, as if she were standing on a step.

"Oh," she said. "That's very easy."

Duke Eric went up beside her a moment later, a good deal less level, and turned all the way round twice before he could stop it, and said a word that Sara had heard in the Debri warehouses but never in silk; and his wife looked at him sideways, from her step in the air, and Sara saw where Trice had got that look.

Tovey went up without a word, straight, like a pike out of water. Rolph whooped. Trice simply wasn't on the ground any more. Tor went last, from his seat on the trunk, the stick tucked through his belt and his splinted leg out in front of him like a bowsprit, and the luggage plates rose after him, one behind another, all ten, in a long obedient line with the trunks on them; and the drivers, standing in the road with their buttons in their palms, watched the whole thing go up into the morning, and one of them took his hat off, and didn't seem to know why.

Then Sara went up.

She'd flown before. She'd flown from school to the capital in a day, in the spring, on the old rig, and had sunk and bobbed and swung round at the turns like a gate in a gale, and learned to brace for it, and been proud of learning. So when the others turned east, she braced, and leaned, and went round so fast and so smoothly that she overshot the whole party by a field and a half and found herself facing west, alone, over a haystack, with her heart in her mouth.

"*Less*," called Tor, faintly, from a long way off. "I said *less*!"

She came back. She leaned less. The rig answered as if it had been listening for her, gentle, at once, the way a good horse answers the knee; and then they were all going, the seven of them and the trunks, up over the fields and the hedges and the last of the market gardens, into the sun.

It was fast. It was so much faster than she'd known that she didn't believe it, at first; she thought it was the height, or the wind. But there wasn't any wind, or not on her face. That was the other thing. On the old rig, at speed, the air had come at you like a wall, and you'd tucked your chin and squinted and breathed through your teeth, and the shield had got warm on your chest with the work of keeping it off. Now the air seemed to go round her. It parted in front of her face, softly, like water round the bow of a boat, and came past her cool and moving and easy to breathe, the way air comes in at an open window on a summer night; and the shield lay quiet on her breastbone and didn't warm at all.

She looked across at Tor. He was flying along with his leg out and his face turned up, smiling at nothing, and his lips were moving. Talking to the rig, probably. Telling it it had done well.

Below them, the country unrolled. Fields went by, gold and green and brown, like a merchant's samples laid out on a counter: barley, and something she didn't know, and a whole hillside of vines. A river, and a town on it with a bridge, and boats. Then the land rose and the fields got smaller and fewer, and there were hedges and then woods and then more woods, and the river they followed went narrow and quick and brown, and then they were over the forest, and it went on, and on, the way the sea goes on, dark green and dark green and dark green to the edge of the sky.

Nobody talked much. You could have, now, with the air so quiet; but nobody did. Eric sang for a while, until Mercy flew over and said something to him, and then he didn't. Rolph flew beside Sara, and didn't say anything either, and didn't need to.

Sara flew, and turned her six questions over in her head, in order, the way she'd have turned stones in her hand. Who the count was. What *rule* meant, when it wasn't the king's. Why the king did as he was told. Why nobody told merchants. How long, as Tor had asked, an Ancient lived. And the last, which wasn't a question exactly, and which she didn't know where to put: a small man in a dark coat, in front of a household starched as if for a wedding, holding a parcel tied with baker's string against his chest, and saying a boy's name as if it cost him something.

She couldn't see that it fitted anywhere. She kept it anyway. Her father had told her once that the things you couldn't price yet were the ones to keep.

The palace bell had been striking the eighth when they left the yard. Somewhere below them, in a village none of them would ever see, a chapel bell was striking the noon when Tor, out in front, put out his left hand and pointed down.

There was a gap in the trees. A river came into it from one side, and another from the other, and met; and in the angle where they met there were roofs, a handful, gray and brown, and the small square tower of a chapel, and gardens, and a green with a cow on it. From one chimney, near the middle, smoke was going up straight and white into the still air, a great deal of smoke, more than any one chimney ought to make at noon, unless there was an oven under it.

Tor had stopped in the air. He hung there, over the forest, with his leg out and his stick through his belt and the line of trunks bobbing gently to a halt behind him, and looked down at it.

"That's it," he said. "That's home."

He didn't say anything else. Sara didn't either. She'd been going to tell him it looked lovely, which it did, and found she'd have to look somewhere else first; so she looked at the smoke.
