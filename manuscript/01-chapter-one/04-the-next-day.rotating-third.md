He had come early on purpose. The copy-work was three days late, and he'd judged that the building master would be gentlest before breakfast, when there was nobody else to be ungentle to. So he was sitting on the bench outside the office with his three lamp-squares wrapped in a cloth on his knees when the scholarship boy came down the corridor at a near run, holding a board against his chest.

The first-year knew who he was. Everyone did, a little. The small third-year from the woods, the one who'd been shut in the isolation chambers half of last week. He had a gray smudge under one eye and his hair was wet at the front, as if he'd washed his face and forgotten to finish.

The boy knocked. The door opened before he'd taken his hand away.

"Baker."

"Sir. I'm sorry it's so early, sir. I finished it. I mean, I think I finished it. I tested it yesterday, at the washing house, it worked on shirts and trousers and a towel, and wool, I think, someone's sleeves, and I wanted to bring it to you before—"

"Give it here."

The building master was a long, gray, folded sort of man in an apron over his tunic, with a magnifier on a cord round his neck. He took the board in both hands and turned it to the window. He brought the magnifier up. He looked at the paint for a long time, the corners especially, tipping the board so the light ran across the lines. He touched one edge with a fingernail and looked at the fingernail.

The first-year, on the bench, held very still and tried to look like part of it.

"Washing house," said the building master. He handed the board back to Baker, took him by the upper arm, and walked him out of the office. In the corridor his eye fell on the bench. "You. What's that?"

"Copy-work, sir. Lamp-squares. Three. They're late, sir. I'm sorry."

"Bring them. I need a witness."

The first-year didn't know what for. He brought them.

They crossed the dormitory court at the building master's pace, which was very fast, with Baker half a step behind, explaining. "It's only a field for cloth, sir, it lifts the water out of the fibers, it doesn't heat anything. I used a closed pattern so it wouldn't spread. The pine's not good, I know the pine's not good, I'm sorry about the pine, it was what I had—"

"Yes," said the building master.

The washing house was empty at that hour, gray and dripping. One tap ran into one tub with a sound like a small stream. The building master let go of Baker's arm, took the board out of his hands, and set it on the bench. Then he took Baker by the collar and the back of the belt, the way you'd lift a sack, and put him in the tub.

The first-year dropped his lamp-squares.

Baker went under all the way. Water slopped over the rim in a sheet. For a count of about two there was nothing but the tub and the building master's arm in it to the elbow; then the master straightened, and Baker came up on his own, sitting in the water to his chest, hair flat to his head, his mouth already open.

"—sorry, sir," Baker said, and coughed.

"Out."

Baker climbed out. He stood on the flagstones and poured. Water ran off his tunic and his trousers and out of his boots and in a little stream from the end of his nose. The building master picked up the board, looked at Baker, looked at the board, and stepped three paces back, so that Baker stood between him and the tub. Then he touched the middle of the pattern with two fingers.

The water left.

It went out of Baker's clothes all at once, beading on the brown wool and running together and sliding down him to the floor in a clear sheet, so that for a moment he stood in a puddle of his own shape. His tunic lightened. His trousers lightened. His sleeves, which had clung to his arms, let go of them. The water ran out of his collar and down his neck and onto the stones and kept running.

Baker stood in his dry clothes with his hair still plastered flat and dripping, because hair was not cloth, and blinked. A drop fell off his eyebrow.

"Hold out your hands," said the building master.

Baker held out his hands. The building master looked at them, front and back. He took one by the wrist and pressed the thumbnail, and watched the color come back. He looked into Baker's eyes, one and then the other, close, as if he were reading small print.

"Any pain?"

"No, sir."

"Dizziness? Thirst?"

"No, sir. My boots are still wet. Inside, I mean. I think it's the leather. Leather's different, it's—"

"Yes," said the building master. He let go of the wrist. "A field like this has to be shown safe for use around people. That's what this was. Now it has been." He turned to the first-year, who had not yet picked up his lamp-squares. "You saw that."

"Sir," said the first-year. "Sir, you *drowned* him."

"I didn't drown him. He's standing there."

"You put him in a *tub*."

"That's where the water was." The building master turned the board over, looked at the back, turned it face up again, and tucked it under his arm. "I'll keep this. Baker, go to your classes. When anyone asks about last week, you were doing a building exercise for my class."

Baker's hair dripped. "But I wasn't, sir. It wasn't assigned. I just—"

"It is now."

"Yes, sir." Baker hesitated. "Will that be enough, sir? So they don't send me home?"

The building master looked at him. It was not a long look. It was the look of a man checking whether he'd heard a word correctly, and finding that he had, and deciding not to pursue it.

"Go to class, Baker," he said. To the first-year: "Pick those up. Bring them to my office. They'd better be good."

Doris came into the meditation room with a thing in her hands, as she did every morning, and set it on the low table in the middle of the circle of mats.

This morning it was a square of black glass, about the size of a palm, rimmed in something that was not quite bone and not quite horn. It had come out of the ground somewhere in the hill country, she'd been told, in a jar with a great many others like it. It was perfectly smooth on the front. Light lay on it without going in. She had owned it for eleven years and had never got to the bottom of it, which was why it was on the table.

The students came in and sat. She counted them without looking up. Twenty-two. Twenty-three.

Twenty-four was Baker, and Baker did not sit. He stood at the edge of her circle with his hair wet.

"Ma'am," he said. "About last week. I'm sorry. I was doing a building exercise for my building master's class." He said it like a lesson he'd been given to repeat. Then he kept going, which nobody had told him to do. "Only, it was a working trance, the deep kind, and that's—I mean, what we do here is the lighter kind of the same thing, isn't it, ma'am? So I was meditating. The whole time. Thirty hours, nearly. So I thought, if it's all the same to you, perhaps I wasn't really absent. From this. In a way."

He stopped. He seemed to hear himself.

"I'm sorry," he said again.

Doris looked at him. He was very pale. His hair was wet and his clothes were completely dry, which was interesting, and his hands were holding each other in front of him.

She nodded once, toward his mat.

He sat.

She let the room settle. It took the usual time. Breath slowing. The small sounds of people trying not to make small sounds. Then she looked at the glass, and they looked at the glass, and the room went quiet in the way it did.

Most of them went down slowly, and not far. Baker went down the way a stone goes into a well. One breath he was a boy with wet hair; the next there was nobody home, and the black glass had all of him. He always did that. She had noticed it in his first year, and noticed it again every year since, and she had not yet decided what she thought of it.

She sat with her hands in her lap and watched him not move for most of a bell.

* * *

The theory master was standing in the doorway when they came in, which he never did, rubbing his hands together, which he did constantly, and when he saw Baker he actually said, "Ha!"

Dorgal took his usual seat, third bench, by the window. He took out his notes. He arranged his pens. He did not look round.

"Baker! There you are. Sit, sit, no, there, at the front, where I can see you. I've been talking to your building master." The theory master's eyebrows went up and stayed up. "At breakfast. At *length*. He was insufferable. I had to leave before the porridge."

"I'm sorry, sir," said Baker.

"Sorry! Ha!" said the theory master, and went to his desk, still rubbing his hands.

Dorgal uncapped a pen. So it worked, then, the little laundry board. He'd assumed it would. He had watched it work, yesterday, on the fifth son's sleeves, and on Baker's shirts, and on the floor of the washing house, which had been a great deal wetter afterward than before. And then Tovey had stood there and asked if it could be bought, and Baker had said *it isn't mine to sell* as if he were confessing to a theft.

Thirty gold. On *pine*.

"Now then. Fields of discrimination," said the theory master, writing the words across the board in his big looping hand. "Which you all know. Which every first-year knows, and every third-year forgets, in my experience, round about the time they start to think they're clever. Everything that lives has a vitality field. Yes? Everything. The moss on that wall. The flies on the moss. Sorvey."

Dorgal looked up.

"The more complex the creature, the stronger and more complex its field. Who's more complex, Sorvey, you or the moss?"

"On a good day, sir, me."

A laugh, small. The theory master grinned. "On a good day. And a human field, being very strong and very complicated, naturally resists any simple field you set against it. It shrugs them off. To touch living tissue directly takes a specific, focused effort, and that, ladies and gentlemen, is why you can't kill your roommate with a lamp-square, however much you might like to. Distinguishing living from not-living. Write it down. It'll be on everything I ever give you."

Dorgal wrote it down. It was on everything already. Two benches ahead, Baker was writing too, steadily, head bent, the way he wrote everything, as if the theory master were dictating instructions for something he meant to build that afternoon.

The theory master talked for most of a bell. Twice in that time he stopped in the middle of a sentence and looked at Baker and smiled, for no reason at all, and then went on.

Baker didn't see either time. Dorgal did.

At the end, as they gathered their things, the theory master said, "Baker. A word, later in the week. Nothing urgent. Something I'd like to show you." He rubbed his hands. "Ha!"

"Yes, sir," said Baker. "I'm sorry, sir."

Dorgal capped his pen and put it away, and found that he'd pressed hard enough on the last line to tear the paper.

"He put you in the *tub*?"

"In the tub. All the way in."

Rolph laughed so hard he had to put his spoon down. Half the long table looked round. He didn't care. "Oh, I'd have paid. I'd have *paid*, Little Tor. Your face. Tell me about your face."

"I don't know about my face. I was under the water."

"Tell me about the first-year, then."

"He said 'you drowned him.'"

"He didn't."

"He said it twice."

The cafeteria was full and loud and smelled of the stew, which was the stew it always was on the first day of the week. Rolph had saved them the end of a bench by the windows. Tor had come in looking like a boy who'd been put in a tub, and eaten two bowls without seeming to notice, and was now working through a third more slowly, telling the morning a piece at a time, between mouthfuls.

He'd got as far as Doris. Rolph had heard about the black glass and was waiting for the theory master, who by all accounts had been very odd indeed.

"He said *ha*," Tor said. "A lot. I think he's pleased about something."

"I should think he is."

"Do you think it's about the building master? They had breakfast. He said the building master was insufferable."

"I think," Rolph said, "it might possibly be about you."

Tor looked at him as if he'd made a joke he couldn't quite follow, and went back to his stew.

Rolph opened his mouth to try again, and then saw Tor's spoon stop.

It stopped halfway, and stayed there. Tor was looking at something over Rolph's shoulder. His face did not change at all, which with Tor was always worse than if it had.

Rolph turned.

Kolb was crossing the cafeteria. He was not hurrying. He was a tall man, middle-aged by the look of him, and completely bald, with a pale scar running from the corner of one eye down into his jaw. He wore a sleeveless jerkin, and there wasn't much of him that wasn't cord: long arms roped with it, a neck like a bundle of it, none of the bulk of a wrestler. He walked between the tables the way he walked the yard, with the noise of the room going down a little on either side of him and coming back up after he'd passed. He reached their bench and stopped. He did not sit.

"Baker," he said.

Tor put his spoon down. He stood. "Sir."

"Sit down."

Tor sat.

"Fifth day," Kolb said. His voice was low and quite pleasant, the voice of a man remarking on the weather to an acquaintance. Nobody beyond their end of the bench could have heard it. "You weren't in my yard."

"No, sir. I'm sorry, sir. I was doing a building exercise for—"

"I don't care." Kolb said it without heat. "I don't have building exercises, Baker. I have a yard. You owe it an afternoon. We'll start collecting today." He considered Tor for a moment longer. "I'll see you at the bell."

He went. The noise came back up behind him.

Rolph turned back. Tor was sitting very straight, with his hands flat on the table on either side of his bowl. He was looking at the stew.

"Listen," Rolph said. "Little Tor. Listen to me. He's all bark."

Tor nodded.

"Well. Mostly bark. He does this every term to somebody, it's practically a hobby. And I'll be there, all right? I'll partner you. He can't run you any harder than he runs me, and he's been trying for three years, and look at me. Fine. Perfectly fine."

Tor nodded again. His eyes hadn't moved from the stew.

"You'll be sore," Rolph said. "That's all. You'll be sore and you'll complain and I'll rub liniment on your back and you'll say sorry about it the whole time, and in a week he'll have forgotten. Tor. Are you hearing me?"

"Yes," Tor said. "Sorry. Yes."

He wasn't. Rolph could see it. He'd seen Tor lifted out of a room by a sentence about apples; this was the other thing, the thing that came for him in rooms full of people and shut him up inside himself, and nothing Rolph said was going to reach through it. Rolph knew how to get Tor out of a washing house. He knew how to get him out of a crowd, out of Tovey's attention, out of a dinner with nobles. He had no idea in the world how to get him out of this.

He put his hand on Tor's shoulder. Tor didn't seem to feel it.

The tower bell began to strike.

Tor stood up at the first stroke, carefully, the way he'd taken the stairs the day before, and Rolph's hand fell from his shoulder. He didn't notice. He picked up his bowl, then put it down again, as if he'd forgotten where it went. He said, "Sorry," to nobody. Then he walked out of the cafeteria toward the yard, very straight and a little too slowly.

Rolph left both bowls where they were and went after him.
