The tower bell at Lairdgren struck the first hour of the rest day, and Bet Haskins got up off her stool to look in on the scholarship boy again.

She had been keeper of the isolation chambers for thirty-one years, and in that time she had learned the bell the way a miller learns his wheel. She could tell you which stroke was the clapper's and which was the echo off the chapel roof. She could tell a student by how they came in under it: the ones who'd be out again in an hour, sulking, having built nothing; the ones who'd sit a day and a night and come out with a lamp-square that worked about half the time. The bell went on whether anyone listened or not. Up in the tower it had been striking since before the school was a school, since before the tower itself, some said. The bottom three courses were not stone at all but something older and grayer and smoother, poured rather than cut, that no frost in living memory had managed to crack. Bet had put her hand on it once as a girl. It had been warmer than it ought to be.

The chambers ran along the undercroft of the east range, six doors in a row, each with a shutter at eye height and a slate on a nail beside it. The slate on the fourth door said, in her own chalk:

*BAKER, T. — In: late, fourth day.*

Under that, a tally. One stroke for every bell she'd looked in on him since. She counted them with her thumb. Thirty, near enough.

She slid the shutter back.

The chamber was a stone box, whitewashed once, with a bench, a stool, a high window no bigger than a loaf, and a lamp that had burned down to a blue bead. On the bench lay a square of pale pine and a saucer of blue paint. On the stool sat the boy, exactly as he had been sitting at the last bell, and the bell before that, and every bell since late on fourth day: hands flat on his thighs, back straight, eyes open and fixed on the pine.

He was a small thing. That had been the first thing anybody said about him, three years ago, when he'd come up from the forests round Two Rivers on a scholarship with the king's own seal on it, though nobody had ever been able to say why the king should have bothered. The kitchens had called him the king's boy for a term or two, then got used to him. He was twenty now, by his records, and could have passed for fifteen in a poor light. He rarely smiled. He talked to himself. He thanked her every time he signed the slate, which no other student had ever done.

He didn't look as though he were breathing. She had seen that before and didn't worry about it. She looked instead at the board.

The paint on it was dry.

She'd watched him lay the last of it down at the turn of the night, the brush going so slowly she'd thought at first he'd fallen asleep with it in his hand. That was perhaps four bells ago. Cheap blue house paint on unsealed pine should still have been tacky; it should have been tacky till the end of the rest day. It wasn't. Even from the door she could see the dull, settled skin it had, like a puddle frozen over. In thirty-one years she had seen paint do that perhaps a dozen times. She had stopped telling anyone when she saw it. Nobody ever believed a keeper about anything.

"Well, then," she said, to nobody.

The boy blinked.

It was a very ordinary blink. It was the first one in some hours. Then he took a breath, a long one, that went down further than breaths usually go, and let it out, and his hands came up off his thighs as if he'd only just noticed they were there. He looked at them.

Bet unbolted the door.

"What bell?" he said. His voice came out cracked and small. He tried again. "I'm sorry. What bell is it, ma'am?"

"First. Rest day."

He looked up at her. She watched him do the sum. She watched it come out wrong, and then come out right, which was worse.

"Rest day," he said. "Then I've missed—fifth day. All of fifth day." His face went white under the gray. "Oh, no. Oh, they'll send me home."

Bet had nothing to say to that, so she didn't say anything.

He tried to stand, and his knees didn't like it. He caught himself on the bench with both hands and stood bent over it for a moment, breathing, the way old men stand when they've got out of a cart. Then he straightened, slowly, a piece at a time, and she heard something in his back go off like a knuckle.

"Sorry," he said, to his back.

"You'll want feeding."

"Yes, ma'am. Thank you, ma'am. In a moment." He wasn't looking at her. He'd picked up the board and was holding it close to his face, tilting it to the bead of the lamp. He touched one corner with the tip of his finger, very lightly, and looked at the finger. Clean. He let out a breath that was almost a laugh.

"It's dry," he said.

"So I see."

"It's not meant to be dry yet." He turned it over, looked at the back, turned it face up again. "I don't know if it works. I don't know if it does anything at all. I have to see." He was already moving to the door, the board held against his chest, his steps short and careful. "I need something wet. Laundry. I need laundry. Thank you, ma'am, I'm sorry, thank you."

"Sign the slate."

He came back and signed the slate. His hand shook a little. Under her chalk he wrote *Out: first bell, rest day*, and then, after a moment's hesitation, *Thank you*.

Then he was gone up the undercroft steps into the gray morning, and she stood in the chamber doorway and listened to his feet cross the court, uneven, hurrying, going toward the dormitories and not toward the kitchens at all.

Bet Haskins looked at the empty stool for a while. Then she wiped the slate clean with the heel of her hand and went up to her breakfast, where, though she had stopped telling anyone anything years ago, she found she had something to say.
