The sun was low in the gate when Tor came back into the yard. His face was gray with dust, streaked where sweat had run, and his tunic had dried and soaked and dried again in rings. He was barely jogging. He stopped in front of Kolb and stood there, swaying a little.

The yard was empty except for Kolb and one tall girl by the rack, working through a guard drill on her own.

"Sir."

"Baker." Kolb picked up an iron bar that had been leaning on the rack, as long as a waster and as thick as a broom handle, and held it out. "The pell. Until you can't."

The pell was a new trunk, pale and sappy, set in the hole where yesterday's had stood. Tor took the bar. His arms sank under it, then came up. He walked to the pell, set his feet, and hit it.

The bar bounced off the wood with a sound like a dropped pot. Tor's whole body jerked with it. He set his feet again and hit it again.

The tall girl stopped her drill.

The blows went on, slower and lower. After a while Tor began to talk under his breath, one word to a blow. The girl bent to her bootlace a few strides away.

"Weight," said Tor, and hit the pell. "Spread." He hit it. "Line. Turn." He hit it. "Air."

The bar came down on the sand. He bent for it, and lifted it as far as his knees, and stopped. His arms stayed where they were, trembling. He looked down at them.

"Enough," said Kolb, from the gate. "Go and eat, Baker."

Tor walked toward the arch, still holding the bar. The tall girl caught up to him and took it out of his hands.

"Oh," he said. "Thank you. That's very kind. Thank you, um—"

He looked at her face for some time.

"Petra," she said.

"Petra. Yes. Sorry. I knew that."

She smiled at him. He blinked. Then he went on toward the dormitory, his arms hanging at his sides.

* * *

In the dormitory corridor, the lamps were far apart, and between them it was dark and cold. Two girls came up the stairs. One had dark hair down past her shoulders, loose; the other wore green, with thin sleeves and a comb in her hair. A second-year coming down passed them, looked back, and missed a step.

They stopped at the fourth door on the top corridor. Trice knocked.

The bar lifted inside with a wooden knock, and the door opened a little way, and then a little more, pushed by an elbow. Tor stood behind it holding up both hands, which were shaking.

"Sorry," he said. "Kolb. The pell. I can't quite— Please come in. Sorry. It's a mess."

Paper covered the desk and the bed. On the floor, a sheet with AIR at the top was held down by a boot. On the desk stood a tray with a teapot and a cup, and beside the cup a ring of spilled tea. The collar of Tor's tunic was damp.

He looked at the two of them in the doorway: the loose hair, the green, the comb.

"Oh," he said. "Are you going somewhere nice? I'm sorry, I won't keep you."

"We came here," said Trice.

"Oh! Before, you mean." He smiled. "That's very good of you. Please, sit. Anywhere. Not there, that's the food field. There. Sorry."

Trice sat. She looked straight ahead.

Sara poured the tea and held the cup to Tor's mouth while he drank. "Thank you," he said into it, twice.

"Tor. When did you last eat?"

"Lunch, I think."

Sara looked at him, and didn't ask the next question.

"You had a visitor this morning," said Trice.

"How did you—"

"Everybody knows. There's a first-year who's told it to half the school." She folded her hands in her lap. "Douglas Sorvey offered you two gold a board for the laundry field, and you told him Sara represents you."

Tor went red from the collar up. "I'm sorry. I shouldn't have said it without asking. Only, at the laundry, when Miss Sara said thirty, and then you said— I thought it was sort of— and he was very—" He turned to Sara. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be," said Sara. "You did exactly right."

"Did I?"

"Who benefits, Master Baker," said Trice, "from a man paying two gold for a thing worth thirty?"

Tor considered it. "Him."

"Him. And from paying for your *failures*? He'll pay for every failure you ever make, and then he'll own every failure you ever make, and the successes that come after them, and you'll find one morning that there's a carriage in the court for you, and it's not taking you anywhere you'd have chosen. That's how the Sorveys buy. They buy early, and then they own the stable."

"He did seem," said Tor slowly, "very sure."

"He's always very sure. It's his best quality."

Sara set down the cup. "Twelve gold," she said. "Each. That's what the Debri house will pay you, for as many as you can make. And twenty if you can get it onto a metal plate."

Tor made a sound that wasn't a word. "Twelve. He said two, and I thought two was—"

"I know what he said. Pine warps, Tor. Paint flakes. It's a beautiful field and it's on a board that'll be firewood in five years, and the people who buy fields like this know it, and that's why it's thirty and not three hundred. Put it on copper, or brass, anything that won't warp, and it lasts. Lasting is what people pay for."

Tor was no longer looking at her. He was looking past her, at the lamp on the desk, at its brass foot. His lips moved.

"You'd have to etch it," he said, to the lamp. "Paint won't sit on metal, not properly. You'd etch it, with acid. The store has— I'd need to think about the depth of the line—" He stopped and looked back at Sara. "Sorry. Yes. Twenty. That's— yes."

"Good," said Sara.

Tor's face changed.

"Only," he said, "I might not be able to. To make very many. Not past two weeks." He looked down at his hands on his knees. "Kolb's set me a shield. It has to stop his blows and a cutter, for twenty minutes, with me inside it. And if it doesn't—" He stopped. "I'd hate to promise the house a lot of boards and then be— not able to deliver them."

Trice looked at him for a long moment.

"Master Baker," she said. "We have every confidence in you. As it happens, we heard Kolb discussing you with the head of our department yesterday, after the bell. He said you'd manage it. He said he wouldn't have set it otherwise."

"Your department?"

"Mm," said Trice. "And besides, the school doesn't kill students. Hardly ever. They're paid by the head."

Tor looked at her with wide eyes.

"Oh," he said. "That's— thank you. That's very reassuring." He sat back a little. "So he only said it to make me work harder. So that I'd take it seriously."

"Yes," said Sara.

"That's very clever of him." A pause. "He did cut the pell in half, though."

Nobody spoke.

"There's one more thing," said Sara. Her voice had changed: slower, more even. "If I'm to represent you. I'd do the selling, and the carting, and the arguing, and you'd do the building and nothing else. And the split would be ten in the hundred."

"Ten," said Tor.

"Ten in the hundred. On everything sold."

His lips moved briefly. "Is that usual?"

"For a first contract it's usual."

"Then yes," he said, and smiled at her, wide. "Yes. Thank you. That's very kind."

Trice looked at Sara. Sara kept her eyes on Tor.

"Good," said Sara. "Then that's done."

She stood, and Trice stood, and Tor got up so quickly that his foot knocked the boot off the AIR sheet.

"I'd give you a card," said Sara. She made a small gesture down at the green dress, which had no pockets. "But. If you need us, ask Rolph. Or Tovey. Either of them can find us."

Tor gave a small, polite laugh.

"Goodnight, Master Baker," said Trice.

"Goodnight. Thank you. Thank you for coming. Before you go wherever you're going. I hope it's nice."

The door closed. The bar dropped into place with a soft knock.

* * *

At the head of the stairs, Sara put her hand to her hair, where the comb had been, and stopped.

"Go on," she said to Trice. "I'll catch you."

She went back along the corridor, quietly. At the fourth door she raised her hand to knock. From the other side came Tor's voice, low.

"Ask Tovey. Ask Count Thompson. To go and *fetch* them. For me." A short breath of a laugh. "Very good. That's very good." Then, quieter: "I suppose I deserved that."

Sara's hand stayed in the air.

Behind the door, a pen began to scratch. "Copper," said Tor's voice. "Not pine. Copper, acid, the depth of the line. Air but not dust. Twelve, twenty." A pause. "Ten in the hundred." A pause. "Very kind."

Sara lowered her hand and walked back down the corridor.

Trice was waiting at the foot of the stairs with her arms folded.

"Where's your comb?"

"I'll get it tomorrow." Sara put her arm through Trice's. "He thinks we were joking. About Tovey."

"Of course he does."

"He thinks Tovey would be insulted to be asked."

"Tovey would be delighted to be asked. He'd go and get us himself. He'd bring a lantern." They walked a few steps. "And Rolph?"

Sara was quiet for a moment.

"He didn't even hear Rolph," she said.

They went out into the court. The bell tower stood black against the stars. Behind them, one window on the top floor of the dormitory stayed lit.
