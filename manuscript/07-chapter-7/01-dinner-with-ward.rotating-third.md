Rolph turned to see what Tor was looking at, and his face changed.

Tor had never seen it do that. In two years he'd seen Rolph's face do almost everything a face could do: laugh until it cried, go white at the news about Wensa, sulk magnificently over a lost bet, go gray and stubborn at the end of Kolb's two hundred cuts. He had never once seen it frightened. It wasn't quite frightened now. But it was very near it, and it was turned on Tor.

"I didn't know," said Rolph. His voice had gone low and quick. "Tor. I swear I didn't. I didn't know she'd married *Ward*. If I'd known, I'd never have— I wouldn't have let you come down. I'd have said you were ill. I'd have—"

He'd said it too loudly. Tor saw heads turn: Countess Thorgood's, Ursula's, one of the footmen's by the sideboard.

And then the queen was there.

She didn't seem to hurry. She simply stepped past her son, a long smooth step in her midnight blue, and turned, so that all at once she was standing between Tor and the rest of the room, and he could see nothing but pearls and lace and the copper square's gray string at the back of her neck where her hair was put up. She looked down at him. She didn't say anything at all. She raised her eyebrows, very slightly, and waited.

Tor opened his mouth, and nothing came out of it.

There wasn't room. Everything had come up into his throat at once, hot and thick and stupid, and filled it: a dormitory corridor in his first year, and the noise at the end of it, and a girl's voice going up and up until it cracked, *poor*, and *ugly*, and *how dare you*, and *above yourself*, and all the doors along the corridor opening one after another, and faces in them. And the weeks afterward. The ceiling of the room. The ceiling for days. Rolph bringing up bread and leaving it on the desk, and taking it away again, and bringing more.

He shook his head. It was all he could manage.

*It's stupid,* he told himself. *It's three years ago. It's nothing. It's a girl on a man's arm at a dinner. It doesn't mean anything now. It can't.*

He breathed in, slowly, all the way down, the way he did before a build, and let it out. He reached for the quiet place, the one under everything, where the room went away and there was only the pattern. He reached for it as hard as he'd ever reached for anything.

It didn't come. Not properly. The room stayed. The queen's face stayed, and the pearls, and his own heart going. But the breathing helped a little. His hands, behind his back, unclenched.

"I'm all right," he said. His voice came out small and flat. "Really. It's nothing important."

He could feel his face. It was burning, all of it, all the way down into his collar, under the king's silver falcon.

And worse than that, worse than all of it, was the thing he'd only just understood, standing here in the cool with the queen in front of him: he hadn't *known*. Back then. He hadn't known she was anything but a girl in his year, no taller than he was, who sat by the window in theory and laughed with her friends at the long table. He hadn't known she had a drop of noble blood in her. He'd thought she was like him. That was why he'd dared.

No wonder she'd screamed.

The queen looked at him a moment longer. Then she looked past him, at Rolph, and tilted her head the barest fraction.

Rolph shook his own head, slowly. When he spoke it was hardly above a mutter, the way you'd say a thing you didn't want to be true.

"It's Wylde and Doretta," he said. "Isn't it. Only maybe for real." He swallowed. "And worse. Doretta was kind. In the story. She said it gently. And she had a reason."

Tor didn't understand a word of it.

But everybody else did. He saw it go round the little group, the way a draught goes round a room: the queen's face, gone very still; Trice's head turning sharply toward Rolph, then toward the door, then back to Tor; Countess Thorgood's hand going to her throat; even Ursula, who had been standing straight and gray and silent by her father, looking at Tor suddenly as if she'd never seen him before. Nobody said anything. The silence had a shape to it that Tor couldn't read and didn't like.

Then the queen turned round.

She turned all the way round, with her back to Tor and her face to the room, and as she turned her arm came round with her and settled across his shoulders, light and warm, as if it had always been there. And she smiled at the Count and Countess Ward, who had reached them.

The bowing happened again. Tor did it without thinking: from the shoulders, hand to the falcon, the way he'd seen it done, down and up. When he came up, he was looking at Maria's shoes, and then at the hem of her green dress, and then, because there was nowhere else to look, at her.

She'd grown. That was the first strange thing. At school she'd been his height, near enough; now the top of her head was well above his, almost to Ward's shoulder. She'd grown into her face, too. It was a fine face, still, with the same quick eyes. She was not looking at Ursula. She was so carefully not looking at Ursula that Tor could feel it from where he stood, the way you feel a draught from a door somebody's left open on purpose: her eyes went to the queen, and to Rolph, and away from the place where Ursula stood, and back to the queen. The king was beyond her, and you didn't stare at the king. There weren't many other places left to look.

And then they came to him.

She smiled.

It was a perfectly good smile. A polite one, the kind you'd give to a stranger at a dinner. But what came off her, under it, through the cool air between them, wasn't polite at all. It was tight and uneasy and moving, like a hand that wouldn't keep still in a lap. He felt it the way he'd felt Dallow's gladness and Karina's sums. Troubled. Something in her was very troubled indeed.

She doesn't know me, thought Tor. Of course she doesn't. Why would she? I'm a falcon on a black coat. I'm a boy at a dinner.

He was almost relieved.

* * *

They were seated at last, and Trice made sure she was across from him.

It had taken some work. Her aunt had placed the table, and her aunt's placing was not lightly disturbed; but Trice had caught Quavel's eye on the way in, and Quavel had caught hers, and somehow, when the chairs were drawn out, Trice was sitting directly opposite Tor with Ursula at her right and a clear view of the far end of the table, where Count Ward sat down heavily in the last chair, with Maria at his side.

Trice had seen the snub. Everyone had; it had been made to be seen. Maria had bowed to the king, the queen, the prince, the Thorgoods, and had not let her eyes rest for one instant on the girl standing beside them. It was neatly done. It said, before anyone had said a word about brooches: *there's a girl at this table who took a married man's gifts, and I am his wife, and I will not look at her.* Trice turned it over and admired it, the way you admire a good cut in the yard that's just been made on someone else. Ward hadn't thought of that. Ward couldn't have. She looked down the table at the slim figure in green, sitting very upright with her hand near her husband's sleeve, and filed her, in a column of her own, near the top.

Tor looked terrible.

Not to anyone else, perhaps. To anyone else he looked like a well-dressed boy sitting very straight with his hands in his lap and the king's falcon on his breast. But Trice had seen him in a ring of fire, and she knew the difference. His face had gone the color of candle wax. His eyes were open and steady and looking at the white cloth in front of him, and they weren't seeing it. His lips moved, very slightly, and then stopped, and then moved again. He was trying to go under. She could see him trying. She could see it not working.

The servers came round with the jugs. Wine for everyone, dark and red; and for Tor, without anyone saying a word, a tall glass of something pale and cloudy that smelled of pears. Everyone at court knew by now that the scholar didn't drink. Something about keeping his head clear for the building. Trice had heard it from three different people in two days, always in the same tone, the tone people used for the habits of very rich old men.

The glasses were filled. Her uncle reached for his.

And Tor, without looking up, without any idea what he was doing, put out his hand and picked up his pear juice and drank.

Trice saw it happen and couldn't stop it. Nobody could. It was one sip. It went down the length of the table like a stone into a pond, and the ripples went out from it, and she watched every face at the table do the arithmetic. Ursula's. The Thorgoods'. Maria's, very fast, and then perfectly still. Ward's, slowly.

Her uncle put his glass back down.

He stood up. All of him, the whole great height of the King of Noram, rising at the head of the table in his plain black, and he turned toward Tor, and bowed. Not deeply. Just enough. Just enough for everyone to see it.

"You honor us," said the king.

Tor looked up, blinking, as if somebody had called his name across a field.

Before he could say anything, before anyone could, Ursula picked up her own glass and drank. She drank a great deal of it, a long, steady, deliberate swallow, with her chin up and her eyes on the king, and put the glass down, half empty.

Her uncle turned, and bowed again, a little less, to her.

Trice let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding. *Clever girl*. There it was, done, in front of Ward and Thorgood both: the crown's own guest had drunk first, and the crown had thanked him for it; and the girl with the dragonfly had drunk second, and the crown had thanked her too. Nobody who'd seen it would ever be able to say the king had sat Ursula Thorgood at his table as a suspect.

Her uncle sat, and drank. Her aunt drank. Alphonse drank. Trice drank.

Tor didn't.

He was sitting with his hands back in his lap, looking at his glass with a puzzled frown, as if he wasn't sure whether he'd done something wrong or something right.

Trice kicked him.

Gently, under the table, on the ankle. Nothing. She kicked again, harder. On her right, Ursula's foot came across, quite unashamed, and took him on the shin. And behind Tor's chair the old wine steward, who was the only man in the room without one of the copper squares at his throat, leaned forward and said very quietly into his ear, "Your glass, sir," and touched his elbow.

Tor jumped, and drank.

The Thorgoods drank. And then the whole table was very quiet, and very busy not looking at the end of it, where Count Ward sat with his red coat and his glass in his big hand.

He looked at the glass. He looked at it for rather a long time. Long enough that the countess beside her husband made a small sound in her throat. Long enough that Maria's hand came to rest, very lightly, on his sleeve.

Then he drank.

* * *

At a royal table nobody talked business while the food was on it. Trice had been raised on that rule; she'd seen grown dukes sit through six courses with a war on their faces, talking about horses. So the soup came, and the fish, and the little roast birds, and the talk went round the table the way it was meant to, light and pleasant and about nothing: the heat, the river, a new singer at the glass halls, somebody's daughter's betrothal, somebody else's terrible horse.

Tor didn't join in. He ate what was put in front of him, carefully, and answered when he was spoken to, carefully, and in between he sat very straight and looked at his plate as if it might tell him something. Twice Trice saw his eyes go to the end of the table, and come back, fast, like a hand off a hot pan. She wanted very badly to kick him again, and didn't know what for.

"Your Majesty, I must ask," said Countess Thorgood, over the birds, with the bright determination of a woman who has decided that *somebody* is going to make conversation, "how on earth do you keep this room so cool? In *this*? I've been melting since the river gate."

"Oh, it'll be one of those new plates, surely." It was Maria. Her voice was light and pleasant and carried exactly as far as it needed to. "We've an order in at the Debri house. Everybody has. There's a list." She smiled. "Even for counts, I'm afraid. We're quite far down it."

It was nothing. A remark. The kind of thing you said over a roast bird to fill a silence.

Her aunt answered it anyway.

"Yes," said the queen. She said it softly. Her voice had gone the texture Trice had learned, at nine, to dread: soft and warm and without a single edge you could catch your fingers on, like velvet laid over a blade. "I'm told the Debri house is giving the shields and the flying rigs first place. For the common defense. Which seems very right." She lifted her glass a fraction. "We're more fortunate here. Wizard Torrance has *lent* us the plate that's cooling this room. His own. And a set of very nearly priceless devices besides, to keep our people cool on their way to the kitchens and back." She smiled down the table. "A very generous gift. I'm not sure I've had a kinder one in years." She turned her head, without hurrying, to the man standing six feet behind the king's chair. "Quavel. How are you finding them?"

Trice watched Quavel understand.

She saw it go through him: the question, and what the question was for, and who it was really addressed to. He stepped forward exactly one pace. He put his hand, flat, to the front of his black coat, over the place where the copper sat, and bowed to the queen, and then, very slightly, to Tor.

"Your Majesty," said Quavel. "I've stood beside the great ovens tonight, ma'am, at the hour the bread goes in. And I've been as comfortable as on a spring morning." He paused, precisely. "I've been in this house twenty years, ma'am, and I have never once said that about the kitchens in summer."

"Thank you, Quavel," said the queen.

He stepped back. Down the table, Maria smiled, and said nothing.

*Ours,* her aunt had said, without saying it. *He is ours. He gives us things he doesn't give the Debri house. Count yourself lucky to be eating in his cool.*

Trice looked across at Tor. He'd gone pink at *Wizard*, and pinker at *priceless*, and was now looking at his plate again. She waited until his eyes came up, the next time, and caught them, and mouthed across the table, as clearly as she could without a sound:

*I want one.*

He blinked at her.

*I want one,* Trice mouthed again. *Mine went to Sara's mother.*

Something came into his face then, for the first time since Maria had walked in. Not much. A flicker. The ghost of the look he got when somebody brought him a broken thing and asked if it could be mended. He nodded, very slightly, and Trice sat back, satisfied, and took a mouthful of roast bird.

She noticed, as the plates were changed, that his were smaller. Everyone's were the same size but his. His fish had been half a fish; his birds were one where everyone else had two. She saw him notice it too, and frown at it, and then his face cleared, and he looked down the room at the servers by the sideboard with an expression of shy, enormous gratitude. Trice could almost hear him think it: *they noticed I couldn't finish last night. How kind.* She looked at Quavel. Quavel was looking at nothing at all.

The plates went away. The cloth was brushed. And then the wine came round again, deep and red, for everyone; and for Tor, without a word, a glass of water.

And the meal was over, and the business began.

* * *

It began well enough. Her uncle opened it; her uncle did it the way he did everything, slowly and plainly, setting each thing on the table where everyone could see it. The dragonfly lay by his hand on the white cloth, where it had lain all through dinner without anyone mentioning it, catching the candlelight on its blue and white wings. He spoke of it. He spoke of where it had been found and what the scholar had said of it, and that it was not a thing made in Noram by any hand Noram knew, and that the law of the continent on such things was older than the crown and not his to bend.

Then he stopped, and let Count Thorgood speak.

That was when it went wrong.

Thorgood had been holding it in all evening; Trice had watched him do it, through six courses, like a man holding a door shut against a gale. Now the door came off. He didn't stand; he didn't need to. He leaned his great grizzled bulk forward over the table and said, in a voice that rattled the glasses, that Count Ward had taken *his daughter*, his only daughter, a girl he'd known since she was in her cradle, and pinned a foreign *thing* on her, an Austran thing, and sent her into the king's own house with it on her collar like a carrier pigeon. That he'd made her a spy without so much as asking her. That he'd made her a *traitor* in her sleep.

Ward went dark red, darker than his coat.

He said it was a trinket. He said he'd bought it from a jeweler on the Hill, the same jeweler half the Hill bought from, a pretty thing for a pretty girl's name day, and how was a man to know what was in it, and who was to say anything was in it at all, except some *schoolboy*—

Maria's hand came onto his sleeve. He stopped, and started again, and changed course.

He said perhaps they should ask where else the girl had been. Who else she'd talked to. What sort of company the Thorgoods kept, down in their county, so near the coast. He said it was a curious thing, wasn't it, that a girl promised to the crown since she was four should be so very ready to take jewels off a married man.

Trice felt Ursula go rigid beside her.

She tried. She leaned in, across the corner of the table, with her hands flat on the cloth and her voice reasonable, the voice she'd learned for exactly this. The jeweler could be found, surely. The jeweler could be asked. Perhaps if the count would give the name, the crown could send to him tonight, and then they'd all know, and nobody would need to say anything they'd be sorry for—

Nobody heard her.

They were both on their feet now, the two counts, leaning at each other over the length of the king's table like two bulls over a wall, and the shouting had gone past words into noise. Countess Thorgood had her hands over her mouth. Ursula sat beside Trice perfectly still and perfectly white, with her chin up, saying nothing, because a girl of nineteen did not answer a count at the king's table, whatever he called her.

And Ward, purple, swinging round on Ursula at last, said it.

He said that it was plain enough what *she* was. That a girl of low character would take a gift from any man who offered one. That she'd *wanted* something from him, and been glad of the trinket, and thought she'd have more, and now that it had gone wrong she'd come crying to—

Alphonse stood up.

Trice had known him since before she could walk. She'd seen him angry perhaps twice in her life, and both times it had been the cold, formal, quiet sort. In the two years at Lairdgren she hadn't seen him lose his temper once. Not with Kolb. Not with the Sorveys. Not with *Wensa*.

He didn't look angry now.

He didn't look like anything. His face had gone quite empty, the way Tovey's had gone empty in the courtyard in the dark, as if whoever usually lived behind it had stepped out of the room. He put both his hands flat on the edge of the king's table.

And the table went over.

* * *

It didn't tip. It *flew*. Tor saw it go: the whole long table, with its cloth and its glasses and its candlesticks and its silver, lifted straight up off the floor on Rolph's side as if a giant had put a shoulder under it, and went over and away in one piece, end over end, with a crash like a barn coming down, and everything on it went everywhere at once. Wine across the painted floor in long red tongues. Glass in a bright spray. A candlestick bounced off the far wall at head height. And down the middle of the room, where the table had been, there was suddenly nothing at all but bare floor and Rolph, walking.

He was walking at Ward.

He didn't hurry. He went straight down the cleared floor, big and steady and blank-faced, with his hands open at his sides, and the air in front of him bent. Tor could see it. A chair that had been in his way simply left, sideways, as if it had been kicked by a horse, and smashed against the paneling. A second one went the same way. There was a sound under everything, very low, like a great wind a long way off.

At the far end of the room, Ward had gone the same way. His face had emptied. His shoulders had come up. And something came off him and out across the room in a wave, and Tor felt it reach the people along the walls: the Thorgoods, the countess, the footmen, Trice, the queen. He saw their faces go slack, all together, and their eyes go vague and puzzled, as if they had walked into a room and forgotten why. Nobody ran. Nobody could quite think how to. They backed away to the walls, slowly, blinking, and stood there in a muddle, and watched.

It reached Tor's hands. His fingers went thick and stupid, as if he'd been out in the snow without gloves. His toes went. The edges of his thoughts began to fur over, softly, like bread going stale.

And through the fur, quite clearly, as if somebody had said it into his ear, he saw what was going to happen.

The prince was going to hit a count. In the king's house. At the king's table. In front of the king.

And after that there was no way back. Not for Ward, not for the crown, not for anyone. There'd be a county full of men who'd heard their count was struck by the prince for defending himself, and a city full of men who'd heard the count insulted the crown and got what he deserved, and the brooch on the floor among the wine. There would be men in fields. Rolph had said so. Rolph had said exactly so, in the carriage, that afternoon.

And there was one person in the room who couldn't be hurt.

Tor put his hand flat on his chest, over the fourth shield, and told it *now*.

He felt it come up round him, close and tight, the egg of still air, and the fur went off his thoughts like frost off a window; there was only a little numb tingle left, far out in his fingertips and his toes, and then not even that. He took a breath. It was clean.

Then he walked out into the middle of the floor, between Rolph and Count Ward, and stopped.

* * *

Ward hit him first.

Tor saw it coming: a great red arm, swinging, the way you'd swing at a dog in your way. It met the shield a hand's breadth from Tor's ear and stopped as if it had hit the side of a house. Ward staggered. Tor didn't. He felt it, the force of it, go round him and down, down through his boots and into the floor, and heard the floor crack.

"Sorry," said Tor, automatically.

Ward swung again, from the other side. The shield took it. The floor cracked again.

Behind him, Tor felt Rolph come on, and stepped back and to the left, and was in Rolph's way. Rolph didn't hit him. Tor had been almost certain he wouldn't, but it was a great relief to be right. Rolph only stopped, blank-faced, and leaned, and tried to go round him; and Tor stepped sideways and was in his way again. And again.

It went on like that. He didn't know how long. Rolph trying to go round; Tor stepping into his path, left, right, the way Kolb had drilled into him, early not fast, watch the hands, go left when frightened. Ward on the other side, swinging at the egg, roaring now, and the shield taking it, and taking it, and every blow going down into the floor through Tor's boots with a crack like a branch breaking under snow. The royal guard had come out from somewhere, from behind the paneling, from doors Tor hadn't known were there, in their purple and black, and had made a wall round the king, three deep, rods out, and were standing there, not moving, because there was nowhere they could put a rod that wouldn't hit Tor.

He felt perfectly safe.

That was the strange part. He'd have expected to be terrified. He'd been terrified of Kolb's twenty minutes, of the cutter, of Wensa's rods. But this was only a shield doing what it was for, and two big men who couldn't get in, and one of them wasn't even trying to. He found, after a while, that his mind had started to wander. It wandered the way it always wandered when his hands were busy with something they knew: out, and sideways, and onto the thing that was actually interesting.

Which was Rolph.

Not Rolph. What came *off* Rolph. Tor could feel it, now that he was standing in the way of it, through the skin of the egg: not a push, exactly. Not a blow. A line. A long straight line of force running out from Rolph's chest, straight ahead, wherever he faced, like a rope pulled tight; and anything in the line was caught by it and carried, along the line, until it struck something hard enough to stop it. That was the chairs. That was the table. That was why the candlestick had gone straight to the far wall, as if it had been thrown along a string.

*Anything in the line.*

Tor stepped left, into Rolph's way. Rolph's line hit the egg, and slid off it, and went past him and took a stool off the floor and carried it, end over end, all the way down the room and into a painted god.

*Anything.*

Not only chairs. Not only stools. A thing that was in the line would go along the line. Sand would. Grain would. Water would.

*Water.*

Ford County. The king's voice, an hour ago, tired. All the water in the world, right there, and not a drop to put on a field, because it was salt. Hundreds of miles to the good land, the wrong way. Carts, and carts, and dead oxen.

But you didn't need to cart water, if you could lay a line. A line from the sea. A line along the ground, and anything in it would go along it, and if the thing in it was water, the water would go, and go, and go, as far as the line went. And then you only had to take the salt out. Which was only a purifier. Which was the *second-year sigil*. You'd want ten of them, one after another, the way he'd done for Ashford. A hundred. You'd want—

Ward stopped swinging.

Tor came back. He found he was standing in the middle of the floor with one hand up, as if he'd been about to draw something in the air. Rolph was still there, still blank, still trying, without any hurry at all, to step round him. But Ward had stopped. He'd stepped back a pace and was standing with his great chest heaving, and his eyes, which had been empty, had found something to fix on.

They'd found Tor.

And Ward came at the shield with everything he had.

It wasn't a swing any more. It was both fists, again and again, and his shoulder, and his whole weight, and under it the wave of muddle coming off him so thick that Tor could feel it pressing on the outside of the egg like a crowd pressing on a window. The shield held. Of course it held; it was the fourth one; it had a stop for this. But the floor didn't. Tor felt every blow go down through him and out into the stone, and heard it go: crack, and crack, and a long splintering crack that ran away from his heels across the room.

And all of it, Tor thought, a little dizzily, between blows, was getting them precisely nowhere.

Rolph wasn't going to stop being in a rage because Tor stood in the way. Ward wasn't going to stop hitting. And none of it, none of the shouting or the flying chairs or the wine on the floor, had done a single thing for Ursula, who was standing against the far wall in her gray dress with her hands at her sides and her face like paper, watching her life go out of the room.

That was what they'd come to fix. Not this. *Her*.

Tor took a breath of the clean air inside the egg, and raised his voice.

"*Excuse me!*" he called, to the walls. To all of them: the Thorgoods, the footmen, the guard, the queen, Trice, the muddled, blinking, frightened faces all round the edges of the room. "I'm sorry. Sorry to interrupt. Does anyone— does anyone here know a nobleman? An unmarried one? That Lady Ursula could marry?" Ward's fist met the egg beside his ear. "Ow. Sorry. Anyone at all? It needs to be somebody of the right— three steps, I think. Somebody suitable. I don't know anybody, you see. I'd ask, but I'm not from here."

There was a silence along the walls. A long one, broken only by Ward's breathing and a glass rolling slowly across the floor.

Then, from somewhere near the windows, a woman's voice, high and thin and shaking and, under the shaking, very dry indeed:

"*Howard Turnbull?*"

It was Countess Thorgood.

And the room changed.

Tor felt it, even inside the egg: something going round the walls like a breath let out. Somebody gave a short, astonished laugh and smothered it. The queen turned her head. And at that moment, as if a string had been cut, Ward's arms came down.

He stood there in front of Tor, swaying, with his fists at his sides, and the empty look went off his face, and something else came into it: something slow and stupid and lost, like a man woken in a strange bed. He looked down at Tor. He looked at the floor. He looked at his own hands, as if he didn't know whose they were.

Behind Tor, Rolph had stopped walking.

Tor turned round. Rolph was standing in the middle of the floor with his hands hanging at his sides and his face come back, and it was gray, and wet at the temples, and very tired. He looked at Tor. He looked at the egg round Tor, and at the overturned table at the end of the room, and at the chairs in pieces against the paneling, and at the cracks running out across the floor from under Tor's boots; and Tor saw him understand, all of it, all at once, and close his eyes.

Maria came across the floor.

She came quickly and lightly through the wine and the broken glass, and took her husband's arm in both hands, and said something to him very low, close to his ear, that Tor couldn't hear; and Ward, still blinking, let himself be turned, and led, back toward the far end of the room, the way a big horse lets itself be led by a small groom who knows exactly where the stable is. She didn't look at Tor as she went. She didn't look at anyone. She was murmuring all the way.

Tor watched them go. He thought, distantly, that the countess led the count about rather a lot. And not only across rooms.

Then he looked down.

The floor round his boots was cracked. Not in one place: everywhere. From under his heels the cracks ran out across the painted stone in every direction, splitting and splitting again, a great crack into two lesser ones, each of those into two more, and each of those into more, fine as hairs at the ends, branching and branching across the whole floor of the room like the veins in a leaf.

Like a river, thought Tor.

Like a great river coming down out of the hills, and dividing, and dividing again, because one river can't water a whole country; you have to split it, and split it, into a thousand small channels, until it comes to every field.

He stood very still and looked at it.

"Sir," said a voice, close by. "*Sir*."

A hand was trying to take hold of his shoulder, and couldn't: it came up against the egg a hand's breadth off his coat and slid, uselessly, the way Ward's fists had. Tor looked up. A guardsman in purple and black was standing beside him, a big young man with a frightened face and his rod in his other hand, trying to shake Tor by a shoulder he couldn't reach.

"Sir. Are you all right, sir?"

"Oh!" said Tor. He came up out of the river all at once, blinking. "Yes. Yes, sorry, I'm fine. I was only thinking about something." He put his hand to his chest and let the shield go, and felt the cool of the room come back in round him, and the guard's hand landed on his shoulder at last and made them both jump. "Sorry." He looked round the room: at the wreck of the table, at the wine, at the guard round the king, at the faces along the walls. "Where were we? *Howard Turnbull*." He turned toward the windows, hopefully, to where Countess Thorgood was standing with her hand at her throat. "Who's he, please? Is he suitable? Would he do for Lady Ursula?"

Nobody answered him.

He looked from face to face. Every one of them was looking at him. Every one, all round the room, the guard and the footmen and the Thorgoods and the queen, was looking at him with the same expression, and it took him a moment to place it, because he'd never had it aimed at him before. It was the look people had in the yard when the pell exploded. Shaken to the boots, and frightened, and something else besides: as if, in the whole wrecked room, the one place anybody wanted to be was next to him.

"Is he not real?" said Tor, uncertainly, into the silence.

* * *

It was the countess who explained, in the end. She did it standing among the broken glass with her hands clasped in front of her, very correctly, in the voice of a woman who has decided that the only way through an evening like this one is to carry on as if it were any other.

There was no Howard Turnbull, she said. There never had been. *Howard Turnbull* was what you called the relative every family had and nobody talked about: the cousin who'd gone strange, the uncle at the end of the north wing who wasn't brought down to dinner, the brother who'd gone abroad and not written. Everybody had one. Everybody called him Howard. And sometimes, when a girl of good family had to be out of the way for a while, through no fault of her own (she looked at Ursula, and her voice shook, once), sometimes the girl was married to Howard Turnbull. On paper. Quietly. And went away to the country with him, to his estate, for a year, or two. And then poor Howard died, as Howards did, and the girl came back to town in black, a widow, with a perfectly good name and nobody able to say a word against her.

"It's a kindness," said the countess. "It's what's done."

Tor looked at her. Then at Ursula, standing against the wall with her chin up.

"But that's not *fair*," he said.

He hadn't meant to say it out loud. It came out on its own, the way his hand went to his chest when somebody threw an apple. He heard it ring in the wrecked room, and felt everybody hear it, and couldn't take it back, and found he didn't want to.

"She didn't do anything," said Tor. He said it to the king, because the king was the one who'd decided, and because the king had told him that afternoon that he'd believed her. "You said so. You said you believed her. She didn't know what it was. Somebody put it on her and sent her in. And now she has to go away. For a *year*. Married to somebody who isn't there. On her own, in the country, where nobody can see her, as if she'd done something wrong." He looked down the room at the far end, where Ward stood with Maria's hands on his arm. "And *he* goes home. To his own house. Tonight."

Nobody said anything.

It went on for a long time, the nothing. Tor stood in the middle of the cracks and felt it, and felt his face going red again, and kept his chin up anyway.

Then the king spoke. He spoke quietly, from inside the wall of his guard, and his voice was very tired.

"No," said King Richard. "It isn't fair, Master Baker. You're quite right. It's only the best the law will let me do tonight." He paused. "I'll remember that you said it."

Tor didn't know what to say to that. He bowed, from the shoulders, with his hand on the falcon, and shut his mouth.

Along the walls, slowly, people began to nod. Not happily. The way you nod at a winter you can't do anything about.

* * *

Rolph had a headache like an axe.

It had come on the moment the rage went out of him, the way it always did, the way he'd been told it would since he was a child and had felt it only once before, at eleven, and sworn on his mother's name never to feel again. His mouth was dry as a road. He was so hungry he could have eaten the tablecloth, wine and all. And behind his eyes something was beating very slowly with a hammer, in time with his heart, and the light from the candles that were still standing went into him like nails.

He sat on a chair that a footman had put behind him, because he'd been told to sit, and drank the water somebody had put in his hand, and tried to follow what was being said.

Hyrees. They were talking about Hyrees. The barony of Hyrees, in the east, which had been empty since old Baron Hyrees died without an heir four years ago and the crown hadn't yet found anybody to give it to. A baron was three steps from a countess-heir; just inside the line. So: Lady Ursula Thorgood would marry the Baron Hyrees, quietly, by royal license, at the end of the week. Nobody would meet the baron. The baron was in poor health. She'd go out to Hyrees and live in the house there for a year, and the baron would, in due course, regrettably, die; and the Lady Ursula Hyrees would come back to town, a widow and a baroness, with her name washed clean.

Through his headache Rolph saw Trice look across the room at his mother. He saw his mother look back, perfectly bland. *A barony or two lying about, gathering dust.* He'd have laughed, if laughing hadn't been likely to split his skull.

Ursula hadn't said anything. She stood beside her mother with her hands folded, gray-faced and dry-eyed and silent, and when the countess turned to her and said, "Urs? Darling? It's the best way," Rolph saw her chin go up the smallest fraction more, and not come down.

His mother saw it too.

She went to Ursula. She took both her hands. And she said, very gently, so quietly that only the people nearest could hear, and Rolph was one of them:

"A year, my dear. Only a year. And a widow is free to marry where she likes. Who knows what might be possible, when you come back?" And she glanced, just once, without any weight at all, across the room at her son.

Ursula's eyes followed the glance.

Rolph looked at the floor. At the cracks running across it, branching and branching. He didn't say anything. There wasn't anything he could say; he knew exactly what his mother was doing, and why, and that it would work, and that it was a kindness of a sort, and a lie of a sort, and that neither of those things would matter to anyone but him. He thought, for no reason he could help, of a white house on a hill with a balance on its banners, and a girl in green on its steps, not waving; and then he put the thought away, because it was no use to anyone, least of all him. A merchant's second daughter. Not three steps. Not thirty.

"Yes," said Ursula, at last. "Yes. All right."

* * *

The Thorgoods went. The Wards went: Maria first, her hand on her husband's arm, steering, her face composed; Ward behind her, still slow and baffled, like a man who has lost a fight and can't remember having started one. The doors closed. The guard melted back into the paneling, all but two. One of them had gone first to the wreck of the table and come back with something small in his gloved hand, and given it to the king; and Rolph saw the blue and white wings of the dragonfly go into his father's coat, and not come out again.

And Trice was on Tor before the doors had stopped swinging.

"Are you *hurt*?" She had him by both arms. Rolph, through the hammer in his head, had never in his life seen Trice take hold of anyone by both arms. "Tor. *Look* at me. Are you hurt anywhere? Did he get through? Your hands, let me see your hands—"

"I'm fine," said Tor, bewildered, letting her turn his hands over. "Really. He didn't. It held."

"*It held*. Look at the floor!"

His mother had come too. And Quavel, hovering. And the two guards by the door kept glancing at him as though they expected him to fall over at any moment, and were ready to catch him if he did. Everyone was looking at Tor with the same frightened face, and Tor stood in the middle of all of it in his black coat with the falcon on his breast, looking more and more embarrassed.

Everyone except Rolph's father.

His father had come out from behind his guard. He stood a little way off, enormous, with his hands behind his back, looking not at Tor's face but at Tor's chest, at the place under the falcon where the shield sat; and Rolph knew that look. It was the look his father gave a map.

"Master Baker," said the king. "A question."

The room went quiet.

"When Ward's field came out," said the king, "everyone in this room went soft in the head. My wife. My guard. *Me*." He said it quite plainly. "I've been in a room with an angry Ward before. It's like trying to think through wet wool. But you walked out into the middle of it, and stood there, and argued with him about barons. Your shield stopped his fists. I saw that. But it stopped *that* too. The muddle. Didn't it?"

"Oh," said Tor. "Yes. Mostly. I got a bit of it in my fingers before I turned it on."

"How?"

Tor looked at the floor. He went pink.

"It's Mistress Wensa," he said.

Rolph closed his eyes.

"At school. She's— you know. She's the royal guard there." Tor glanced, apologetically, at the two by the door. "And I think she wants to kill me. Well. I don't *really* think so. Not really. Probably not. But I think it a bit. Sometimes. In the evenings." He rubbed the back of his neck. "So I keep making the shield better. In case. Every time I think of a way she could get round it, I fix it. She's got rods that do things to your head; I've seen them. So I made it stop things that come at your head as well as things that come at your body. That's the fourth one. That's all." He hesitated. "I still haven't worked out poison. So— please don't tell her that. If you write to her. Please."

There was a silence.

His mother made a small sound that might have been a laugh or might have been something else. His father didn't make any sound at all. He went on looking at the shield, and Rolph watched his face, and saw in it, very clearly, a thought that his father was not going to say out loud in this room or perhaps in any room: that for as long as there'd been a crown in Noram, nobody had ever been able to train a rage. That the rages were too dangerous to put two boys in a yard together and let them learn. That there had never, in all that time, been anything that could stand between two raging men and not be killed.

Until tonight.

"I won't tell her," said the king, at last.

"But *look at the floor*," said Trice. She hadn't let go of his arms. "Tor. Look how much he was hitting you. Look what it did to the *stone*."

Tor looked down at the cracks, branching away from where he'd stood.

And his face changed, and Rolph, who knew that face better than any other face in the world, watched him stop being frightened, and stop being embarrassed, and start being something else.

"Oh, but that's not— I'm all right. Truly. It went into the floor, that's all. That's what it's meant to do, it's the second version, like a cake on a plate." He waved a hand at the cracks, dismissing them, and then didn't dismiss them, and looked at them again. "But I was thinking. While he was— while it was going on. About Ford." He turned to the king. "About the drought. The water. I think— I'm not sure yet. I need to think about it properly. But I think I might have something."

His father looked at him.

"Something," said the king.

"Could I come and see you about it? In a few days? When I've had time to—" Tor made a shape in the air with both hands, a long line, and then a lot of smaller lines branching off it. "To think."

"Three days," said the king. "Luncheon. Here."

He held up two fingers, without looking round. Behind him, one of the two guards by the door nodded and took a small book out of his tunic and wrote something in it. Tor looked at the two fingers, and at the guard, and at the king, with an expression of total incomprehension, and the king didn't explain, and Rolph, for the first time since the table went over, very nearly smiled.

* * *

They walked back through the dark palace together, the two of them, along the long lamp-lit corridors, slowly, because Rolph's head would not let him walk fast and Tor didn't seem to want to.

Rolph waited until they were well away from the dining room before he said it.

"I don't do that," he said. "I want you to know that. I don't. I *never* do that."

"I know," said Tor.

"No, I mean it. That was—" He stopped walking. He put a hand on the wall. His head beat. "I haven't done that since I was eleven. Not once. I promised my mother. I hate it, Tor, I hate it more than anything. You lose— you're not *there*. Somebody else is driving. And I don't want you to think—"

"Rolph," said Tor. "We've lived in one room for two years."

Rolph looked at him.

"You've never once lost your temper," said Tor. "Not with Kolb. Not with Dorgal. Not with me, and I put your boots on the wall for an hour. Not with Wensa, and she tried to put me in a cell." He shrugged. "You're the calmest person I know. I know that. I know it better than anybody. One dinner doesn't change it."

Rolph stood there with his hand on the wall for a while.

"I thought he'd declare war," he said. "Father. When the table went. I thought, that's it, that's done, I've done it now." He laughed, and it hurt, and he stopped. "A rage gets you out of most things, you know. At court. Nobody can blame you, exactly; it's in the blood. But it's shameful. It's the most shameful thing there is, to let it out. And in front of him. At *his table*." He shook his head, very carefully. "But he didn't. He didn't say a word about it. Because— I don't know. Because she'd been defended. Right there, in front of everybody, the second Ward opened his mouth. The crown defended her. That's what everyone saw." He looked at Tor sideways. "And then what you did."

"I only stood there."

"You only stood there," Rolph agreed. "Between the prince and a count. In the king's falcon. And nobody got hurt." He started walking again, slowly. "That's why it isn't a war, Tor. That's the whole of why."

Tor didn't say anything to that. He walked along beside Rolph in silence, and after a while Rolph saw that his lips had begun to move again, and that his right hand, down at his side, was drawing something small in the air: a line, and lines off the line, and lines off those.

* * *

In his room the cool was waiting, and the dark.

Tor didn't light a candle. He took off the black coat, carefully, by feel, and laid it over a chair, and unpinned the falcon and put it on the table on the green cloth, where it lay catching a little gray light from the window. He took off the waistcoat and the stock and his boots, and stood a moment in his shirt in the middle of the cool room, with his hand flat on his chest, on the fourth shield, which had held.

Then he lay down on the enormous bed, on top of the covers, in his shirt and his trousers and his socks.

He meant to think about Ford. He meant to think about the river, and the line, and the branches. He meant to think about Maria's smile, and what had come off her under it, and why the room had gone so quiet at a story he didn't know.

He was asleep before he'd finished meaning it.
