Karina knocked, which she almost never did, because this time she needed him to open the door.

It took him a while. She heard a thump, and a muffled word that might have been *sorry*, and the slow scrape of somebody getting up off a bed they'd fallen asleep on top of; and then the door opened a hand's width and the scholar looked out at her round it. He was in yesterday's shirt and last night's black trousers, with no boots, and his hair had given up the struggle entirely. There was the print of a pillow seam across one cheek.

"Oh," he said. "Good morning. Princess. Your Highness." He blinked. "Karina?"

"Good morning, Tor." She smiled at him, and while he was still deciding what to do about it she stepped past him into the room, quite gently, the way water goes round a stone.

It was cool inside. Of course it was. She'd known it would be, and still it caught her the way it had caught her the first time, on the bare skin of her arms and the back of her neck, so that she very nearly sighed. She didn't. She turned round in the middle of his sitting room instead, in the blessed cool, with her back to the green table and the copper plate lying on it like a coin, and arranged her face.

He'd shut the door behind her, she noticed. To keep the cool in. He was standing by it now with one hand still on the latch, looking at her with the faint, patient puzzlement of a boy whose sisters had often come into his room without being asked, for reasons that would become clear eventually.

"I'm so sorry to wake you," said Karina, who wasn't. "I'm going out today. All day. To Lady Arden's, and then on the river, and then to the Fenwicks', and it's going to be *dreadful*, it's going to be like this everywhere." She waved a hand at the window, at the white glare already lying on the roofs. "And I heard— Alphie said— those little ones you made. The ones you wear." She let her eyes go to his collar, where there was nothing. "Is there any chance at all you could spare one?"

He yawned.

He didn't mean to. She could see him not mean to; it came up out of him all at once, enormous, and he got a hand over it, and went pink behind the hand, and said through it, "Sorry. Sorry. I'm not— I didn't sleep much. The night before. Or the one before that, really."

"Of course."

"And I haven't got one." He took the hand away and spread both hands, empty, apologetic. "Not one. I'm so sorry. I gave them all away last night. To the people in the kitchens. And then I gave mine away too, there weren't enough." He looked genuinely distressed about it, as if she'd come to him in need and he'd failed her. "I could make some. If you like. It doesn't take long now. A couple of bells. Only I'd need—" He looked round the room, and his eyes went past the table, and the trunk, and stopped on the desk by the window. "Oh. There's copper."

There was. Karina looked at it. It was a stack of small bright squares, thin-gauge, new, squared off neatly on the desk beside a coil of gray string. She had walked past this room twice yesterday afternoon on her way to and from the fig walk, and stood in it at dinnertime, and she was nearly sure there'd been no copper on that desk. Not like that. Not new.

"Where did that come from?" she said.

"Mm?" He'd gone to it and was counting it with one finger. "Oh, it's mine. I think. It must be. I had some left." He frowned slightly. "More than I thought. That's lucky." He looked up, and she saw the question go out of his face entirely, the way a stone goes out of sight in deep water. "So that's the copper. And I've resist; I've a pot. But I'd need chains. Proper ones. Not *that*." He nodded at the string with something like loathing. "It rots. I found out in the bath. And acid. I've none left at all. Or—" his face brightened "—sandpaper would do, really. Fine sandpaper. You could do the line with sandpaper if you were careful, it'd only take longer."

"Acid," said Karina. "And chains. And brushes?"

"And brushes. Little ones. For the resist."

She looked at him. She thought about the river, and Lady Arden's, and the Fenwick girls, who had all been to Lady Arden's last week and talked about nothing else for days; and she thought about walking into Lady Arden's morning room with a copper square on a fine chain at her throat that nobody in the whole city had but her family and the king's own servants. She let her lower lip go out the smallest fraction.

"I wanted to be away by noon," she said.

"Oh," said Tor. He considered this, quite seriously, as though it were a perfectly reasonable thing to want. "What time is it now?"

Karina lifted the little watch on its chain at her waist and opened the case. It had been her grandmother's; it was old, and heavier than it looked, and very slightly slow, and it ticked like a small determined insect. "Nearly the ninth bell."

"Then there's lots of time," said Tor, with great kindness, as if he were reassuring her. "If you can find the acid. Bring it here. And the chains and brushes. And I'll have them ready by noon. I promise."

She went.

* * *

It took her the better part of a bell and three servants and one visit to the palace silversmith, a very old man who looked at her request for "ten fine chains, plain, not too heavy, by noon" as though she'd asked him to bring down the moon in a basket, and then produced them from a drawer in under a minute. The acid came from the gardeners' shed, where it was kept for something to do with the paths, in a stoppered stone jar that one of the under-gardeners insisted on carrying for her as far as the door of the small house, at arm's length, with his face turned away. The brushes came from her own sitting room. They were very good brushes. She used them for her watercolors.

When she got back, the room smelled of soap.

He'd washed. His hair was wet and combed, more or less, and he'd put on a clean shirt and his brown school trousers again, and boots, and he was sitting at the green table with a row of ten bright copper squares in front of him, each one painted over with something dark and waxy except for a fine looping line that went round and down like the shape of an egg with a tail. There were no candles lit. The room was quite cool. And there was something about the squares, she thought, coming closer, something she couldn't put a name to: a sort of stillness on them, like the stillness on a pond with fish under it.

"Oh, good," he said, when he saw the jar. "That was quick. They're done."

"Done?"

"Made. They work. I only need to put the line in, so you can tell them from the others." He held out his hand for the brushes, and she gave them to him, and he looked at them, and turned one over, and pressed the bristles gently with his thumb, and his face fell.

"Oh," he said. "These are lovely. Are they yours?"

"They're for painting."

"They're beautiful. But the acid would eat them. Look." He showed her the ferrule, the little silver collar that held the hairs. "That's glued, in there. And the hair's soft. You'd put it in the acid and the whole thing would come apart in your hand, and it'd spoil your brushes, and I don't want to spoil your brushes." He gave them back to her, carefully, handle first. "I'm sorry. We'll manage without."

Karina took them back and found, to her surprise, that she was disappointed. Not about the brushes. About having been wrong in front of him.

"How?" she said.

"Outside," said Tor. "Have you got a bit of old canvas? Or a sack? Anything. And a bucket of water."

* * *

They did it in the little paved yard behind the kitchen garden, where the washerwomen hung things to dry, on an old canvas awning that had been folded in a corner since spring. Tor laid the ten squares out on it in two rows of five, painted side up. Then he unstoppered the stone jar, very carefully, holding it well away from himself and from her, and poured a little of what was in it into the jar's own lid, and from the lid, a few drops at a time, onto the squares.

The acid hissed where it touched. It fizzed along the bare line on each square, a fine bright seething, and a smell came up off the canvas like a copper kettle boiled dry, sharp and metallic, so that Karina put her hand over her nose.

"You don't have to be careful about where it goes," said Tor, pouring. "That's the nice part. The resist keeps it off everywhere I painted. It can only bite where the line is. So you just splash it on, more or less." He splashed some on, more or less. "And it doesn't matter much if the line isn't perfect anyway. It's not doing anything, the line. It's only so you can tell these ones from the old ones. It's like— you know when you mark the bread, so you know which loaf's which? It's that."

"It's not doing anything?" said Karina.

"The line? No. It's only a mark." He looked up at her, as if surprised she'd ask. "The *making's* already done. I did that inside. This is just— so you can see it."

She didn't understand, and filed the fact that she didn't, and watched.

When the fizzing stopped he poured the bucket of water over everything, slowly, and the canvas ran with it, and the acid smell went thin and sour and blew away; and then he knelt and scraped the wax off each square with his thumbnail, and there it was on each one, fine and dark and a little uneven, the looping line. He threaded the first one onto one of the old silversmith's chains, and stood, and held it out to her.

"Here," he said. "Try. Put your hand on it, flat, and think *right*. Just *right*. It knows the rest."

She put it over her head. She laid her hand on it, flat, on her collarbone, in the full sun of the yard, where the heat came up off the paving stones like the breath of an oven, and thought *right*.

And the heat went.

It didn't go at once. It went the way the cold goes out of a room when someone opens a door in winter: from the skin inward, quietly, until she was standing in the middle of a white-hot yard in the middle of a white-hot city in the middle of a white-hot summer, and was cool. Not cold. Just cool, all over, like the moment after you step out of a bath. She shivered. She couldn't help it. She looked up and saw that he'd put his own on and was shivering too, with his eyes closed and a small, private, delighted smile on his face.

"Oh," said Karina, quite honestly.

"Isn't it nice?" said Tor, without opening his eyes.

She stood in the sun and felt it, and let herself feel it, for a moment longer than she'd meant to. Then she opened her eyes and put her face back on.

"Thank you," she said. "Really. Thank you, Tor."

"Oh, it's nothing." He opened his eyes, gathering up the other eight squares and chains into a fold of his shirt like eggs. "Where's the kitchen, please? I've missed breakfast. I'm afraid I'll miss lunch too, if I don't find it."

She pointed. "Through there. Past the herb beds. The big door with the steps going down."

"Thank you."

He was turning to go. And Karina, who had been deciding something since the moment the heat went out of her, and had now decided it, stepped forward and kissed him on the cheek.

It was a very small kiss. Quick and light, the sort of kiss you'd give a cousin at a wedding. She'd judged it to the inch. Then she stepped back, and put her hand to her own cheek, and let her eyes drop, and let the color come up into her face, which she could do almost at will, and had been able to since she was thirteen; and she turned, in a little flurry of sage-green skirt, and ran away across the yard and in through the kitchen-garden gate, as if she were overcome.

She didn't look back. She didn't need to. She could feel exactly how his face looked.

* * *

The boy stood in the kitchen doorway for five minutes before anyone noticed him, and Ada Pell, who ran the palace kitchens and had done for eleven years, was sorry about that afterward, because she'd have liked to have seen his face when he arrived.

She was busy. It was the hottest kitchen in the city on the hottest morning of the year, with the bread out and the stock on and lunch for the whole household in hand, and her people were running, and the great range was going like the mouth of a forge. Four of them had the copper things at their throats, the ones the Wizard had given out at dinner, and those four were going about the place like people walking in a cool cellar, quite unhurried and quite dry; and the rest were wet as fish and cross with it, and none of them had time to look at the door.

When she did look at it, there was a boy there.

A small one, in school brown. Brown tunic, brown trousers, boots. Holding a fold of his shirt up in front of him like an apron, with something lumpy in it. Hair damp. He was standing on the top step, just inside the door, very straight, and when he saw her see him he looked so relieved that she knew he'd been waiting for a while, and was too polite to say so.

She took him for one of the new garden boys, or somebody's nephew sent up from the country, and wiped her hands, and came over.

"Well?" said Ada. "What can we do for you, then?"

"Oh, thank you. I'm sorry to bother you, I know you're busy." He looked past her at the range, the steam, the running, with an expression she couldn't quite read: something that was nearly homesick. "Only I missed breakfast. And I'm afraid I'll miss lunch, too. Is there anything— whatever's lying about. I don't mind what. I'm not fussy."

"*Lying about*," said Ada.

But he'd said it so hopefully. She went and found him some. Half a loaf from the morning's baking, still warm in the middle. A good handful of the dried fruit from the crock on the dresser, apricot and pear and a few cherries, from the new boxes the household had been using since the spring. A stack of cheese, sliced thick. She put it all into his free hand and on top of the fold of shirt, since he didn't seem to have any other way to carry it.

"Sorry about the heat," she said, out of habit. "It's like this every summer. You'll want to eat that outside."

But he wasn't listening. He'd put a piece of the dried pear in his mouth, and stopped, and was standing in her doorway with the food piled up against his chest, chewing slowly, with his eyes gone somewhere far off.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's *interesting*." He chewed. "It goes soft. On your tongue. It just goes. And it's all still there, all the pear, it tastes more of pear than a pear does. And then at the end it's a bit chewy." He swallowed, and looked at the rest of the fruit with enormous respect. "Did that come out of one of the boxes? The drying boxes?"

"It did."

"It's better than I thought it would be. Much better. I thought it would be like leather." He ate a cherry, thoughtfully. "Huh."

Ada had a range to see to. She flapped a hand at him, kindly. "Go on, then. Off you go. Out of the heat."

He turned to go. And the cheese slid. And he caught it against his chest with his elbow, and the dried fruit went sideways, and the lumpy thing in the fold of his shirt clinked, and he stood there on her top step trying to hold all of it at once, like a boy juggling eggs.

And then he stopped trying, and looked at her, and his face changed.

"Oh," he said. "But *you* haven't got one."

"Got one what?"

He didn't answer. He knelt down on her top step, among the food, and put the bread and the cheese down very carefully on a clean cloth he took off her dresser without asking, and opened the fold of his shirt; and Ada looked down and saw, lying in it, little squares of copper, each with a fine dark line on it and a fine chain through a hole at the top.

He counted out five, with one finger, and held them up to her in a bunch, chains and all.

"Here," he said. "For in here. For whoever's working."

Ada looked at them. She didn't understand. She looked at the boy, and at the copper, and then past him into her own kitchen, at Danny by the range with one of these exact same copper squares on a loop of gray string round his neck, cool as a cucumber, stirring the stock as if it were a spring morning.

She took one, slowly. She put it over her head, and laid her hand on it, the way she'd seen Danny do, flat, and thought, because she couldn't help thinking it, *oh, please*.

The kitchen went away.

Not the kitchen. The *heat*. It went off her like a wet cloak lifted from her shoulders, and she stood on her own top step in her own kitchen at the hottest hour of the hottest day, and was *cool*; and for a moment she didn't say anything at all, she just stood in it with her eyes shut, the way you'd stand under rain after a drought.

Then she opened her eyes and held it out to him.

"I can't," she said. "Sir. These are for— these are the ones the *Wizard*—"

"They're for the kitchen," said the boy, firmly. He put the other four into her free hand and closed her fingers over them. "All of them. Truly. They're not for anything else. And if you need more, will you tell me? There's—" he glanced into the kitchen, counting heads "—there's a lot of you. I'll make some more." He stood, and gathered up the bread and the cheese and the cloth, and the last of the fruit. "Thank you so much for the food. It's lovely. The pear especially."

And he went down her steps and away round the herb beds, before she could say another word.

Ada Pell stood in her doorway with five copper squares in her hand and one at her throat, cool as a cellar, and watched him go; and it was a full minute before it came to her, from Danny's face, from the way the whole kitchen had stopped to look at the door, exactly who it was she had told to eat outside.

* * *

Tor found a bench in the shade of a wall, under a fig tree heavy with fruit that wasn't ripe yet, and sat on it with the bread on one knee and the cheese on the other and the cloth full of dried fruit beside him, and ate.

It was very good. He ate the pear slowly, piece by piece, pressing each one against the roof of his mouth with his tongue to feel it go. He'd never thought much about the drying boxes after he'd made the template. They'd gone to the Debri house, and that was that. He hadn't imagined anyone eating out of one. He hadn't imagined it would *taste* of anything. He sat on the bench in the green shade and thought that he'd like to try it on plums, and on apples, and on the little sour cherries that grew by the stream at home, and whether you could do bread, and what Mother would say if you could.

Then, because his mind had been waiting all morning for him to give it something, it went back to Ford.

Water. A line. The line that had come off Rolph last night in the dining room, straight out from his chest, the way a rope comes out of a well; and anything in the line went along it, until it hit something. Chairs. Stools. A candlestick.

Water.

The trouble wasn't making the line. He could feel already, sitting there with a cheese slice in his hand, roughly how you'd make a line. It was a push, that was all, a push that kept on pushing, like a hand on the small of your back. The trouble was making it *keep going*. Rolph's line had only lasted while Rolph was in a rage, and Rolph couldn't be in a rage for the whole of a summer, down the whole length of a county, over hills. You'd want something that went on by itself. And you'd want it *gentle*, or it would throw the water about like Rolph threw the chairs. You'd want it to carry the water the way a hand carries an egg.

He needed to try it. He needed to try it small, first. On some water.

He thought about where there was water.

He thought about the bath.

* * *

The first one hit the ceiling.

Tor stood in the white-and-blue bathroom in his shirtsleeves, with the bath half full in front of him and a small square of copper in his hand, and looked up at the wet patch spreading across the plaster overhead, and at the drips coming down from it onto his head, one at a time, and said, "Too much."

The second one did nothing at all. He'd made it so gentle that it didn't move the water; it just sat in the bath and was very slightly warm.

The third one moved the water. It moved all of it, at once, in one direction, so that the whole bathful sloshed up the far end of the tub in a long smooth wave and went over the rim and across the tiles and under the door into the bedroom. Tor stood ankle-deep in it, holding the third square up out of the way, and watched it soak into the hem of the green bed curtains.

"Sorry," he said, to the bed.

He mopped up with towels. He refilled the bath. He sat down on the edge of it and thought, very hard, with his chin in his hands.

The trouble was that he'd been thinking of it as one push. And it wasn't one push. It was a line, yes, but a line was made of lots of little bits, one after another, each one passing the water on to the next, like people in a row passing buckets at a fire. You didn't need each one to be strong. You needed each one to be *gentle*, and to know which way the next one was.

He made a fourth square. He made it as a little piece of a line, the end of one, the bit that starts the water off; and a fifth, as the bit that catches it again; and he laid the fourth at the bottom of the bath at one end, under the water, and stood the fifth up on the rim at the other end, and put his hands flat on both of them, and told them.

The water in the bath stirred.

Then, slowly, from the near end, a thread of it lifted.

It came up out of the bath in a thin bright arc, no thicker than his finger, clear as glass, and went up and over in a long curve through the air of the bathroom, and came down at the far end, right in the middle of the rim, where the fifth square was; and went *into* the fifth square's line, and turned, and came back. Under the surface. Along the bottom of the bath, as a little current he could see moving the soap-scum. And up again at the near end. And over.

It kept going.

Tor sat down on the bathroom floor, on the wet tiles, and watched it. A thread of water, going round and round through the air, over and over, catching the light from the window and throwing it on the ceiling in small bright wobbling coins. It made a sound, a very small sound, a sort of continual soft *tik* where it came down. It wasn't throwing anything. It wasn't hitting anything. It just *went*, the way a stream goes, because that was the way the water had been asked to go, and it saw no reason to stop.

"*Oh*," said Tor, to the bath, quite softly. "Oh, there you are."

* * *

That was how Rolph found him: sitting on a wet bathroom floor in his shirtsleeves, with his knees drawn up and his chin on them, watching a thread of water go round and round over a bath, with a look on his face that Rolph hadn't seen since the morning after the first flight.

Rolph's head still hurt. Not like last night; last night it had been an axe. This morning it was only a sort of dull hammer in the back of his skull, which a great deal of water and two breakfasts had done something to soften. He'd come along the corridor to see whether Tor was alive. He'd knocked twice and gone in, as one did, and followed the sound of trickling to the bathroom door, and stopped there.

"Tor," he said. "What is *that*?"

"A fountain," said Tor, not turning round. "Sort of. It's a test."

"It's *beautiful*."

Tor did turn round at that, and looked surprised, and then looked back at the bath, as if seeing it for the first time.

"Is it?" he said. "I suppose it is. I was only seeing if I could get the water to go along. In a line. Without throwing it." He pointed. "That one throws it, there, and that one catches it. And they pass it back and forth. It's very weak. It'll only last a couple of weeks, I didn't put much in. But it works. I'm pleased it works."

Rolph came and crouched down beside him on the wet tiles, which his trousers would not thank him for, and watched it too. The little arc went up, and over, and down. The light went round the ceiling. It was, he thought, the most purely pleasant thing he'd seen in a fortnight, and he had a feeling he knew what it was really for, and decided not to ask yet.

"You know what that wants?" he said. "That wants to be outside. In the pond. The big one. In the water garden."

"Is there a pond?"

"There's a— yes. There's a pond." Rolph sat back on his heels. "Mother's got people coming this afternoon. The Hill ladies. Half a dozen of them. They come and sit on the terrace over the water garden and drink cold tea and take each other to pieces, very politely, for three bells. Every week of the summer. It's like watching cats sharpen their claws on each other's furniture." He looked at the arc of water. "If there were *that*, out there, going over the pond, they'd have something else to talk about. For once."

Tor was already on his feet.

"Show me," he said.

* * *

It wasn't a pond.

Rolph had grown up with it, and had stopped seeing it about the age of eight, and so he saw it again now through Tor's face, which was the best way to see anything: the water garden lying below the long terrace on the garden side of the small house, all at once, in the full blaze of the late morning. There were twelve of them, the waters. Long narrow rills cut in white stone, running between low hedges. Round basins with lilies in them. A square tank of dark still water where the carp lived, big as dogs, gold and white and black. Little stepped cascades that hadn't run since his grandmother's day, when the old fountains had cracked and nobody had known how to mend them. And in the middle of all of it, the great pond: oval, wide as a parade ground, green at the edges with weed and clear in the middle, with the sky lying on it.

Tor stood at the top of the terrace steps and looked at it with his mouth open.

"*Oh*," he said.

Then he went down the steps two at a time, and Rolph went after him.

They worked at it for four hours. Rolph found out afterward that it had been four hours; at the time, it felt like twenty minutes, and also like the whole of his life. He didn't know anything about fields. He'd never known anything about fields; that had always been Tor's half of the room. But it turned out you didn't need to know anything about fields. You only needed to know where the water ought to go. And that, it turned out, Rolph knew. He'd known it since he was eight, without knowing that he knew it.

"There," he'd say, pointing. "From the carp tank. Over the path. Into the lily basin."

And Tor would look where he was pointing, and frown, and nod, and kneel at the edge of the carp tank with a square of copper, and put his hand on it, and his lips would move; and a thread of water would lift out of the dark tank, and go up, and over the white gravel path, glittering, high as a man, and come down in the very middle of the lily basin without so much as rocking a lily.

"And then *back*," said Rolph. "From the lily basin. Over the hedge. Into the rill."

And it went.

They did the carp tank to the lilies, and the lilies to the rill, and the rill to the round basin at the end, and the round basin over the low wall into the great pond. They did the dry cascades, so that they ran again for the first time in sixty years, in long glittering steps. And then they did the great pond itself: a great rope of water, as thick as Rolph's arm, lifting out of the near end and going up and out in a long, slow, shining loop over the whole width of the pond, the height of the terrace, and coming down at the far end with a sound like rain on a roof; and from the far end, back again, a second loop, lower, crossing under the first.

Rolph stood at the edge of the pond in his shirtsleeves, soaked to the knees, and watched it go over, and couldn't speak.

"Hm," said Tor.

He was standing beside him, looking up at the loops with his head on one side. There was water in his hair and down the front of his shirt. He was frowning.

"What's wrong?"

"That's about half," said Tor.

"Half of what?"

"Of the pond." He pointed down. "Look at the edge."

Rolph looked. The water had gone down. It had gone a long way down; there was a band of wet stone a foot deep all round the rim of the great pond, glistening in the sun, where the water had been an hour ago. Half the pond, Rolph saw, was in the *air*. Going round. And down in what was left, the great gold and white carp were swimming about with an air of mild, dignified bewilderment, rather closer together than they were used to.

"I think that's enough," said Tor. "I don't want to kill the fish." He knelt at the edge and put his hand flat on the nearest square, and the great loop thinned, a little, and steadied, and the band of wet stone at the rim stopped getting wider. "There. That'll do. It'll run like that for a fortnight or so, and then it'll get tired and stop, and the water'll all come down again and the fish can have it back." He sat back on his heels and looked up at the whole of it, all twelve waters, singing, and something came into his face that Rolph knew and loved and had never been able to put a name to.

"It's pretty," said Tor. As if that surprised him.

* * *

Constance had promised the ladies roses.

She'd promised them the rose walk, and cold mint tea, and a quiet hour on the long terrace out of the heat, which was as near to mercy as she could manage in this weather; and she brought them out through the garden doors at the second bell after noon, five of them, Lady Arden and the two Fenwicks and old Dame Coyle and the Marchioness with her fan, already talking, all of them, about the Thompson boy and the Thorgood girl and what everyone said had happened at the palace last night, of which they each knew a different and entirely wrong version.

They came out onto the terrace, and stopped talking.

The water garden was in the air.

That was the only way Constance could think of it, afterward. It was all in the air. It went up out of the carp tank in a long glittering arc, and over the white path, and down into the lilies; and up out of the lilies, over the hedge, into the rill; and the rill ran, bright and fast, the length of the garden, and leapt the low wall in a sheet like glass; and the old cascades that she'd never once seen wet in twenty-six years of marriage were running in long white steps, chattering. And over the great pond, higher than the terrace they stood on, so high that the ladies had to tip their heads back to follow it, a great shining rope of water went up and out and over in a slow loop, and came down at the far end with a sound like rain on a summer roof, and under it, crossing it, a second, lower; and the sun went through all of it and threw light on everything, on the hedges and the white stone and the terrace and their faces, in moving, wavering, dappled coins.

And down at the edge of the great pond, among all that flying water, there were two boys.

One of them was her son. He was in his shirtsleeves, soaked to the knee, with his hair in his eyes, pointing up at something and talking very fast. The other was small, in school brown, kneeling at the water's edge with one hand flat on the stone, looking up at where her son was pointing with his head on one side, frowning, and then shaking his head, and saying something she couldn't hear, and pointing somewhere else entirely. They were arguing about where the next loop should go. They had not noticed that there was anybody on the terrace at all.

Nobody said anything for a long while.

"Constance," said Lady Arden at last, faintly, with her fan forgotten in her hand. "Who on *earth* made that?"

"Our guest," said Constance.

She watched it go down the line of them: from Lady Arden to the Marchioness, from the Marchioness to the Fenwicks, from the Fenwicks to old Dame Coyle, who had once told her across a dinner table that the small house's water garden was a dead thing and a disgrace. She watched it go, the way water goes along a rill. She didn't say anything else. She didn't call the boys up. She sent for the cold tea and had the chairs set along the terrace rail, facing outward, and sat her ladies down in a row in the dappled light; and nobody, for the whole of the next two bells, said a single unkind word about anyone.

* * *

They came up to the terrace at last, Rolph and Tor, when the ladies had gone, soaked and hungry and pleased with themselves, and sat on the top step in the shade with their backs against the warm balustrade and their legs stuck out in front of them, steaming gently.

The fountain went on below them, all twelve waters, singing.

"I'll need salt water next," said Tor, after a while.

Rolph turned his head.

"For Ford." Tor said it as if it were obvious, as if they'd been talking about Ford all afternoon. Perhaps, Rolph thought, they had. "That's the next bit. That one's only moving water. I've got to see if I can move *salt* water, and then put a purifier on the line, so it comes out the other end fresh. Like Ashford, only the other way round. Take the salt out instead of the—" He made a face. "Instead of the other thing." He pulled his knees up. "I thought I'd ask the kitchens. For a barrel. And a pound of salt. I could do a little one, a barrel's worth, and see."

Rolph looked at him for a long moment.

"Tor," he said. "Have you ever seen the sea?"

Tor thought about it. "No," he said. "I've heard about it, of course. In theory. And the king said, last night. About Ford." He frowned slightly. "Actually I've been a bit worried about that. If you take a river's worth of water out of it, every day, all summer, for a whole county. Won't it run out? It'd go down. Wouldn't it. Like the pond." He nodded down at the band of wet stone round the great pond, glistening. "And then there'd be fish everywhere. Gasping."

Rolph put his hand over his eyes.

He started to laugh. He laughed until his ribs hurt and his head, which had almost stopped hurting, started again; and Tor sat beside him on the step, looking puzzled and a little offended, waiting for him to finish.

"Sorry," said Rolph at last, wiping his eyes. "Sorry. Tor. No. It won't. I promise you it won't. It's—" He gave up. There wasn't any way to say it. "You need to *see* it. That's all. You need to stand on a beach and look at it, and then you'll understand. And you'll never worry about it again as long as you live."

"All right," said Tor doubtfully. "Where's the nearest one?"

And Rolph sat up.

"We've a house," he said. "On the coast. South of Ford. We used to go every summer, when I was small. It's right on the beach. There's a terrace, and you walk down off the end of it, and there it is." He was getting to his feet without having decided to. "And it's only a few hours. If we fly. We could go *tonight*. Now, practically. Today's gone, but if we went this evening we'd have two nights there, a whole day in between, and back in time for Father's luncheon. Easily." He was looking down at Tor, and his face had come alight in a way it hadn't in two days. "And we'll send to the Debri house. For Sara. And Trice. They'll come. Trice will come if she has to fly there on her own. Sara will come if I ask nicely." He paused, very slightly. "I'll ask nicely."

Tor looked up at him from the step, with water drying in his hair and the fountain going over behind him in long bright loops, and his face, slowly, broke into a smile.

"A holiday," he said, "from the holiday?"

Rolph laughed again, and held out a hand, and pulled him up.

"A vacation," he said, "from our summer vacation."
