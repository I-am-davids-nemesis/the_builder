The west gate stood open at dawn for the market carts, and Sara and Trice sat on the mounting block beside it with their bags at their feet, and waited.

It was the only cool hour the capital had. The stones of the gate arch still held a little of the night, and the carts came through it one after another, creaking, loaded with melons and onions and cages of complaining hens, and the carters looked at the two young women sitting on the mounting block in plain traveling clothes and decided, every one of them, not to ask. The sky over the roofs was the color of the inside of a shell.

"They could have come for us," said Sara.

"They could."

"Rolph offered. Twice."

"I know." Trice was folding her shawl. She had folded it into a square, and then into a smaller square, and now she was unfolding it to start again. "It was easier this way."

Sara didn't say anything to that. She had spent the night at the Morgan house, at Trice's invitation, which had come with a reason attached about packing, and she had understood the reason was not the reason when Trice met her at the door. The Morgan house in the capital was three windows wide. It stood in an old street on the wrong side of the Hill, where the houses leaned on each other like tired men, and the stair carpet had been good forty years ago and was now mostly the idea of a stair carpet. The duke kept his money in his county, where it did him some good, and kept a house in the capital because a duke had to have one. Sara had looked at the stair carpet, once, without meaning to, and Trice had said, quite pleasantly, "Don't," and Sara hadn't, again.

The Debri house took up most of a street. Tovey's mother's house had a ballroom. Neither of these things, Sara knew, was any business of hers, and she sat on the mounting block in the dawn and kept them to herself, and was a good friend about it, and felt Trice know she was being a good friend about it, which was its own small trouble.

The carriage came down the street at the first bell, plain and black, with no crest on the door, which fooled nobody who knew horses. It pulled up by the gate, and Rolph got down, enormous and cheerful and yawning, with a covered basket on his arm; and after him, in his school brown, with his hair still wet from a wash and sticking up at the back where he'd slept on it, came Tor.

He came straight over to them, digging in his pocket.

"Good morning. Sorry. Were you waiting long? We'd have come for you, you know, Rolph said we could, it wasn't any trouble." He brought out his hand, and there was something in it that flashed: three little squares of copper on three fine silver chains, tangled together. He began, earnestly, to untangle them. "These are for you. I made some more. I owed Sara one, because she gave hers to your house, and Trice asked at the dinner, and Rolph gave his to the man with the soup." He got the first one free and held it out to Trice. "Karina got the chains. Aren't they nice? Much better than string."

"Karina," said Trice, taking it.

"She wanted one, for going out. So she helped." He handed Sara hers. "Where were you staying, Trice? Is it far? I'd have liked to see it."

"Not far," said Trice. "It's very small."

"Oh, I like small," said Tor warmly, as if this settled it, and turned to give Rolph his.

Trice put her chain over her head. She didn't look at Sara. Tor, untangling Rolph's chain, glanced back at her once, and then at Sara, with a small puzzled line between his eyebrows. Then he looked at the gate arch, as though the gate might explain it, and said under his breath, "*Small*," to himself, the way he said things he meant to come back to; and let it go.

Sara watched him let it go. He'd grown up, she knew, in a baker's house in Two Rivers with a houseful of sisters and one stair. He would never, as long as he lived, understand being ashamed of a house that had a roof on it. She found that she was very glad of that, and that she couldn't have said why.

* * *

They went up over the wall with the sun behind them, and the capital fell away under their boots, all its roofs and towers and smoke, and the river shining through it like a wire; and then there was only the land.

It was brown. Sara hadn't seen it from the air since the spring, and it had been green then, every field. Now the fields were the color of bread crust, and the roads were white, and the streams were dry stony lines with a green thread down the middle where there was any water at all. Further south and west it got worse. Ford was a long tawny country of hills with the grass burned off them, where the farms sat in the middle of their dead fields like crumbs on a plate, and the dust from one cart on one road hung behind it in the still air for a mile.

Tor flew at the front, the way he always did, so the rest of them could ride in the still air behind him. He didn't talk. He looked down at Ford the whole way across, with his head on one side, and Sara, flying behind him, could see his lips moving.

Then the hills ran out.

There was a line of haze across the end of the world, and a smell came up through it, sharp and green and rotten all at once, a smell Sara had known all her life from the river docks and never smelled this clean; and then the land stopped. It just stopped, in a long ragged edge of bluffs and sand, and beyond it there was nothing but blue. Blue to the end of everything. Blue with a white seam stitched along its edge, where it met the land and kept meeting it, over and over.

Tor stopped in the air so suddenly that Rolph nearly flew into him.

"Keep going," said Rolph, laughing, and swung round him. "It's not that. That's just *from above*. Wait."

He took them south along the coast, low over the bluffs, looking for something. He looked for some time. He went down to look closer at a bay, and said "No," and came up again; and then at another bay, and said "*No*, that's the wrong rock," and came up again, and Trice said, "You don't know where it is," and Rolph said, "I know exactly where it is, it's *moved*," which was a thing Sara intended to remember.

Then he found it. A line of old gray stumps marching out of the sand and into the surf, square and pitted, far older than anything that had any business being on a beach: the remains of something that had once gone out into the sea and now went nowhere. The waves broke white against them, one after another. "The Teeth," said Rolph, triumphantly, and turned in over them, and brought them down onto the sand.

The house stood above the beach on a low rise, with the dunes in front of it and the grass blowing on the dunes.

Sara, who had been prepared for a palace, looked at it and found herself pricing it before she could stop herself, and then had to price it again, because the first number had been too low to be right. It wasn't a palace. It was a long low house of gray shingle, silvered by salt, with blue shutters gone pale and a deep roof that came down over a wooden terrace running the whole length of the front. There was a chimney at each end. There was a garden of sorts, which was mostly sea-grass and a bench. Beyond the house the land went on, open and empty and gold, as far as she could see, with no other roof in it, except one: a small cottage, far back by a gate in a low stone wall, with smoke going up from its chimney straight as a string. A dozen beds, she guessed. Perhaps fourteen, if they were friendly.

"It's—" she started, and didn't know how to finish it kindly.

"*It's a proper size*," said Tor.

He was standing on the sand below the terrace, looking up at it, with an expression she hadn't seen on him in the whole of the capital. He looked as if something had stopped pressing on him.

"It's the right size for a house," he said. "You could know where everything was. You could hear someone call you from the other end." He turned round to Rolph. "Would anybody mind, do you think, if I just stayed here? For the rest of the summer? I'd be no trouble. I'd keep it nice."

"All *summer*?" said Rolph.

"I could make things. There's nobody to—" Tor waved his hand, vaguely, north and east, toward the capital and everything in it. "There's nobody."

"There's Walt," said Rolph. "In the cottage. He keeps the place." He looked at the house, too, then, and something went across his face that Sara had seen there only once or twice, in the cold snap and at her mother's table: a look of being much younger than he was. "I used to think that. Every summer. When we had to go back." He cleared his throat. "Come on. Never mind the house. That's not what you came for."

* * *

Rolph had promised him the sea, and so he walked him to it the proper way.

Not over the dunes. There was a path through them, a sandy trench between banks of blowing grass, that came out at the end of the terrace, and if you walked down it with your eyes on your feet, the way Rolph had at six, you heard it before you saw it: a long low roar that came and went, came and went, like something enormous breathing in its sleep. Rolph made him walk it like that. He put a hand on the back of Tor's neck and said, "Feet," and Tor, who'd have stood on his head if Rolph had asked him nicely, watched his feet.

Then the grass fell away on both sides, and the sand opened out, white and wide and blazing, and Rolph took his hand away.

"Now," he said.

Tor looked up.

Rolph had seen people see the sea before. His sister had seen it at three, and screamed, and run straight at it. Tovey had seen it at seven and said that it was fine, he supposed, and gone to look for crabs. Rolph had been told about his own first time and didn't remember it, which he'd always thought was a waste.

Tor didn't do anything. That was what Rolph would remember afterward. He just stood there, at the edge of the dune grass, in his school brown, with the wind off the water blowing his hair straight back from his face and flattening his shirt against him, and looked. He looked at it for a long time. The waves came in, one after another, tall and green, and stood up, and you could see the light through them, and broke, and came hissing up the sand in long white sheets and slid back again, rattling the shells, and the next one was already standing up behind it. Out past the Teeth they were breaking on rocks, and going up in white, and the white hung, and fell. And past that there was only the blue, going out and out to a line so far away it was hardly a line at all.

"Oh," said Tor, at last, very quietly. "It doesn't *stop*."

"No."

"Where does it go?"

"Gallaia's that way. Somewhere. Weeks." Rolph pointed, vaguely, at nothing. "After that, nobody's sure."

Tor walked down the sand toward it. He walked slowly, the way people walk into a temple. He went right down to where the sand was wet and dark and shone like glass, and a wave came up round his boots and he didn't step back, and it went back down again and took the sand from under his heels, and he wobbled, and still didn't step back. He went along the wet edge to where the first rocks were, black and furred with weed, and stood by them while a wave hit them and went up in a white burst and came down all over him; and he shut his eyes in it, and licked his lips, and stood there dripping, tasting.

"It's *all* salt," he said, to nobody. "All of it. All the way down."

Then he turned round to Rolph, wet to the skin, with the white still coming down round him like rain, and said, in a voice Rolph had to lean in to hear over the noise, "It won't run out."

"Told you," said Rolph.

"I was worried about the *fish*," said Tor, and began, helplessly, to laugh.

Behind them there was a shriek. Sara and Trice had come down off the dunes with their boots in their hands and their skirts kilted up, and Sara had gone straight into the backwash and straight out again, shrieking, because it was cold; and Trice, who did not shriek, had gone in after her to the ankles with a face of enormous dignity and stood there while it climbed to her knees, and then said a word that the special school had not, Rolph felt sure, taught her. Rolph went down to them at a run, and kicked off his boots on the way, and the next wave came in over all three of them together.

When he looked back, Tor was sitting down.

He'd gone up the beach a little way, to just above where the wet sand stopped, and sat down in the dry, facing the water, with his legs crossed and his hands flat on his thighs. His eyes were half shut. His face had gone smooth and calm and far away, and his lips were moving.

Rolph knew that look. He'd sat up with that look for four days and fed it broth.

"Well," he said, to the sea. "There he goes."

He gave it up, and went and threw Sara into a wave.

* * *

Trice counted two bells by the sun, and then she counted half of a third, and then she got up.

The tide had come in. Nobody had told her that it would; nobody had told *him*, at any rate, because he was sitting exactly where he'd sat down, in exactly the same position, with his hands flat on his thighs and his face turned to the water, and the water had come up the beach to meet him. Every seventh wave or so it reached him now. It slid up round him in a thin white sheet, and soaked the seat of his trousers, and drew off again, and he did not notice. There was a crust of white drying in his hair. There was sand stuck to one side of his face. His lips were still moving.

She stood over him with her boots in one hand and looked down at him for a while, and was angry, in the cold and quiet way she was only ever angry with him; and noted that she was, and put it down in the account with the rest.

Then she went up to the terrace, where there was a wooden bucket under the steps with a crab's leg in it, and tipped the crab's leg out, and brought the bucket down, and filled it in the next wave, and came back and stood behind him, and poured the whole of it over his head.

He came up out of the trance the way a fish comes up out of a pond: all at once, choking. He coughed, and spluttered, and doubled over, and got the heels of his hands in his eyes, and coughed up what sounded like a good deal of the Pacifico; and then he sat there, dripping, gasping, with his hair in his face, and licked his lips.

And then he said, in tones of real wonder, "*Oh*."

"Tor."

"No, that's— *oh*. That's what it's like *in* the water." He licked his lips again, thoughtfully, like a man tasting a sauce. "When it's gone in. Dissolved. I couldn't get that, I could only feel it from outside, and that's not the same, and that's—" He looked up at her, streaming. "Thank you."

"That," said Trice, "was not help."

"It was, though." He was serious. "It was a great help. I'd never have thought of it." He wiped his face with his wet sleeve, which did nothing. "And it's in the water at home. A bit. I never noticed. Not salt, but something like. In the well. You'd taste it if you knew to." He frowned out at the waves. "I think that's why Mother's kettle goes white inside."

She found she had nothing whatever to say to that, and sat down beside him in the wet sand, because her legs had decided to, and watched him take two squares of copper out of his pocket and lay them on his knees and put his hands flat on them and close his eyes again.

She didn't pour anything on him the second time. She sat beside him with her knees drawn up and her arms round them, and watched the side of his face, and watched the sea, and from time to time she looked north along the coast to where a headland stood up blue in the haze, and past it, and did a small sum about distances, and how long, and how high, and in what dark, which she didn't mention to anybody. Her ears were warm. That was the sun.

* * *

It took him the rest of the afternoon, and when he was done he was so pleased that he couldn't stand still.

He'd put one square on a flat black rock at the very edge of the water, where every wave washed over it, and he'd carried the other one up the beach, counting his paces under his breath, and wedged it upright in the sand forty yards away, near the dunes. Then he came back to the first, and knelt, and put his hand on it.

And the sea stood up.

That was how it looked to Sara. A rope of water lifted off the face of the next wave as it came in, and didn't go back. It went up. It went up in a long, slow, shining curve, as thick as a man's body, over the rock and up and over the beach, higher than Rolph's head, so that they all stood under it and looked up into it; and at the top of the curve it came down toward the second square in the sand, and turned there, as smooth as a ribbon, and went back. Back over their heads, and out over the sand, and down into the sea again, into the trough behind the next wave, without a splash. Round and round. A loop of water, standing in the air over the beach, humming very faintly.

And it was clear. That was the thing. The sea was green and gray and full of sand and weed and drifting bits of things, and the loop that came out of it was as clear as window glass. Clearer. You could see the sky through it without a ripple. Sara put her head back and looked up through it and saw a gull, quite sharp, going over on the other side.

"It's the second one does that," said Tor, beside her, hopping slightly. "The one in the sand. It's a purifier, but the other way round from Ashford, it's not taking anything *out* of the water, it's keeping things *from getting in* in the first place. Salt. And all the little— there are tiny things in it, did you know? Living. Thousands. Like dust, but they're alive. And sand. And stone." He was talking very fast. "That was the hardest. Keeping the stone out. Because the sand's in everything, and it's very nearly the same as stone, so the field kept arguing with me about it. But it's out. It's all out." He reached up and put his hand into the loop where it came down low near the rock.

The water put his hand out again. Not roughly. It just moved his hand out of the way, the way you'd move a child's hand off a hot stove, and flicked a little splash of it over his wrist in passing.

"It doesn't want anything in it," said Tor, delighted. "Not even me."

Rolph put his hand in. Out it came, with a splash. He put both hands in, cupped, and they came out full, and he drank it, and stood still with his eyes shut.

"Sara," he said. "Drink some."

She did. It was cold. It was the coldest, cleanest, sweetest water she'd ever had in her mouth, and there was no salt in it at all; it tasted, if it tasted of anything, of nothing, the way snow does. She stood there in the sand with it running down her chin and did what she always did with magic, without being able to help it: she priced it. The copper. The work. The years. And then she thought of Ford, of the farms in their dead fields like crumbs on a plate, and a loop of this water going over them, and over, and over, and she found that the sum would not come out. It just went on, the way the sea did.

Then Rolph hit her in the face with a wave.

He'd put his whole forearm into the loop, flat, and swept it sideways, and the water had flung his arm out again and taken a great sheet of itself with it, cold and clean and perfectly salt-free, all over her. She stood there and dripped.

"*Rolph*."

"Sorry," said Rolph, not sorry at all, and did it again, and got Trice, who said the word again. Then he did it a third time, to Sara, because she'd laughed at Trice, and a fourth, because she was still standing there. He was very large and it was very easy for him. He was laughing so hard he could hardly stand.

Sara wiped her eyes. She looked at Rolph. She looked at the loop, going round and round over his head, and down along it, to the rock at the water's edge, where the first square lay with the waves washing over it.

She walked down to it. Nobody stopped her. She knelt on the wet rock and put her hand on the copper, flat, the way Tor always did, and thought at it, quite clearly, *stop*.

It stopped.

All of it, all at once. The whole great shining loop let go of the air. For a moment it hung there, a long curve of glass over the beach with the sun in it, and then it simply fell: tons of water coming down at once onto the sand with a sound like a barn roof falling in. It hit the beach and burst. It knocked Rolph flat. It went over Trice to the waist. And then it ran: back down toward the sea in a dozen brown roaring rivers, carving the sand out as it went, deep trenches, knee-deep, waist-deep, with the walls caving in behind them, and the whole lot went back into the next wave in one long brown sigh and was gone, leaving the beach scored and gullied like a field after a flood.

Rolph sat up in a trench, streaming, and laughed until he cried. Trice, beside him, was laughing too, without any sound, holding her ribs. Sara stood on her rock and laughed, and turned round to see if Tor was laughing.

Tor was white.

He was standing where he'd been standing, up the beach, with his hands at his sides. He was looking at the trenches. He wasn't laughing. He looked, Sara thought, the way he'd looked in the practice yard when the fire went up at the shield test, before anyone else had moved: as if he were seeing something that hadn't happened yet.

"Tor?"

"Anyone," said Tor.

He said it to himself. Then he sat down on the sand, quite suddenly, as if his knees had been taken away.

It was some minutes before he could tell them. They came and sat round him, wet, the three of them, and Sara held his hand without asking, and he told them in bits, with his eyes on the gullies in the sand. It was the line for Ford. That was what this was *for*. Not a loop over a beach; a river. A river through the air, all the way from here, up over the bluffs, over the hills, over a hundred miles of farms, two hundred, as much as Ford needed. And anyone could walk up to it and put their hand on it and stop it. Anyone. A child, playing. A goat, for all he knew, if it stood on it the wrong way. A man with a grudge. A man from Austra. And then all of it, all that water, the whole length of the county, would come down at once, wherever it happened to be, on whatever was under it: on a barn, on a road, on a village, on somebody's house with the family asleep in it. And everyone downstream of the break would wake up the next morning with no water at all, in a drought, in the middle of summer.

"I'd have done that," he said. "I was going to go home and *do* that. I'd thought it all out." He was shaking. "I'd never have seen it."

Sara looked at the gullies, and saw it, and her stomach went cold, and she let go of his hand without meaning to and then took it again, harder.

It was Rolph who laughed first. It wasn't much of a laugh; it was the nervous sort, that comes out of you because there's nowhere else for it to go. He reached over and clapped Sara on the shoulder, hard enough to rock her.

"Well," he said, "that's the most useful thing anybody's done all year. Isn't it? Sara Debri, you've saved a county. Possibly two. With your *hand*." He grinned at her. "Best bit of playing about since we were born. I'm putting it in a letter to Father."

"Rolph," said Sara.

"No, I mean it. Trust me." He turned to Tor. He leaned in, and put his big wet hand on the back of Tor's neck, the way he had on the dune path, and gave it a gentle shake. "Little Tor. Listen. It didn't happen. It happened *here*, on a beach, to us, and we're all wet and that's all. And now you know. And you'll fix it. You'll have it fixed by breakfast, probably, and be cross it took that long." He shook him again, gently. "What is it you said to me? Second year. When I broke the lamp you made, and you weren't even angry, and I asked you why not. You said—"

"Everything's got a flaw," said Tor, slowly. "You just have to find it. And then fix it, if you can."

"Well, there you are. She found it." Rolph let go of him. "Fix it."

Tor sat and looked at the sand. Then he looked at the black rock with the square on it, and at the other square up by the dunes, tilted crooked now in the wreck of its trench. His head went over on one side. His lips moved.

"A key," he said, under his breath. "Or a lock. Or it only listens to— no. It *only* listens. That's it. Think, Tor. It only listens to—"

Sara let go of his hand, because he'd stopped needing it, and sat back on her heels in the wet sand, and looked at Rolph over his head. Rolph looked back at her, and raised his eyebrows; and she found, to her own surprise, that she had to look away at the sea.

* * *

Ada had packed them a basket, and it was a beautiful basket, and it was gone by the middle of the afternoon.

There had been a cold roast chicken, two loaves, a great wedge of cheese, a pot of butter, a dozen peaches in a nest of leaves, and a jar of cherries in syrup. It was exactly, Trice saw, looking at the wreckage on the terrace table, what you'd pack for four people's luncheon. Ada had packed them a luncheon. She'd packed it for people who would of course find dinner waiting for them when they got there, because that was what happened when you got anywhere.

"Oh," said Rolph, looking into the basket. "Oh, no."

"There'll be something," said Trice. "In the house. There's always something."

There was a pantry. It was a cool, narrow, whitewashed room off the kitchen, with a little high window full of sea, and slatted shelves; and on the shelves, when the three of them had gone through everything, there were a tin of flour, a bag of yellow corn flour, a crock of salt, a twist of paper with something white in it that might have been anything, a jar of honey gone solid, two jars of jam, a row of dented tins with no labels, a sack of dried beans, a bottle of oil, and a mousetrap.

They stood in the pantry door and looked at it. Trice had been taught to poison a man four different ways using only what could be found in a lady's dressing case, and she stood in front of a tin of flour and a crock of salt and could not see a meal in them anywhere.

"We could fly back," said Rolph. "To the village. It's only—"

Tor came in behind them. He looked past them at the shelves, and opened the twist of paper, and sniffed it, and put a fingertip in his mouth.

"That's soda," he said. And he began to laugh.

It wasn't an unkind laugh. Trice had never heard him laugh unkindly at anything; she wasn't sure he could. But it went on for some time, and he had to lean on the doorpost, and when he looked up at the three of them, standing in a row in the pantry door with their faces all doing the same thing, he laughed harder.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I'm sorry. It's only— all three of you. You can do *everything*. You can talk to kings. And you're standing there looking at a tin of flour as if it's going to bite you." He wiped his eyes. "It's *biscuits*. It's flour and soda and salt and some of the butter, if there's any left, and something wet. That's biscuits. That's dinner. And in the morning—" He was already turning round, looking at the kitchen, at the black iron range and the open hearth beside it, with the light in his face of a man coming home. "Is there a fire? There'll need to be a fire."

There wasn't a fire.

Trice went out for wood, because she was not going to stand there and be laughed at, and came back with an armful of driftwood from the beach, silver and smooth and light as bone, very pleased with it. Rolph had gone the other way and come back with half a young tree off the dunes, still in leaf, dragging it behind him through the kitchen door.

Tor looked at the tree for a long moment. Then he looked at Rolph.

"That's alive," he said.

"It's *wood*."

"It's wet wood. It's wet all the way through. Feel it. It'll smoke and hiss and sulk and you'll be sitting there till midnight blowing on it." He took a stick of Trice's driftwood and turned it over and sniffed it, and nodded. "That'll burn. That'll burn lovely. It'll burn blue, with the salt in it, you'll see. But it'll go in no time. You want those for starting, and then something that lasts." He looked round the kitchen. "Where does Walt put the logs?"

Rolph opened his mouth. He closed it again.

The logs were under the eaves at the back of the house, stacked to the roof in a long, neat, seasoned wall, where they had been every summer of Rolph's life, and where he had walked past them every summer of Rolph's life without once asking what they were for.

So they made a fire. That was to say, Tor made a fire, and the three of them did what he told them, which was to break up the driftwood into kindling and hand him logs in the order he wanted and, in Rolph's case, take the tree back outside. The driftwood went up blue and green, as he'd said, in little crackling tongues the colors of a peacock, and Trice knelt on the hearthstones and watched it and forgot, for a moment, to be annoyed. Then the logs caught, and the kitchen filled with warmth and the smell of woodsmoke, and Tor rolled up his sleeves.

He was a different person in a kitchen. Trice had known that, in a general way. She'd seen him at the school bakehouse. But she hadn't *watched* it before, and she watched it now from the kitchen table with her chin on her hand. His hands went into the flour and knew what they were doing. He didn't measure; he looked, and added, and looked. He set Sara to cutting the butter in with two knives, and showed her how, and when she did it wrong he said, "That's it, that's lovely," and quietly did it right after her. He set Rolph to fetching water, which Rolph could do. He set Trice to watching the oven door and telling him when the back of her hand couldn't stand it any more, which she suspected was a job he'd invented to keep her out of the way, and which she did with total attention.

Walt came up at dusk.

He came up the path from the gate cottage with a lantern in one hand and a basket in the other, a long, bent, brown old man with a white moustache and a straw hat, and stood in the kitchen door, and looked at Rolph for a long moment. Then he said, "Thought that'd be you. Nobody sent," and Rolph said, "*Walt*," and went across the kitchen and hugged him, carefully, the way you'd hug a fence post you were fond of.

In the basket there were eggs. Two dozen, brown, with straw on them, and a crock of milk with a cloth over it. "Hens don't know there's a drought," said Walt, setting it down. He looked at Tor, floury to the elbow, and at the biscuits coming out of the oven on their tray, and nodded slowly, as if a question he'd had about the evening had been answered. "You'll do," he said.

He wouldn't stay to eat. He took a biscuit, hot, and broke it and looked at the inside, and ate it, and nodded again. He'd be down at the cottage, he said, on the porch with his pipe, same as always. He slept light. Always had, with this one in the house. He looked at Rolph, and then, without any expression at all, at Trice and at Sara, and then at Tor, and said, "Goodnight, young sirs. Young ladies," and took his lantern and went.

Trice watched the lantern go away down the path in the dark, bobbing, and stop by the gate, and stay there. A small orange point came and went beside it, which was the pipe. She filed the porch. She filed the distance to it, and that the path was gravel, and that he slept light. She did it from habit, and then looked down at the biscuit in her hand and was briefly ashamed of herself, and then filed that too.

* * *

They ate in the big room, on the floor, round the fire.

There were chairs. Nobody used them. There was a long old room at the front of the house with a low beamed ceiling and a hearth at the end of it you could have stood up in, and faded sofas with sheets over them, and a rag rug, and the shutters open to the terrace and the dark and the long breathing roar of the sea; and the four of them sat on the rug in front of the fire with the tray of biscuits between them and the honey jar, which Tor had stood by the hearth till it ran gold again, and ate until they couldn't.

The biscuits were extraordinary. They were light and hot and broke apart in layers, and the salt in them was just enough, and the honey ran into them; and Rolph, who had eaten at the king's table every night of the summer, ate nine, and lay back on the rug with his hands on his stomach, and looked at the ceiling.

"Right," he said. "What's next?"

He said it hopefully, to the room. A cushion hit him in the face.

"Homework," said Trice.

"It's the *holiday*."

"It's a holiday for some." She didn't look at Sara. Sara didn't look at her. "There's work for school. The summer kind. You remember."

"Oh," said Rolph, from under the cushion. "*That*." And he was quiet.

"You've got homework?" said Tor. He was sitting cross-legged with his back to the sofa, flour still in the creases of his knuckles. "In the summer? What sort?"

"The dull sort," said Trice.

"Oh." He accepted it, the way he accepted most things that were said to him in a definite voice. Then he looked at the fire, and smiled, privately, at something.

"What?" said Sara.

"Nothing. Only I was thinking. About this morning." He touched the fine silver chain at his own neck. "About Karina. When she got the chains. She was ever so kind about it, and then when the amulets were done she—" He went pink, quite suddenly, from the collar up. "Well. She kissed me. On the cheek. And then she sort of ran off. As a joke." He laughed. "I kept thinking, if I told them that at home. In Two Rivers. *A princess kissed me.* Teira'd laugh till she was sick. Nobody'd believe it for a minute."

Sara looked at Trice.

Trice looked at Sara.

It was not a long look. It didn't need to be. They had known each other for nine years, and there were looks between them that held whole conversations, with footnotes; and this one said, roughly, *Karina*, and *Karina doesn't run*, and *Karina hasn't done anything as a joke since she was nine*, and *one of us had better*, and *you, then*.

"Tor," said Trice.

"Mm?"

"Karina doesn't make jokes like that."

"Oh, she does," said Tor, comfortably. "She's lovely. She was only being—"

"Tor. Listen." Trice sat up. She folded her hands in her lap, which was what she did when she meant to say something in order, from the beginning, with the sums at the bottom. "I'm going to tell you something about us. About nobles. And you're not going to like it, and you're going to need it, so listen anyway."

He listened. He always did, when you said that; he sat up straight, with his floury hands on his knees, like a boy in the front row.

"Nobody says no," said Trice. "Not among us. Not to anyone. Not if it can possibly be helped. Not *out loud*." She watched it go in. "If someone asks me to dance and I don't want to, I don't say no. I say how kind, and my card's full, and perhaps later, and then later never comes. If someone wants to marry me and I'd sooner die, I don't say so. My father says it would be premature. And it goes on being premature until it goes cold by itself, and everybody's face is saved, and nobody ever has to have been *refused*."

"But that's—" Tor frowned. "That's not true, though. Any of it. It's lying."

"It's manners."

"It's why they never pay their bills on time," said Sara, to the fire. "They can't bring themselves to tell you they're not going to."

"*Thank* you, Sara." Trice didn't take her eyes off him. "We do it because a no is a debt. You put someone down in front of people, you shame them, you tell them they're not good enough; and they remember it. They don't forget. They grow up, and they get power, and they come back to collect. There are stories. Every noble child hears them before they can read."

"What stories?"

"Count Wylde," said Trice.

She watched his face. Nothing. He'd never heard the name in his life.

"Who's Count Wylde?"

"A long story. Not tonight." She went on before he could ask again. "The point is, we all learned it. We're all afraid of it. We're kind to each other, Tor, all of us, the ones who hate each other most of all, because we're terrified of what happens if we aren't. And now you have to learn it too. You especially."

"Me?" He looked genuinely startled. "Why me? Nobody's going to ask me to dance."

"A great many people," said Trice, "are going to want you."

"Oh, the *building*." His face cleared. "Yes. Well, I know that. Sara says. But that's not the same as—"

"I don't mean your squares."

He stopped.

Trice found that she had to go on now, because she'd started, and that it was considerably harder than she'd expected. She kept her hands folded. She kept her voice even, as if it were a matter of accounts, which it was. "You've a good face, Tor," she said. "A *very* good face. You don't know it, because nobody's ever told you, and it's time somebody did. Half the girls at the Ward dinner were looking at you. Not at the prince. At *you*. And when they ask, and they will, and you don't want them, you can't just walk off and start thinking about copper. You have to be kind about it. You have to let them down so gently they never feel the floor." Her ears were burning. She could feel them. She left them to it. "Because if you don't, you'll have made an enemy for life, and you won't even know you've done it."

There was a silence. The fire cracked. Out in the dark the sea came in, and stood up, and broke.

Then Tor laughed.

He laughed quite gently, and kindly, and a little sadly, the way you'd laugh at a friend who'd said something lovely and silly, and you didn't want to hurt them by pointing out how silly it was.

"That's very kind of you," he said. "Both of you. Really. But Trice. Look at me." He held his arms out a little, as if to show her: the brown tunic, the flour, the hair still stiff with salt, all of it, small. "I'm the smallest person at school. I'm shorter than Davie's little sister. Strangers think I'm about fourteen. Girls don't— it's all right. Truly. I don't mind. I know what I look like." He smiled at her, reassuringly. "Nobody's going to want me like that. So I don't have to worry about being careful, do I? It won't come up."

"Tor," said Sara. "Look at me. She's right."

"You're both being nice." He was still smiling. "It's all right."

He believed it. Trice could see that he believed it entirely, the way he believed the sun came up in the east, and that nothing either of them could possibly say was going to shift it an inch. It was the strangest thing she had ever seen in a face. He thought that *they* were the ones who didn't understand.

Then Rolph spoke.

He'd been lying on his back all this time with the cushion on his chest, looking at the ceiling, so quiet that Trice had half forgotten he was there. He didn't sit up. He didn't use a nickname, which she noticed at once, because Rolph always used a nickname. He said it to the beams, quite quietly, in a voice she'd heard from him only a few times in her life.

"I know why he thinks that."

They all looked at him.

Rolph turned his head on the rug and looked at Tor. Not at Trice, not at Sara: at Tor. And his face was not cheerful at all.

"He's had his Doretta," said Rolph.

Sara went very still.

Trice went still too. It went through her all at once, cold, the way the water had come down out of the air that afternoon: the story from their first year that nobody had quite remembered, the dance, the cafeteria, and a small boy in brown standing in the middle of all that noise; and then the Ward dinner, two nights ago, and a woman in deep green at the king's table smiling at Tor with something troubled under the smile; and the whole length of that table, she saw it now, every noble face at it, looking at the king's small new favorite and thinking, every one of them, the same thing. She had seen the board. She'd seen every piece on it. She had not, until this moment, seen what game they thought it was.

Tor looked from one face to the other. He looked puzzled, and polite, and a little worried, as if he'd said something wrong and couldn't think what.

"Sorry," he said. "Who's Doretta?"

Nobody answered him.

Rolph sat up, slowly, and put the cushion aside, and put another log on the fire, and watched it catch. Then he looked at Tor across the hearth.

"Sit down, Tor," he said, though Tor was sitting. "It's a story. And you're in it."
