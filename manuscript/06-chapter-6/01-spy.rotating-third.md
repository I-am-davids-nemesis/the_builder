Ursula had been introduced to a great many people in her life, and she had never before seen one look at Alphie as if Alphie had just told him the sun came up in the west.

"Your," said the small young man again. He stopped. He tried once more. "Your *fiancée*?"

"Since I was seven," said Alphie quickly. "Well. Since I was seven and she was four. Our parents arranged it. It's one of those things. You arrange it and then you sort of—" he made a vague gesture, as if smoothing a tablecloth "—stop thinking about it. For a bit. I meant to mention it."

"You *meant* to mention it."

"I did. Several times. It never came up."

"It never came *up*?" The small young man's voice went up with it. It was a very well-bred voice, quite a Hill voice, which Ursula had not expected from the rest of him; and then, as he went on, something rougher and broader crept in at the edges, as if it had been waiting behind the door. "Rolph, we've shared a room for *two years*. You told me about the dog. You told me about the dog that's afraid of the *stairs*."

"Admiral," said Alphie, helplessly.

Ursula found that she wanted, very much, to laugh, and didn't, because she had been taught not to, and because nobody had yet told her who this was.

She knew who he was meant to be. Alphie had written to her about him, of course, in the dutiful letters they exchanged once a month, letters that were always very kind and always about something else; and the something else, for the last year, had been almost entirely this. *My friend Tor. The scholar. You'll like him.* She had built a picture of him from the letters, the way you do. She'd built someone taller.

He was not tall. He came up, she thought, perhaps to her shoulder, and she had to look down at him to meet his eyes, which she was used to doing with almost everyone except Alphie and his father. He was in a gray summer coat that had been Alphie's, she was almost sure; she had a dim memory of it at a garden party when Alphie was fourteen; with the cuffs turned back to show the green. The stock at his throat was beautifully tied. The rest of him looked as if it had been up all night.

And the room was cool.

She'd felt it on the threshold, and she felt it still, all round her: not the cool of a shuttered room, which was only a kind of stale, dim warm, but real cool, like the cool under a bridge, or in the vaults beneath the river temple. Her walking dress had stuck to her back in the carriage all the way from the Thorgood house. Now it didn't. There was a copper plate lying on the table in the middle of a green cloth, like a dish, and stacks of smaller ones beside it, and papers covered in drawings of eggs.

She looked at the plate. Then, politely, at him.

He wasn't looking at her face.

He was looking at her brooch.

She'd pinned it on that morning at the neck of her walking dress, where the high collar closed: a dragonfly, as long as her thumb, with a body of some pale, smooth metal that wasn't silver and wasn't gold, and four wings set with stones, blue and white, very small, that caught the light and seemed to hold it a moment before they gave it back. It was the prettiest thing she owned. It was certainly the most expensive. She'd worn it today because it was summer, and the dragonfly was a summer thing, and because—

He was still looking at it. He'd taken half a step closer without, she thought, noticing. His head had gone a little on one side.

"I'm sorry," said Ursula, coolly, the way her mother would have said it. "Is something wrong?"

"Oh!" He went pink and stepped back. "No. Sorry. I'm so sorry, my lady, that was rude." He put his hands behind his back, like a boy told not to touch. And then, almost at once, as if he couldn't help it: "What *is* that?"

"It's a brooch."

"Yes. Yes, I can see that. But what *is* it?"

Ursula looked at Alphie. Alphie looked at his friend, with the expression of a man watching a cart begin to roll downhill.

"It's a dragonfly," said Ursula. "It's only jewelry."

"It isn't, though," said the scholar.

He said it quite simply. Not rudely. As if she'd said it was raining, and he'd glanced out of the window and found it wasn't. He was looking at the brooch again, and his eyes had gone narrow and intent and very, very interested, and she saw in them, quite suddenly, why Alphie had written about nothing else for a year.

"It isn't magic," he said. "That's the thing. That's what's odd. I'd know if it was magic; there'd be a pattern, there's always a pattern, you can feel the shape of it even if you can't see it. And there isn't one. There's nothing. There's no field in it at all." He shook his head slowly. "But it's *doing* something. It's— it's humming. Not so you'd hear. Under. Like—" He searched for it. "You know before a storm? When the air goes thick, and your hair stands up, and you can feel it coming over the hills before there's a single cloud? It's like that. If you squint. A very small storm. Sitting on your collar."

Ursula's hand had gone to the brooch. She hadn't told it to.

"It was a present," she said.

"Who from?"

"Tor," said Alphie.

"Sorry. Sorry, I know, it's not my business. Only I've never felt anything like it. Not once. Not in anything. And I don't know how you'd *make* it, if it isn't magic, I don't know what you'd make it *out* of—"

"Count Ward," said Ursula.

She heard herself say it, and heard how it came out, too quick and too high, like a girl caught in the pantry, and was furious, because there was nothing to be caught *at*. There was nothing. She made herself breathe. She made herself look at Alphie, not at the scholar, and say it properly.

"Count Ward gave it to me. At my name day, in the spring. He's an old friend of my father's, he's always been very kind. It came in a little box with a note, and the note said—" She stopped. She had been going to say *the note said to wear it the first time I saw you this summer*, because it had, in a very pretty hand, *it would please an old man to think of it at court*; and some cold small part of her, the part her mother had spent nineteen years training, closed its hand over the words before they got out. "It said many happy returns." She lifted her chin. "Alphie. I've kept every term. Every single one, since I was four. You know I have. I've never so much as—"

"Urs," said Alphie, gently. "Nobody said—"

"He's *saying*—"

"I'm not!" The scholar looked appalled. "I'm not saying anything. I only meant it's *interesting*. It's the most interesting thing I've seen since—" He gestured at the stacks of copper. "Since ever, really. Look, can I— would you let me just— I wouldn't touch it, I'd only want to hold my hand near—"

"That," said a voice from the door, "is quite enough, I think."

* * *

Rolph turned round so fast he nearly knocked the flowers off their stand.

"*Father!*"

Beside him, all in one movement, Ursula sank: down and down, her skirt spreading, her head bowed, her long back perfectly straight, into the deep court curtsey that Tor had once seen a picture of in a book and had assumed nobody really did.

"Your Majesty," said Ursula, to the carpet.

The man in the doorway was enormous.

He filled it. He filled it the way a bull fills a gate, side to side, with a little room left over at the top only because the door had been built for him. He was bigger than Rolph, bigger than Tovey, bigger than anyone Tor had ever stood in a room with; a great wide man in a plain dark coat, with a broad, heavy, clever face and a short beard going gray at the chin, and a huge head of gray-brown hair, and Rolph's eyes. Exactly Rolph's eyes. Behind him, smaller, in the dark dress from last night, with her rings on, stood Rolph's mother.

*Father.*

*Your Majesty.*

Tor stood in the middle of the cool room in his borrowed coat, and the two words went into him one after the other like nails, and something that had been sitting crooked in his head since yesterday afternoon, since the gate, since the purple and the black, since *young sir* and the list and the small house and the dome over the wall and the first bite of soup and *we supply the king with something, possibly cloth*, slid, quite smoothly, all at once, into place.

He heard it go. It made almost no sound at all.

Rolph wasn't a merchant's son.

Rolph was Prince Alphonse.

And that was the queen in the doorway. Queen Constance. *Connie.* Who'd asked him about his mother's sorrel soup. Who'd let him talk about cellars for half an hour over the fish. And Karina, who'd walked in on him with his shirt off, was a *princess*. And Varley. And he'd given a princess four plates, out of his trunk, in sacking, like a man selling apples at a fair, and told her where to put the nails.

And that meant that the man in the doorway, the enormous man with Rolph's eyes, was—

Tor was very tired. He had been awake for a day and a half and spent a good part of it in a trance, and he'd just felt a storm on a girl's collar, and his mouth was a long way ahead of the rest of him.

"Oh," he said. "If Rolph's mother is *Connie*—" He looked at the king. "Then you must be— *Rick*."

The silence was perfect.

Somewhere far off in the house a door shut. Ursula, still down in her curtsey, made a very small sound into the carpet. Rolph closed his eyes.

The king winced.

It was a small wince, and a pained one, and it went across that broad heavy face and was gone; but Tor saw it, and he would remember it, afterward, as the moment he found out that kings could wince.

"Not Rick," said the king. His voice was deep and slow and surprisingly mild, like a big bell rung softly. "Please. Not Rick. My brother called me Rick. *Icky Ricky*, when we were boys, for the whole of one summer, because I'd fallen in the carp pond, and it was a long summer." He sighed. "Rich, if you must. Richard, if you'd rather. Not Rick."

"Rich," said Tor.

"Thank you."

"Sir," said Tor. "Your. Majesty. Sir."

And then the rest of him caught up with his mouth, and he understood that he was standing in a borrowed coat in a borrowed room in the *palace*, the actual palace, with the dome, and that he had just told the King of Noram his own name, and then argued about it, and his knees went.

He didn't fall. He sat. There happened to be a chair behind him, one of the chairs whose only purpose in life was to be sat in, and it caught him, and he sat in it, with his hands on his knees, and looked at the carpet, and breathed.

"Sorry," he said, to the carpet. "Sorry. I think I'll just— sit. For a moment. If that's allowed. I don't know if it's allowed. You're probably not allowed to sit."

"Sit," said the king. "You look as if you'd better."

Nobody else sat. Rolph's mother came quietly in past her husband and stood by the window, with her hands folded, watching Tor with an expression he couldn't read and didn't try to. Rolph was looking at him with an expression he could read perfectly well, and it said *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm so sorry*, and under it, faintly, something that looked like the beginning of a laugh. Ursula had come up out of her curtsey and was standing very straight, a little apart from all of them, with her hand at her collar.

The king hadn't looked at Tor for long. He'd looked at Ursula.

"Lady Ursula," he said. The mildness was still there, but something had come in under it, the way the cold comes up under a frozen pond. "I didn't mean to listen at the door. I'm afraid I did. The scholar was saying something about your brooch."

"It's nothing, Your Majesty. It's only—"

"Count Ward gave it to you."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"When?"

"In the spring, Your Majesty. At my name day. The fourteenth of—"

"How often have you worn it since?"

Ursula's chin went up. "Often, Your Majesty. It's very pretty."

"Here?" said the king. "To this house? To court? To dinner at my table, last month, when the Gallaian envoy was here?"

There was a pause.

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"And Count Ward," said the king, very gently now, too gently, "is what to you, my dear? Exactly?"

Tor, in his chair, looked up.

He saw it go round the room. He saw Rolph see it: Rolph's face, which had been sheepish, went still and then white, like milk when you tip the jug. He saw the queen's hands, by the window, tighten on each other. And he saw Ursula see it, last of all, because she'd been too busy holding her chin up to look where the questions were going; he saw her get there, all at once, the way you get to the bottom of the stairs in the dark when you thought there was one more step.

Her face crumpled.

She didn't make a sound at first. Her mouth went square, like a child's, and her eyes filled, and then she put both hands over her face, the long hands with the rings on, and bent forward as if somebody had hit her in the middle, and wept.

And Tor felt it.

He felt it the way he'd felt Dallow's gladness in Tovey's hall, and Karina's quick bright sums: off her, under her, through the air of the cool room like heat off a loaf. He'd expected— he didn't know what he'd expected. Fear, maybe. The thing you'd feel off someone who'd been caught. A tight, cornered, sideways thing, like a cat under a cart.

It wasn't that. It wasn't anything like that. It was something wide and flat and dreadful, like a field after a flood. It was shame, but not the shame of someone who'd done a wrong thing. The other kind. The kind you feel when you find out you've been *used*: that you were kind to someone, and trusted them, and wore their present because it was pretty, and wore it again because they'd asked, and all that time you'd been a pair of hands for them, carrying something into the one place you'd sworn on your whole life to keep safe. She hadn't known. She hadn't had the least idea. And now she knew, all of it, in one go, standing in front of the king, and she couldn't take any of it back.

*You did that,* said a small cold voice in Tor's head. *You. Just now. You couldn't leave it alone. You pointed at her collar in front of everybody and said look, a storm.*

"It isn't magic," Tor heard himself say. His voice came out strange. "Sir. Your Majesty. I want you to know that. It's not a pattern. Nobody built it. Not here. Nobody in Noram could. I don't think anyone here would know *how*." He swallowed. "So it's from somewhere else. It must be. And the only people I've ever heard of who can do things without magic that we can't do with it are—"

"The Austrans," said the king.

"Yes, sir."

The king looked at Ursula for a long moment, weeping into her hands. Then he looked at the brooch, the little dragonfly at her throat, with its four wings of blue and white stones holding the light. Tor saw his face as he looked at it, and was suddenly very glad that he was sitting down.

"Then by immediate royal decree," said King Richard, quite quietly, "the betrothal between my son Alphonse and the Lady Ursula Thorgood is dissolved. As of now. As of this room." He turned his head. "Constance. You'll see it's written."

"Father—" said Rolph.

"As of this *room*, Alphonse."

Rolph shut his mouth.

Ursula had taken her hands away from her face. She stood there with her cheeks wet and her eyes red and swollen and her long face gone gray, and didn't wipe them, and didn't speak. Tor understood, watching her, though nobody said it, though he'd grown up among people who'd never been betrothed to anyone, what the decree meant. He'd heard enough at school. A girl put aside by the crown, for *that*, for a spy's brooch, for a foreign thing carried into the king's house: nobody would marry her. Nobody would dare. She'd be the girl who'd been put aside. For the rest of her life. Whatever she'd known or not known. People would look at her, at dinners, and think *Austra*, and move their chairs.

She was nineteen, he thought, or thereabouts. Her whole life had been arranged since she was four. And he'd just knocked the bottom out of it, because he'd wanted to know what a brooch was made of.

He stood up.

He didn't mean to; it was like the slap at his chest when someone threw an apple. He was up out of the chair and across the carpet before his head had any say in it at all, and then he was standing in front of Ursula Thorgood, who was a whole head taller than he was, looking up at her wet gray face.

"I'll marry you," said Tor.

Ursula stared down at him.

"I mean it," said Tor. "I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I know it's— I know I'm not— But it's my fault. All of it. If I hadn't said anything, you'd still—" He stopped, and made himself say it properly, the way you'd want to be told. "Nobody will have you now, will they? Because of this. Because of me. So I will. If you want. I've got money now. Quite a lot, actually, it's in a box. And I can bake. I can keep a house warm in winter and cool in summer, I can do that easily, you'd never be cold. You wouldn't be left with nothing. I wouldn't let you be." He thought, and added, honestly, "I don't think I'd be much trouble. I'm usually working."

There was a silence in the cool room.

Then Ursula Thorgood made a sound that was half a laugh and half a sob and wholly something else, and bent down, a long way down, and put her arms round him, and held on.

Tor stood very still inside the hug, with his arms out at his sides, because he didn't know where they were meant to go, and his face pressed into a gray-blue shoulder that smelled of lavender and carriage, and the dragonfly brooch an inch from his ear, humming its small storm. After a moment, carefully, he patted her on the back.

"There," he said. "There."

Over her shoulder he could see the king.

The king was looking at him. It was a long, heavy, considering look, and it went on for some time, and something in that broad face had changed. Tor couldn't have said what. Not softer, exactly. More as if a man who'd been looking at a field had noticed for the first time that there was a house in it.

"That's a very fine offer," said the king, slowly. "A very fine offer, young man. And very nobly made." He paused. "But you're not of the peerage, are you? Forgive me. I'm only saying what the Lady Ursula's father will say, and her mother, and every house in this city. You're a—" He hesitated, and frowned a little, and looked at Tor more closely, as if Tor were print that had gone slightly out of focus. "I'm sorry. Who *are* you? I ought to know. I'm sure I ought to know." He shook his great head, slowly, like a bull bothered by a fly. "Forgive me. I have the noble condition. All of us have, the old lines. It was done to us a long time ago: we don't see people properly who are— who are below a certain size. Faces slide. Even faces we've been told about. It's nothing personal." He looked again, harder, and his face cleared a little. "There. I have you now."

"I didn't know that," said Tor, from somewhere in Ursula's shoulder. "That's interesting. That's *very* interesting, actually, I wonder how they— " He stopped himself. Ursula had let go of him, and stepped back, and was wiping her face with the heel of her hand, and he remembered what they were talking about. "But it doesn't matter. I'm not anybody, sir. Your Majesty. Rich. I'm not anybody special at all. That's rather the point. If it's me, nobody can say she married up, can they? Or that anybody bought her. They'll only say she married a baker. And there's nothing wrong with bakers."

The king opened his mouth.

By the window, the queen began, very gently, to laugh.

It wasn't an unkind laugh. Tor knew unkind laughs; he'd grown up on the receiving end of a few, and had one screamed at him across a dance floor in his first year. This wasn't that. It was warm, and low, and a little helpless, the laugh of a woman who has been watching something wonderful happen in front of her and can't keep it in any longer.

Everyone turned to look at her.

Queen Constance unfolded her hands. She came away from the window, across the carpet, in her dark dress with the rings on, and put one hand on her enormous husband's arm, and looked down at Tor with her eyes shining.

"Dear," said the queen. "This is Torrance Baker."
