The cool was still in the room when Tor got back to it, waiting for him like a dog by the door.

He stood in it for a moment with his eyes shut. Then he took off Rolph's gray coat, very carefully, and hung it on the back of a chair, and took off the waistcoat with the little leaves, and loosened the stock that Burks had tied, and sat down at the end of the great table in his shirtsleeves, where the green cloth was, and the plate, and the pages of eggs from the night before.

There were twelve copper squares left in the bottom of his trunk. He took out ten.

"Dinner," he said to them. "There'll be people carrying things. In and out. All night. In those coats." He'd seen the coats at lunch: the Debri servants in good dark wool with brass buttons, sweating quietly into their collars. He'd seen the palace servants too, last night, all eight of them along the wall of the small dining room, standing perfectly still in the heat, and nobody had seemed to think it was anything worth looking at. "You won't be enough," he told the squares. "But you'll be ten."

It was a short one. It always was, now: he knew the shape of it as well as he knew the shape of a loaf. He laid his hands flat on the cloth on either side of the first square and let his breathing go down, and the room went away, and the bells went by him without his counting them, two or perhaps three; and when he came back the light at the window had gone from gold to amber, and there were ten small squares in a row in front of him, with a fine dark line on each going round like an egg and down like a hook, and his neck had set like a pot left to dry.

"There," said Tor, hoarsely, and put his head down on his arms beside them, just for a moment.

* * *

Quavel had been told to go up and see whether the scholar needed anything before dinner. He had not been told that Count Lairdgren would already be in the corridor when he got there, with a suit of black over one arm and a small flat box of dark leather in his other hand, as though he'd been waiting for Quavel to come and hold the door.

Quavel held the door.

The scholar was asleep at the table again. He was asleep with his cheek on his folded arms and his hair standing straight up at the back, beside a row of copper squares, and when the Count said "Sir," in that quiet, level, borrowed servant's voice, he came up out of it so fast he nearly took the cloth with him.

"Burks!" He looked absurdly pleased. Then he looked down at his shirtsleeves, and at the gray coat on the chair, and at the window, and his face fell. "Oh, no. Is it time? It's not time. I haven't— I look *terrible*, don't I. I've been asleep on a table."

"You look very well, sir."

"You'd say that anyway," said the scholar, with perfect good humor. "You'd say that if I'd been in the pond."

The Count considered this. "Oh, no, sir," he said. "If you looked terrible, I would certainly tell you. How else would we fix it?"

The scholar blinked at him. Then, slowly, he smiled, as if that were the most reasonable thing anyone had said to him all day.

"All right," he said. "Then what are we fixing?"

They fixed the hair first, with water and a comb, which helped a little and then stopped helping. Then the Count laid the black on the bed and stood the boy in front of it, and Quavel, who had stayed by the door because nobody had told him to go, watched the gray summer coat come off and the black go on.

It was very plain. A black coat, close-cut and high in the collar, with no braid on it anywhere; black waistcoat; black trousers, which, Quavel noted, had been taken up at the hem by somebody that very afternoon, in stitches so small you'd have needed a glass to find them. A fresh white shirt. A fresh white stock, which the Count tied, without hurrying, his small neat hands going round and through and under. And then he opened the flat leather box, and took out what was in it, and pinned it on the left breast of the black coat, over the boy's heart.

Quavel had seen that badge on four coats in twenty years. He had never seen it pinned.

It was a falcon. A silver falcon, the size of a man's palm, with its wings spread out wide from its shoulders and its head turned to one side, and in its talons, held close to its body, a small shield. The silver lay on a ground of enamel the deep purple of a plum. It caught the last of the amber light from the window and held it.

The scholar looked down at it. He touched one silver wing with one finger, very lightly, as if it might be hot.

"Burks," he said. "That's the king's."

"It is, sir."

"I can't wear the king's."

"You can, sir, if he sends it." The Count straightened the pin by a hair. "It's his favor. When a man wears the crest on his breast, at the king's table, in the king's colors, it tells every guest that the king holds him in favor. That he stands near." He stepped back and looked at it. "Which means, sir, that tonight you must be careful."

"*More* careful?"

"Differently careful. Anything you promise, at that table, wearing this, the king has promised. Any rudeness you offer anyone, the king has offered." The Count's voice didn't change at all. "So you'll make no promises tonight, sir. None. Not even small ones. And you'll be rude to nobody, however much they deserve it."

The scholar looked at the falcon for a long moment.

"I'm never rude to anybody," he said, a little uncertainly.

"No, sir," said the Count. "I'd noticed that."

He turned the boy by the shoulders toward the long glass. Quavel looked too; he couldn't help it. A small young man in plain black, with a white stock and his hair already going back to its own opinions, and on his breast, catching the light, the king's falcon in silver on purple. He looked, Quavel thought, like the guard. Like a very small and very young officer of the royal guard who'd lost his rod and found a crest instead. Behind his shoulder, a little taller, the neat dark figure of the Count looked into the glass too, and for the second time that day Quavel's eye caught on something between the two faces and couldn't let it go.

"Oh," said the scholar, to the glass, quietly.

Then he went and fetched things.

Quavel watched him do it, the way you'd watch a man pack for a long journey. A cloth bag, which clinked: ten small copper squares, each on a length of the gray string, doubled this time and knotted twice, as if somebody had told him it would rot. A copper plate the size of a book, flat, with a downward hook etched on it, which he wrapped in a napkin and tucked under his arm like a ledger. And last, from the inside of his shirt, a different plate on a cord: he took it out and looked at it and put it back, and then took it out again and looked at the back of it, and Quavel saw that there were four small marks scratched into the corner of the copper, one after another, like a tally.

"Fourth one," said the scholar, to nobody, putting it back. "The other three had things wrong with them."

"What sort of things, sir?" said the Count.

"Oh, all sorts." He patted his chest, over the plate, twice, the way a man pats a pocket to be sure his purse is still there. "This one's better. I think. I'll know if somebody hits me."

"Let us hope," said the Count, "that nobody does."

* * *

Rolph caught him at the head of the main stair.

He'd meant to. He'd been waiting at the turn of the corridor for a quarter of an hour in his own black and his own crest, the one he'd worn to dinners since he was nine, rehearsing; and then Tor came round the corner with a bag that clinked and a parcel under his arm and the falcon on his coat, and Rolph forgot every word of it.

"*Father* sent you that?"

"Burks pinned it on." Tor looked down at himself. "Is it wrong? Is it on upside down? I don't know which way a falcon goes."

"It's not upside down." Rolph took a breath. It had come out of the leather box on his father's desk, the one that stayed locked; he'd have bet his lockbox on it. "It's exactly right. Come here a moment. Walk with me. Slowly. There are things you need to know before we go in, and I've got about two corridors to tell you."

They walked. The palace at this hour was all lamps and long shadows and servants going past fast and silent with trays and folded cloths, and every one of them, Rolph saw, looked at Tor's breast and then looked again.

"Food first," said Rolph. "You remember last night. The soup."

Tor went pink to the stock. "I ate first."

"You ate first. You weren't supposed to. At a table like ours the host eats first. Always. Before anyone." He kept his voice low. "It's because of poison. The host takes the first bite, and everyone watches the host, and then they know it's safe."

"Oh, no." Tor stopped walking. "Oh, *no*. I— your *mother*—"

"It's all right. Walk. It's all right." Rolph steered him on. "Mother decided you'd done it on purpose. That you'd gone first to show there wasn't anything in it. Like a taster. She told me so afterward. She thought it was rather gallant." He grinned, briefly. "So you're forgiven. But tonight you don't. Tonight you watch Father, or Mother, and when they've eaten, you eat. And you make sure you've eaten before Ward does."

"Before Ward."

"There's an order to it. The host first. And whoever's most likely to be poisoned, the person the table trusts least, the person who might have *done* something, sits at the far end and eats last. Once everyone else has eaten and lived, so nobody can say afterwards that their food was got at." Rolph paused. "It's a sort of guarantee. Both ways."

Tor walked for a few steps in silence.

"That doesn't make any sense," he said.

"It doesn't have to. It's tradition."

"But the person who might have done something could just— if they were the one with the poison, they'd—"

"Tor," said Rolph. "Tradition."

"Sorry." Tor frowned. "So who's last?"

"Ward," said Rolph. "And Ursula."

Tor stopped again.

"*Ursula*? But she didn't do anything. You said so. Your father said so. She didn't even know."

"I know." Rolph took him by the elbow and walked him on, gently, past a footman who had flattened himself against a tapestry to let them by. "We know. The table won't. All the table will know is that she's the one who walked into my father's house wearing the thing. That's how it looks. And how it looks is half of what happens tonight." He let go of the elbow. "So she'll eat last, and she'll smile, and she won't be poisoned, and everyone will see her not be poisoned. It's the best thing we can do for her."

Tor didn't say anything. His hand had gone flat to his chest, over the fourth shield, and stayed there.

"One more thing," said Rolph. "And this one's serious."

"The other ones weren't?"

"Don't challenge Ward to a duel."

Tor turned and stared at him.

"Rolph," he said. "I'm a *baker's son*. He's a *count*. If I stood up at the king's table and challenged a count to a duel, they'd— even if I won, *especially* if I won, they'd put me to death. Wouldn't they? You told me. You told me in the spring, about Tovey. That's the law."

"It is."

"Then why on earth would I—"

"Because you'd win," said Rolph. "Because you'd stand there in that shield and he'd swing at you for twenty minutes, like Kolb, and nothing would happen, and everyone would know it. He'd never accept, Tor. He'd find some way out. He'd plead his age or his honor or the king's peace. Because you're the one man in that room he can't hurt." He shrugged. "And the next morning the whole city would be telling the story of how Count Ward backed down from a baker's boy. Which would feel very good for about a day. And then it would be a war."

They had reached the top of the last stair. Below them, through an arch, there was light and the smell of beeswax and the sound of a great many people moving quietly.

"All right," said Tor, slowly. "I promise. No duels." He thought about it. "I'll only challenge people I could actually beat. Children. Babies, mostly." He considered further. "Small babies."

Rolph laughed. It came out of him before he could stop it, loud, in the hush of the stair, and a footman below looked up. And then it went away again, and he stood on the top step and looked down through the arch at the light, and didn't feel like laughing at all.

"I'll try to do the same," he said. "No promises. It might not be easy."

* * *

The formal dining room was the size of the Lairdgren chapel, and his mother was already in it.

She stood near the head of a table that could have seated forty and was laid for ten, at the near end, under a ceiling of painted sky and painted birds and painted gods looking down over the edges of painted clouds, one of whom had six fingers on his left hand. The windows were open on the fig walk. It was still very hot. His mother was in midnight blue, with the pearls, and the copper square on its gray string tucked out of sight under the lace at her throat, and Quavel stood at her shoulder in his best black with his face composed into its usual nothing.

She turned as they came in, and her face lit.

"Alphonse," she said, and came and put her hands on his shoulders and kissed the air beside his cheek, one side and then the other, the way she had done every evening of his life. And then she turned, without a pause, and put her hands on Tor's shoulders, and did exactly the same.

Tor went scarlet. He went scarlet from the white stock to the roots of his hair and stood absolutely still with his arms at his sides, the way a boy stands when a large and friendly dog has put its paws on him and he's not sure yet what it wants.

"Good evening, ma'am," he said, to her collarbone.

"Good evening, Tor. Don't you look splendid." His mother's eyes went to the falcon, rested there, and came up again, and she said nothing about it at all. "Quavel," she said, over her shoulder, "it's going to be an oven in here by the second course. Would you have a couple of the plates brought down? From the morning room, and mine?"

"Oh!" said Tor. "No, you don't need to. I've got one." He pulled the parcel out from under his arm and unwrapped the napkin from it, a little clumsily, and held out the copper. "I brought it. For in here. It'll do. I promise it'll do, even in a room this size. It'll do the whole thing, it's only the air." He looked round for somewhere to put it. "It needs to be in the middle, really. Low down. Could it go on the— under the table? Would that be all right?"

His mother looked at the plate, and at him, and at Quavel. Quavel took the plate in both hands, as if it were the sacrament, and went and laid it on the floor under the very center of the table, where the cloth hung down and hid it, and stood up again.

Tor knelt, without asking, and put his hand under the cloth, flat on the copper, and his lips moved. Then he got up, brushing off his knees.

The air in the dining room began, very gently, to change.

"But that's not really what I'm worried about," said Tor, before anyone could thank him. He was looking past them, at the far doors, where the servants came and went: three of them coming in now with stacks of plates, two going out with a basin, one by the sideboard with a cloth over his arm, every one of them in black wool to the throat, every one of them shining at the temples. "It's them. They have to go in and out. Through the passages. Through the kitchens. All night, in those coats. The plate can't do the passages." He held up the clinking bag. "So I made these. This afternoon. Ten. For them." He held it out to Quavel. "Would you give them out? Please? To whoever's working. They just go round the neck, and you put your hand on it, flat, and—"

"Sir," said Quavel. He took the bag. He looked down into it, and something went across his face that Rolph had not seen there in his lifetime, and was folded away again at once. "Sir. Thank you, sir."

He began to give them out. He went along the line of servants by the wall, and down to the sideboard, and out through the far doors, where there were more; and they came back in one at a time, the men and women of the household in their black, with a loop of gray string at their collars and a strange still look on their faces, as if they were listening to something a long way off. Rolph watched Tor count them. He watched Tor's lips move. He watched Tor get to ten, and see that there were more servants still coming in through the far doors, two and then three and then another two, and keep counting, and get to fifteen.

Tor's hand went to his own collar.

He had the copper square off over his head before Rolph understood what he was doing. He took two steps and held it out to the nearest woman without one, a kitchen girl with a tray of glasses, who looked at it, and at him, and at the falcon on his breast, and nearly dropped the tray.

"Please," said Tor. "Go on. It's warm in the kitchens."

She took it.

Rolph laughed. It was mostly breath. He put his hand inside his own collar and pulled out his own square on its string, the one Tor had given him that morning in the cool guest room, which had kept him comfortable through the whole long hot afternoon at the Debri house and in the carriage, and held it out to Quavel.

"Here," he said. "For whoever's next."

And his mother, at the head of the table, in her midnight blue and her pearls, looked at her son, and at Tor, and at the two remaining servants without anything at their collars, a young footman by the door and an older man with the wine; and Rolph saw her understand that she had been left no choice in the matter whatsoever, and saw her be amused by it, and put her hand to the lace at her throat.

"Quavel," said Queen Constance. "One more."

Quavel took it from her with the face of a man receiving a relic, and gave it to the footman.

That left one. The old man with the wine stood by the sideboard in his black coat with his collar bare, and Rolph saw Tor look at him, and look at the empty bag, and look at his own empty hands, and go quiet and polite and miserable all at once.

"I'm sorry," said Tor to the old man, across the room. "I'll make more. Tomorrow."

The old man bowed, and smiled, and said "Sir," and went on decanting.

Tor turned round, looking for somewhere to put himself. His eyes went along the table, to the chairs.

"Not yet," said Rolph. "Nobody sits till everyone's here."

"Oh," said Tor. "Right."

He stood. He stood by the end of the table with his hands clasped behind his back and his chin up, very straight, the falcon on his breast catching the candles, in a room that was growing, minute by minute, around all of them, cool.

* * *

Trice came down the last stair at the precise moment she had calculated, which was late enough that her aunt would already be in the room and early enough that the Thorgoods wouldn't be, and stopped in the arch to see who was where.

Her aunt, by the table. Quavel. Alphonse in black with his crest, looking as he always looked before a difficult dinner, like a large dog trying to remember which of its tricks was allowed. And beside him, very straight, hands behind his back—

She looked twice.

It was Tor. Of course it was Tor; nobody else at court was that size. But they'd put him in black, plain black, beautifully cut, and somebody had tied his stock, and on his left breast, over his heart, where she'd expected nothing at all, there was the falcon. Silver on purple. *Her uncle's* falcon. She'd seen four men wear it in her life, and two of them had been generals.

She filed it. She filed it very carefully, in a column of its own. Then she came across the room.

She'd taken some trouble. Light violet, which her mother said made her look like a crocus and which she wore anyway; her hair up, for once not pinned close but loose enough that a few ringlets had been allowed to fall round her face, which had taken her maid three quarters of an hour and a good deal of language. She saw Alphonse see it. She saw Quavel see it. She saw Tor turn, and look at her, and go very still, with his mouth a little open, and then shut his mouth and look at the floor; and she put that in the account as well, in its own small column, and didn't look at it yet.

"Aunt Constance," she said, and was kissed. "Alphie." And then, to Tor, "Master Baker," and she smiled at him, and watched him go pink. She looked from him to her cousin. "Well? Is the cat out of the bag?"

"My dear," said her aunt, "it's very hard to hide a whole palace."

And then, while the servants came and went behind them with the new copper hidden at their collars and the cool spread out round the table like water filling a basin, her aunt told her.

All of it. Trice had had most of it already, in the letter that had come for her at the Debri house that afternoon in her aunt's own hand, five lines, *come to dinner, Thorgoods and Ward, wear something*. But the letter hadn't had the brooch, or the dragonfly, or what Tor had felt on Ursula's collar, or the king in the doorway, or *Rick*. Her aunt told it well. She told it the way she told everything, lightly, with the weight in the pauses. Trice listened to it with her hands folded and her face composed. A spy's brooch in the palace. A broken betrothal. Ward, possibly a traitor, possibly a fool. She'd been raised for exactly this kind of dinner, and she could feel herself already arranging the table in her head: where Ward would sit, where the Thorgoods, who'd watch whom.

She was aware of Tor watching her face as she listened, with a sort of anxious puzzlement, as if he'd expected her to be more upset and couldn't work out why she wasn't.

"—and then," said her aunt, "Tor offered to marry her."

Trice's eyes flew open.

She couldn't help it. It was the only thing in the whole story that she hadn't, somewhere at the back of her mind, already half expected. She turned and looked at Tor. Tor looked at the floor.

"He what?"

"It was rather wonderful, really." Her aunt's voice had dropped, warm and amused. "He didn't know who she was. Not past her being engaged to Alphonse. He didn't know who *Alphonse* was, Tricia. He simply saw that the girl would be ruined, and that it was his doing, and offered himself. On the spot. Without a thought for what he'd get, or what it would cost him." She shook her head slowly. "Of course it can't be allowed. Not as things are. Rank, and the law, and three steps, and so on. But the offer *was made*, in front of the king. Nobody who hears of it will be able to say this house did less than its best for that girl." She tilted her head, and something came into her voice, light, but not only light. "Unless, of course, we found him something to stand on. A title. I'm almost sure we've a barony or two lying about, gathering dust."

Trice looked at her aunt. Her aunt looked back, perfectly bland.

Trice glanced over her shoulder at the room. Quavel had his back to them. The servants were busy. Nobody was looking. So she turned back to the Queen of Noram and stuck out her tongue at her.

"Aunt Constance," she said. "That's cruel. You know it's cruel. Look at him."

Tor had gone, if anything, redder.

"Is it?" said Alphonse.

Trice turned. Her cousin wasn't smiling. He stood with his hands behind his back, mirroring Tor without seeming to know it, and he looked tired, and grave, and much older than he had looked at Lairdgren.

"You think she's teasing," said Alphonse. "She might be. But by the end of tonight, Trice, it might be the only thing we've got that keeps County Ward from marching on somebody." He paused, and something in his face moved. "And he'd make a very good baron. Wouldn't he?"

Trice stood quite still.

She thought about it. She thought about it properly, for perhaps the space of a breath, the way she thought about everything: a column for, a column against, the cost, the return. And then she found she wasn't thinking about columns at all. She was thinking about a small boy in a courtyard in the dark, with a sack of copper on his shoulder and a dead leg, saying *please* to it. And about fifty drying fields in a night. And about the twelfth spoon of broth.

She took a breath. A long one. Longer than she meant.

She didn't smile. She looked at Tor, who was looking at the falcon on his own coat as though he hoped it might fly off with him, and then back at her cousin.

"Yes," said Trice quietly. "I think he would."

* * *

The Thorgoods came in together, and Trice watched Tor see Ursula.

Ursula came in first, a little ahead of her parents, which was not the order but nobody was going to say so tonight. She was in gray, which was clever, and very straight, and she'd been crying at some point in the afternoon and had repaired it so well that only another woman would have known. She curtsied to the queen, deeply. Then she turned, and saw Tor, and something happened to her face, and she crossed the room to him directly, skirt and all, and took both his hands.

"Mother," she said. "Father. This is Master Wizard Torrance Baker."

Tor looked as if he'd been hit with a plank.

"*Master*—" he began.

"*Wizard*," said Ursula firmly. "You make things nobody's made. That's what it means. Don't argue with me tonight, Tor, I haven't the strength." She turned to her parents. "This is him."

Countess Marigold Thorgood was a soft, round, comfortable woman with Ursula's height and none of her edges, and she came forward with both hands out and tears standing in her eyes and said, "Oh, my dear boy," and took Tor's hands out of her daughter's into her own, and pressed them. Count Horace Thorgood came behind her more slowly. He was huge, the way the old counts were huge, a great grizzled mountain of a man with a beard like a hedge, and he looked down at Tor from a very long way up, and Trice watched his face.

It was quite a study. The count looked at Tor's face, and then at the falcon on his breast, and then at the top of his head, which came, roughly, to the count's lowest waistcoat button. And Trice saw him reach, quite clearly, the only conclusion his eyes would let him: a boy. A noble boy; who else would be wearing the crest? A small one; so a young one. Twelve, perhaps. Thirteen. Somebody's son, dressed up and brought down to dinner. And then she saw him hear his daughter again, *Master Wizard*, and try to put the two things together, and fail, and try again, with the slow, painstaking courtesy of a man trying to fit a key into a lock in the dark.

"Master Baker," said the count at last, rather carefully, as if the words might break. "We— my wife and I— we owe you a great deal. What you said for our girl. In front of the king." He cleared his throat. "Very great deal."

"It wasn't anything, sir," said Tor. "I'm sorry it couldn't be."

The count's eyebrows went up into his hairline, and stayed there.

Alphonse stepped in, smoothly, before anyone could decide what that meant.

"Tor, may I. Count Horace Thorgood, and the Countess Marigold. Ursula's father and mother." He smiled at the Thorgoods. "You both know of Tor already, it seems. Tor and Patricia are going to help us this evening, if they can."

"Patricia," said the count, and bowed to Trice, and Trice curtsied, and filed the fact that he still couldn't stop looking at the top of Tor's head.

* * *

The king came in through the far doors without being announced, because it was his house; and everyone in the room but his wife went down.

Trice went down with the rest, into the curtsy she'd been doing since she was three, and from down there, under her lashes, she watched Tor do it. He did it well. He'd been practicing, she thought; there was a stiffness in it, but it was the right bow, from the shoulders, and his hand had gone to his breast, over the falcon, the way the officers did it. She wondered who'd shown him that. She thought she could guess.

"Up, up," said her uncle. He came down the room, huge and red-haired and tired, in black with no crest at all, because he didn't need one. "Horace. Marigold. Ursula, my dear." He took the girl's hand, briefly, and Trice saw Ursula's chin go up and stay there. "Thank you for coming. All of you." He looked round the table. "I'll be honest with you. I want this done quickly tonight. I want it done well, and fairly, and quickly. Because I've a letter on my desk from Ford County, and it isn't good."

"The drought," said her aunt quietly.

"The drought." He rubbed his face. "It's come round again. You know Ford, Horace. West of here, out on the coast, all the way out to the Pacifico; dry as a biscuit and warm all year. All the water in the world sitting right next to it, and not a drop you can put on a field, because it's salt. Every decade or so the rains don't come, and the wells go down, and then the whole county's looking at us." He turned, and seemed to notice Tor for the first time, and something in his face eased a little. "You'll not know it, Master Baker. The best land's all inland, hundreds of miles east. Your end. We grow it there and we bring it here, and from here it goes everywhere else. But when Ford's dry, we have to send food *west*. The wrong way. Uphill, against everything. Cart after cart after cart, along roads that were never built for it. It takes a year to plan and kills half the oxen in three counties." He sighed. "I'd rather have been thinking about that all afternoon, frankly, than about brooches."

Trice heard Tor make a small sound.

She glanced at him. He was standing exactly as he'd been standing, hands behind his back, chin up, the falcon on his breast. But his eyes had gone. They'd gone past the king, past the painted sky, past the wall, out somewhere west, and his lips had begun to move.

"Carts," said Tor, under his breath. "You'd want a lot of them. A *lot*. And they'd have to go all the way. Not just— and you'd want them to go together, in a line, like the plates, so one man could—"

Trice reached out, without looking, and pinched the back of his hand. Hard.

He jumped. "*Ow*."

"Not now," said Trice, very low, still looking at the king, still smiling. "Later. Tomorrow. You can have Ford tomorrow. Tonight you need to be *here*."

He looked at her, wounded, rubbing his hand. Then he looked at the falcon on his coat, and at the door, and at the empty chair at the far end of the table, and something in his face came back into the room.

"Sorry," he said.

"Don't be. Just stay."

He stayed.

* * *

And then Count Ward came in.

He came in with his wife on his arm, through the main doors, under the arch, as if he'd been invited to a ball. He didn't hesitate on the threshold. He didn't look round the room to see who was there, or where he was meant to stand, or what his welcome was going to be. He simply walked in, at his ease, smiling, nodding to the Thorgoods as if they'd met in the street, and Trice thought: *bold*. Very bold. Almost a declaration. *We have nothing to fear here; we are not the kind of people anything happens to.*

She didn't think much of Count Ward. She never had. She'd sat across tables from him since she was twelve, and in all that time she'd never once heard him say anything that he hadn't heard somebody else say first. He was a big, handsome, bluff, expensive man, nearly as tall as her uncle, broad as a door, in a coat of dark red that was very nearly too much, with a laugh you could hear across a hall. If he'd planned to send an Austran toy into the palace on a girl's collar, she'd eat her slippers.

Which meant that somebody else had planned it. And Trice's eyes, which knew their business better than she did, had already left the count, and gone to the woman on his arm.

She was much shorter than her husband. Most people were. She was young; much younger than Ward, close to Alphonse's age. She was slim and very carefully dressed, in deep green, in a way that didn't look careful at all, and she walked into the king's dining room on her husband's arm with her chin at precisely the right height and her eyes moving, quickly and lightly, round the room; and Trice watched those eyes go to the queen, and to the king, and to the Thorgoods, and to Alphonse, and to the empty chair at the far end of the table, and take the measure of every one of them, the way Trice herself would have done.

And then the eyes reached Tor. And stopped. And something happened in them that Trice couldn't read, and was gone.

Trice knew her. She'd known her at once, the way you know a face from a corridor years ago without being able to say from where. Then she could say from where. Lairdgren. Their own year, Alphonse's and Tor's; gone before the end of the first one, and nobody had quite remembered why. There'd been a story about her. There'd been a dance.

Trice turned, slowly, and looked at Tor.

He was looking at Count Ward. He'd been looking at Count Ward since the doors opened, the way he looked at anything very large, with frank interest: the size of him, the red coat, the easy confident way he moved, the way a room bent round him. He was taking it all in. Trice saw his eyes go up, and across, and down Ward's arm, idly, to the hand resting on it, and to the woman the hand belonged to.

And Trice saw his face.

She had seen Tor afraid. She'd seen him with a knife at his throat, and in a ring of fire, and walking into a warded room to be tried for his life. She had never seen him look like this. The color went out of him all at once, as if somebody had pulled a plug. His hands, behind his back, came apart. His chin came down. He stood in the cool king's dining room in his black coat with the king's falcon on his breast, and for a moment he wasn't a Master Wizard or a wearer of the crest or anybody's friend at all; he was a very small boy in a very large room, and someone across it had just called him a name.

"Oh," said Tor, hardly louder than a breath.

And then, so quietly that Trice was the only one close enough to hear it:

"*Maria*."
