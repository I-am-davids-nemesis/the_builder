"The man who saved Ashford," said Connie. "This spring. You had the report on your desk for a week, Richard, I saw it there; you said it was the best news out of the south in a year." She felt her husband's arm go still under her hand, and kept going, gently, because she had been married to him for twenty-six years and knew exactly how long it took a thing to arrive in that great head once it had started on its way. "The shields, dear. The ones Curtis keeps writing to you about, the ones every officer in the guard would sell his mother for. The flying rigs, which Curtis also keeps writing to you about, in a rather different tone. And, far more important than any of that—" she smiled at the small young man in her son's old coat "—the person who has made it possible for me to sleep in this house in summer for the first time since I married into it. This is Alphonse's friend, Richard. This is *Tor*."

She watched it arrive.

She watched her husband look down at the boy, properly now, the way you look at a word on a page when somebody has told you what it says and the letters finally settle into it. She watched him take in the size of him, and the coat, and the stock, and the shadows under the eyes, and the stacks of copper on the green cloth, and the plate lying in the middle of it like a dish, and the cool in the room, which he had walked into without noticing, as he walked into most things.

"Oh," said the King of Noram.

The boy, for his part, had gone the color of a radish.

He wasn't looking at the king. He was looking at her, and his face had on it an expression she had seen once or twice on very young officers, at their first court, when somebody had read out a commendation in front of everyone and they'd realized it had their own name in it.

"*Tor*-shields?" he said faintly.

"That's what they're calling them."

"That's a *terrible* name."

"I'm afraid it's stuck."

"Who *calls* them that?"

"Everyone, dear," said Connie, kindly. "Since the spring."

The boy put his hand to his forehead. Then he took it away again, abruptly, and his eyes went past her, over her shoulder, to the green table with its copper, and widened, and Connie watched him remember something so dreadful that the shields went clean out of his head.

"Oh, no," he said. "Oh, *no*. You're the *queen*."

"I'm afraid so."

"And he's— and I haven't— I *made* you something. Both of you. All of you. Last night. That's what it was *for*." He was already moving. "I'm so sorry. I'm so sorry, I should have— you've been standing there all this time, and I've been—" He gestured at Ursula, at the chair, at the whole wreck of the last quarter of an hour, and gave up, and went to the table.

* * *

He came back with his hands full.

He came back across the carpet the way a boy at home comes in from the oven with a tray of something hot, quick and careful at once, eyes on the tray, and there on his two palms lay copper squares, each no bigger than a playing card, each threaded on a length of plain gray hemp string. Connie had worn the great crown of Noram twice in her life and the lesser one more often than she could count, and she had never been offered anything in quite that way: as if it might spill.

"This one's yours," he said, and held one out to her. "Ma'am. Your Majesty. I'm sorry about the string. It's all I had. It wants to be wool, really, or a chain, it'll rot if it gets wet, I found that out this morning. In the bath." He went pink again and pressed on. "It's like the plate. On the table. Only it's just for you. It keeps the air right next to you right. Cool when it's hot. Warm when it's cold. You put your hand on it, flat, like this, and you tell it what *right* is, and then you don't have to think about it again."

Connie took it. It lay on her palm, small and plain and slightly warm from his hand, a square of copper with a fine dark line etched on it that went round in a loop like an egg and then down. She looked at it for a moment. Then she put it over her head, over her rings and her pearls and the collar of her best dark dress, and laid her hand flat on it, as he'd shown her, and thought, quite deliberately, *right*.

And the heat went away.

It didn't go all at once. It went the way a headache goes, when you've stopped noticing it was there: one moment it was everywhere, in the backs of her knees, under her arms, in the damp at the nape of her neck under her hair that had been there, it seemed to her, since the first week of summer, since the first summer she'd come to this city as a girl of nineteen to be married, and the next moment it simply *wasn't*, and she was standing in her own skin as if she'd just come in from a walk on a fine spring morning. She hadn't known how tired she was of it. She hadn't known until it stopped.

She found she was holding the copper square against her chest with both hands, the way you'd hold a letter.

"And yours, sir," the boy was saying to her husband, and holding one up, a long way up. "And Rolph's. Alphie's. Sorry. And—" He stopped, and turned, and Connie saw him look at the girl standing apart by the door, with her cheeks still wet and her chin still up, and saw him not even hesitate. "And this one's for you, my lady. I made spares. I made twenty. I didn't know how many people there'd be."

Ursula looked at the copper square on his outstretched palm. She looked at it for long enough that Connie felt her own throat tighten.

Then she took it.

Richard had put his on too, over his coat, with a sort of baffled care, the way he'd once let Karina put a daisy chain on him in the fig walk when she was five. Alphonse, beside him, had already got his round his neck and was tucking it away inside his shirt, out of sight, with the quick, practiced fingers of a boy who'd been tucking things out of sight all his life.

"The city," said Alphonse, "is Ashford, Tor."

The boy turned to him.

"The water. The purifier, the big one, the pipe one. You built it for Sara's family in the spring, remember? For the town on the river with the sewer in the cistern?" Alphonse was buttoning his collar over the string. "Ashford's a city of eleven thousand people, and in the spring it was burying children. And now it isn't. That's *saving a city*, Tor. That's what everyone calls it. That's what the report on my father's desk called it." He finished the button. "And everything you've ever made is a Tor-something now. Tor-shields. Tor-plates, the heating and cooling. The Tor-rigs, for flying. The food dryers. Even the clothes one, the drying field, the very first one from the laundry; Lady Arden has her maids use it in front of guests." He looked down at the copper hidden under his shirt, and then up, and something went across his face that Connie, who had watched that face since before it could talk, couldn't quite put a name to. "And now whatever *these* are going to be called. Tor-something. They're good, Tor. They're really good." A pause. "When did you make them? I honestly thought I knew about everything you'd built."

Connie heard it. He'd tried to say it lightly, and it hadn't come out light. *I thought I knew.* He'd written to her for two years about this boy, and he had been, she understood now, very proud of knowing.

Well. He had hidden a whole Ursula from his friend for two years. Perhaps it would do him good.

"Last night," said the boy.

"Last *night*?"

"I didn't sleep. I wanted to make something nice for your mother. Since she'd gone to so much trouble about having me." He looked at Connie, and then away, shy again. "And, well. It's a bit warm. Here. In the capital." He considered. "I don't know if any of you have ever noticed that?"

Alphonse laughed. It burst out of him, loud and helpless, the way he'd laughed as a little boy before he'd learned that a prince doesn't. And then, somehow, everybody was laughing: Richard, in his great slow rumble, as if it had been a long time since he'd had cause; Ursula, wetly, into her hand, with the copper square still clutched in her fist; Connie herself, holding her amulet to her chest with both hands and laughing in the cool, in her own house, in summer, like a girl.

The boy looked round at them all, a little puzzled, and then smiled, uncertainly, as if he'd made a joke without quite meaning to and wasn't sure which part had been funny.

* * *

It was Alphonse who did the rest. Connie let him.

She watched him do it the way she'd once watched him, at twelve, take a horse that had thrown two grooms and talk it down to standing in the middle of the yard, quiet and patient and not once raising his voice. He went to his father, and stood beside him, and didn't argue. He didn't go formal. She noticed that; she'd half braced for it. He spoke in his own voice, the one he'd brought home from school, the one with all the edges worn off.

"She didn't know, Father."

"So she says."

"So I *say*. I've known her since she was four. You've known her since she was born. Does she look like someone who knew?" He nodded at Ursula, by the door, with her ruined face. "And Tor says so."

Richard looked down at the boy.

"I felt it," said the boy, simply, before anyone could ask. "Sir. When she— when you asked her. I can feel it, sometimes, off people. If it's strong. It's just the field, everybody's got one, they told us in theory. And it wasn't— she wasn't *caught*. It wasn't like someone who's been caught." He searched for it. "It was like someone who'd been robbed. In their sleep. And woke up."

There was a silence.

"Hm," said Richard, which in twenty-six years of marriage Connie had learned meant *yes*.

He held out his hand to Ursula. Not unkindly. She looked at it, and understood, and unpinned the dragonfly from her collar with fingers that shook only a little, and laid it on his enormous palm. It looked very small there. It looked like a child's toy. Richard closed his hand over it slowly, and Connie saw the boy's eyes follow it all the way in, with a hunger he didn't trouble to hide.

"I'd like to look at that," said the boy. "Later. If I may. Properly."

"I'm sure you would," said the king.

He turned back to Ursula.

"My dear," he said. "I believe you. I believe you didn't know." He let that sit a moment. "And I'm sorry. But I won't undo it. I can't. A betrothal to this house can't carry a question in it, not even one with an answer. You know that. Your mother will tell you the same."

Ursula curtsied. Not deep this time. Just enough. "Yes, Your Majesty."

"Then we'd better talk to your mother," said Alphonse. "And your father. Today. All of us." He looked at Connie, then back at his father. "And Count Ward, if he can be got. Before anyone decides what this means without us. Before somebody in County Ward decides the crown's called their count a traitor in front of his own neighbors, and somebody in this city decides County Ward's an Austran province. That's how wars start. That's exactly how. Somebody pins a brooch on a girl, and six months later there are men in a field."

Connie looked at her son.

She'd sent him to Lairdgren with a lockbox and a nickname and Wensa in the accounts office, to learn to be a man people didn't bow to; and he'd come back, it seemed, having learned something else as well. She didn't say so. She folded the thought away, with the others, for later.

"Tonight," said Richard, after a moment. "Dinner. The small dining room. Everyone's in town; the council doesn't sit till autumn, but half the counties have come up for the season. Thorgood and his lady. Ward, if he'll come." His mouth went grim under the beard. "He'll come."

"I'll write the cards," said Connie.

* * *

Ursula found her voice just as they were turning to go.

She'd been standing by the door all this time with the copper square in her fist, and Connie had seen her, more than once, look at the boy: at the back of his head, at the borrowed coat, at the green table with its stacks of copper and its pages of eggs. Looking, Connie thought, the way you look at a sum that won't come out.

"You didn't know," said Ursula.

The boy turned round.

"Any of it," said Ursula. "Did you." It wasn't a question. Her voice was hoarse and very steady. "Who I was. Who *he* was." She tipped her head at Alphonse. "You thought he was a merchant's son. You thought I was some merchant's daughter he'd been promised to, and you'd made a mess of it, and so you'd—" She stopped. "You offered to marry me, and you didn't even know there was anything to *get*."

"Get?" said the boy, bewildered.

"A title. A place. Money. Favor. The crown's gratitude for taking a problem off its hands." Ursula's mouth twisted. "Do you know how many men in this city would have made me that offer this afternoon, if they'd been standing where you were? None. And the ones who'd have made it tomorrow would have been counting the whole time." She looked down at him, a long way down. "You weren't counting."

"I don't—" The boy looked genuinely lost. "I just thought you'd be left with nothing. And it was my fault."

"That's not noble," said Ursula. "Noble is what my father is, and it's mostly counting. That's—" She shook her head. "I don't know what that is."

"It's Tor," said Alphonse.

He came and stood beside his friend, and put a hand on his shoulder, a big hand on a small shoulder in a gray coat that had once been his own.

"He's going places, Urs," he said. "You watch. He should already have a mark of valor, by rights, for Ashford. Nobody asked him twice. Nobody paid him. Sara came up with a letter, and he read it, and he got cross about where they'd put the sewer, and then he packed a basket and went down to the chambers and didn't come out for *six days*." His hand tightened. "We didn't know if he was going to. Not by the end. Nobody did."

"It was only a purifier," said the boy, to the carpet, scarlet to the collar. "It's the second-year sigil. Anyone could have—"

"*Tor*," said Alphonse, and Ursula, and Connie, all at once.

He shut his mouth.

Somewhere far off over the roofs, the city bells began to ring the third hour after noon: first the thin one down the hill, and then the great one in the palace tower that made the windows hum.

Alphonse's head came up like a hound's.

"*Sara*," he said.

The boy's face went white. "The luncheon."

"The luncheon."

"We're *late*. We're late for *Sara*. Trice'll—" He looked round wildly, at the copper, at the king, at the queen, at Ursula, at the door. "I'm so sorry. Sir. Your Majesty. Ma'am. I'm so sorry, I have to— we promised—"

"Go," said Connie.

He went. He went with Alphonse's hand at his back, steering, across the carpet and out of the door, and then, a moment later, back in again, at a run, to snatch up two of the copper squares from the table and stuff them into the pocket of the gray coat, "for Sara, and for Trice," and out again; and then his head came round the door one last time, wild-haired, and he said to the king, with great earnestness, "Thank you, Rich," and was gone.

His feet went away down the corridor, very fast.

Richard Cordes, King of Noram, stood in the middle of the cool guest room with an Austran brooch in one fist and a copper square on hemp string round his neck, and looked at the empty doorway for some time.

"Rich," he said.

"You did ask," said Connie.

He looked down at her. She took his arm again, and found, to her surprise, that she was smiling, in the cool, with her hand on her chest.

"Well," said the king. "I suppose I did."
