"I called him *Rich*."

The carriage had got as far as the bottom of the palace hill before Tor said it. He'd been quiet until then, sitting very upright on the seat opposite with his hands on his knees, the two copper squares bulging in the pocket of the gray coat, and Rolph had thought, for one optimistic street, that he might have taken the whole morning in stride.

"You did," Rolph agreed.

"To his *face*. I said *thank you, Rich*. On my way out of the door. Like he was the man who brings the coal." Tor put both hands over his face. His voice came through them. "And before that I said *Rick*. And I sat down. In front of him. And I told him he had the wrong number of— no, that was the soup. That was your mother. I told your *mother*—"

"You told my mother her soup was lovely."

"I *ate it first*." Tor took his hands away. His face was the color of the stock at his throat. "I took the first— Rolph, they'll— is there a law? There must be a law. About the king. About what you're allowed to call him."

"There are several," said Rolph. "None of them covers *Rich*. He asked you to."

"He was being *polite*."

"My father," said Rolph, "has never in his life been polite by accident."

The carriage swung round a corner into the corn market, and the noise came in at the window, and the smell of hot horse and hot bread, and Rolph waited until they were through it.

"Listen," he said. "Tor. Look at me. You gave them presents. You gave my mother a thing she's going to wear until it falls apart, and she's going to tell every woman in the city where she got it. You gave my father one, and he put it on in front of you, and I've seen him take presents from ambassadors with a face like a shut door. That's done. That's yours." He leaned forward. "And the other thing's done, too. You talked to him like a man. You can't take that back now."

"I could apologize."

"You can't. That's the point." Rolph tried to think how to put it so it would go in. "If you go back tomorrow and bow to the floor and call him Your Majesty three times in a sentence, after today, it won't look like respect. It'll look like you're making fun of him. Like you're doing an impression." He watched that land. "In public, in front of the court, in front of people, you're careful. You bow. You say *Your Majesty*. You do everything right, and you do it better than anyone, because you would anyway. But in a room with the door shut, you've set it now. That's how you talk to him. That's how he's going to expect you to talk to him."

Tor looked at him for a long moment.

"That's *worse*," he said.

"It's how it works."

"Who decided that's how it works?"

"Everybody," said Rolph. "Trust me."

Tor turned and looked out of the window. The white city went past, awning after awning, blue and saffron and green. His lips had begun to move. His right hand came up and lay flat on his chest, over the shield, and stayed there, and his eyes went somewhere a little beyond the street, and Rolph watched him go.

It wasn't a full trance. Rolph had sat through enough of those to know the difference: in a full one Tor didn't blink, didn't hear, and had to be fed. This was the shallow kind, the one he slipped into on the stairs on test days, or at the back of a crowded room. His breathing slowed. His shoulders came down. He was still there, in a way; if Rolph had said his name he'd have answered, after a moment, from a little further off than usual. He was just somewhere quieter.

Rolph let him have it. He sat back in the heat and watched the city go by, and straightened his collar, and then caught himself doing it, and stopped, and found that he was thinking about the Debri house, and about who would be standing on the steps of it, and made himself think about something else.

* * *

Sara had been standing in the shade of the great door for most of a quarter of an hour when the carriage came into the forecourt, and she had spent all of it rehearsing.

It hadn't helped. She'd said every version of it in her head to the white stones and the palms and the banners hanging still in the heat, the dark balance on each one with its pans perfectly level, and none of them had sounded like anything she could say to Tor's face. *I should have told you.* *I meant to tell you.* *It isn't a secret, exactly, it's only—* She'd written to him, in her head, three different letters, and torn them all up.

The carriage stopped at the foot of the steps. Rolph got out first, enormous in his blue calling coat, and looked up at the house, and then at her, and smiled with half his face. Then Tor got out.

He was in Rolph's old gray linen, the summer coat she remembered from a garden party years ago, with the cuffs turned back twice to show the green lining. Somebody had tied his stock, and tied it well. Somebody had put a waistcoat on him with little leaves all over it. Under all of that, plain as a signpost, were his own brown school trousers and his own school boots, dusty from the forecourt; and above it, his hair had given up entirely. He looked like a boy who had been dressed for a wedding by several different relatives who hadn't spoken to each other first.

He looked up at the house. He looked at the whole of it, the terraces climbing one above another, the carved lattice, the arches at the top open to the white sky, and his mouth opened a little, and he said nothing. His hand was flat on his chest.

Sara came down the steps.

"Tor," she said. "Rolph." She found she was smiling, and that it was costing her something. "You found us."

"It's very big," said Tor, a little faintly.

"It's a house."

"It's a very big house." He looked at her properly then, and his eyes cleared a little, as if he'd come back from somewhere. "Hello, Miss Sara. Thank you for having us. You look—" He stopped, and she watched him decide that whatever he'd been going to say wasn't allowed. "Well. You look well."

"Everything's ready," said Sara. "They're all inside. We're set up in the hall." She hesitated. "It's a working lunch. I did say."

"Trice said."

"Trice said." She turned to go up the steps, and then turned back. "Tor. There are rather a lot of people."

* * *

There were twenty-six of them.

Sara had counted. She'd counted the chairs that morning, with the steward, and then counted the names, and then counted again, and then gone and stood in the forecourt so she wouldn't count a fourth time. Now she watched Tor count them, standing just inside the arch of the hall with Rolph at his shoulder, and saw his lips move, and saw him get to twenty-six, and saw him go somewhere else.

It was a fine room. She'd always thought so. It ran the depth of the house, long and high and cool, with a painted ceiling of vines, and on either side, instead of walls, there were arcades: rows of slender white columns with horseshoe arches between them, open all the way along onto the two inner courts. Through the arches on the left you could see the old court, with its fountain in a basin of blue and white tiles; through the arches on the right, the new one, with a wider pool and a jet that went up and fell back in the sun like a handful of thrown coins. The sound of the water came in on both sides, quietly, all the time. The palms in their pots stood between the columns. The light came in green and broken through the leaves and lay in bars across the floor.

And down the middle of all of it ran the table, long enough for thirty, with the cloth half hidden under bowls of fruit and cold fowl and bread and ledgers and rolled papers and inkstands and someone's spectacles, and round it sat the house: the factors from the river warehouses, the woman who ran the glass-hall stall, old Peder from the wharf, her aunt Merrin, her cousins, three men from the copper buyers, the two clerks who did nothing but shields. Half of them men and half women. All of them had stopped talking. All of them were looking at the door.

Sara looked at Tor.

He'd gone into it. She'd seen it before: in the yard with a knife at his neck; on the stair in the cold. His face had gone perfectly polite and perfectly still, and his eyes were open and looking at the table, but not quite at anyone at it, and his hand was flat on his chest again, over the place where the shield sat. He looked as though he were listening very carefully to something a long way off.

She knew she wouldn't get much more out of him than *please* and *thank you* for a while, and that he wouldn't notice half of what anyone did with their face. She thought, with a sort of hopeless tenderness, that it might even be a mercy.

At the head of the table, a woman stood up.

She was tall, as tall as most men at the table and taller than some, and straight, and somewhere past forty, in a dark green day dress with a high collar and no jewelry at all except a ring with a seal on it. Her hair had been fair once and was going quietly gray at the front, and she wore it up close and plain, like a woman who had no time to wear it any other way. She had Sara's nose and Sara's chin and Sara's way of looking at a thing before she decided what it was worth. She had looked at it, Sara knew, all the way across the hall, the moment they came in.

Sara had imagined this moment a hundred times. She had never once imagined it with her heart going like this.

"Tor," she said. "Rolph. This is my mother." She took a breath. "Heather Debri. Head of the Debri house."

Tor turned his head, slowly, from a long way off. He looked at the woman in green. Sara saw him look at the face, and at the ring, and at the chair at the head of the table, and saw something try to fit itself together behind his eyes, and not manage it, because he was too tired and too far away and there were twenty-six people.

"I thought," said Tor, quite politely, "your father—"

"My mother," said Sara, "is the head of the house."

She said it too quickly. She heard herself. Her mother heard it too, and something passed over Heather Debri's face, very briefly, and was put away.

"Master Baker," said her mother. Her voice was low and dry and carried without effort, the voice of a woman who'd spent thirty years making herself heard across a wharf. "My daughter wrote to me in the winter. She said she'd seen a thing she couldn't put a price on, and that I should come and see it for myself." The corner of her mouth moved. "She has never once in her life told me she couldn't put a price on anything. So I've come."

Sara's face went hot. *Father,* the letter had said, at the top, in her best hand. Her mother had never once remarked on it. She didn't now.

And then her mother turned, and saw Rolph properly, and her face changed again, and Sara understood a whole second too late what was about to happen.

Heather Debri curtsied.

Not the merchant's bob she gave to counts and factors. The real one: down, and down, and held, her head bowed, the seal ring hand flat against her skirt. And behind her, all down both sides of the long table, in a scrape of chairs that sounded like a wave going over shingle, twenty-five members of the Debri house got to their feet and bowed and curtsied to the young man in the blue coat.

"Your Highness," said her mother, to the floor. "The house is honored."

Sara's eyes went straight to Tor.

She couldn't help it. Every muscle in her went toward him at once: two years. Two years of *Rolph's family are merchants, Tor*. Two years of steering him off the subject at breakfast, of kicking Trice under tables, of changing the subject to drying fields. Two years, and her own mother had just undone all of it in one curtsy in front of him, and in front of the whole house, and she could feel her mouth opening to say something, anything, *he's a friend of the family*, *it's a joke we have*—

Tor was looking at the bowing table with an expression of mild, polite interest, as if it were weather.

Her mother rose. She hadn't seen Sara's face; she was looking at Rolph. "Prince Alphonse," she said. "Welcome. And Master Baker." She turned the full weight of her attention on Tor, and Sara saw her take him in, all of him, the gray coat and the leaf waistcoat and the stock and, last and longest, the brown school trousers. "Welcome. Will you sit? Here, across from me. Both of you."

There were two chairs, across the head of the table from hers. Tor went and sat in one of them, carefully, as though the chair might have opinions. Rolph sat in the other. The table sat down again, all down its length, with a sound like the wave going back.

Sara stood where she was, with her heart still going.

Then she went and sat at her mother's right hand, where she'd been put, across from Tor, and found that Rolph was looking at her. He gave her the smallest shrug in the world.

* * *

Her mother began, the way she always began, with something that wasn't business.

"I'm afraid you'll find us very warm, Master Baker," she said. "I apologize for it. My daughter will have told you we've had requests for your cooling plates." She lifted one hand from the table, and let it fall. "We've had nothing *but* requests. Since the spring. Every house on the Hill, the glass halls, two temples and a hospital. We've a list as long as this table. And we can't make a single one." She smiled, thinly. "No template. We've been turning away nobles at the door like a baker on the day the oven cracks."

Tor said something. Sara saw his lips move. Then he seemed to hear the question underneath, and come up a little way out of wherever he was, and look at her mother properly.

"Oh," he said. "But I've got one."

The whole table went still again.

"A template," said Tor. His voice was very odd. Sara listened to it and couldn't place it at first, and then could. He was trying to talk like the room. She could hear him trying. It came out with Rolph's long, lazy school vowels on the front of the words and her own clipped trade endings on the back of them, so that every sentence started on the Hill and finished on the wharf, and none of it matched. "For the cooling. It's in my trunk. At the—" He stopped. "At Rolph's. I can't get it to you today, I'm sorry, I didn't think to bring it. But I can send it. Tomorrow. If that's all right."

"If that's—" Her mother stopped.

At the far end of the table one of the copper buyers had put his hand over his mouth.

"That's very generous," said her mother, after a moment, quite evenly. "The house is proud to work with you, Master Baker. I want you to know that. Whatever else is said today." She paused. "I mean it."

"Oh," said Tor. "Thank you." Then his face changed, and he put his hand into the pocket of the gray coat, and took it out again full of copper and gray string, and looked at it with an expression of sudden distress. "Oh, no. I only brought two."

"Two what?"

"These." He held them out across the table, on his open palm, two little squares of copper on hemp. "They're for keeping cool. Just you. Not the room. I made them last night." He looked down the length of the table, at twenty-six warm faces, and Sara watched the arithmetic go across his own. "I brought one for Miss Sara and one for Trice. I didn't know there'd be—" He swallowed. "I'm so sorry. I should have brought more. I made twenty. They're all at the palace."

"At the palace," murmured someone down the table, very quietly.

Her mother reached out and took one.

She did it slowly. She held it up on its string and looked at it the way she looked at everything, the way she'd taught Sara to look: the metal, the line, the knot, the hand that had made it. Then she put it over her head, and laid her own hand flat on it, as Tor was showing her with his, and Sara saw her mother's face, which had stayed perfectly composed through a prince and a template, do something it had not done in years.

It went young. Just for a moment. The lines round her mouth eased, as if someone had loosened a string.

"Oh," said Heather Debri.

The second one went down the table. It went from hand to hand: to her aunt Merrin, who said "*Oh*" in exactly the same voice; to old Peder, who put it on and then took it off and then put it on again, as if checking it wasn't a trick of the mind; to the clerks, the factors, the woman from the glass halls, who held it a long time and didn't say anything at all and passed it on with her eyes shining. Wherever it went, people went quiet in the same way. By the time it came back up the other side of the table, the whole room was looking at Tor with an expression Sara had only ever seen before on the faces of people at an auction when something came up that wasn't on the list.

"We'll want to make these," said her mother.

"Of course," said Tor.

"Master Baker, I'll want the template. For these as well."

"Yes. I'll send that too. I'll send them all."

"Mother," said Sara, quietly.

Her mother looked at her. And then down at the table, at a ledger lying open by her plate, and Sara saw her decide.

"There's something I have to tell you," said her mother, "before we go further. And I would rather tell you myself than have you hear it from a clerk." She put two fingers on the ledger. "We have no money for you, Master Baker. Not at present."

Tor blinked.

"The shields," said her mother. "In the spring we had an order. A very large order. From the crown, for the army: every officer in every regiment, and then every man in the border forts, and then the river guard." She didn't look at Rolph. She very carefully didn't. "It's the largest single order this house has had in a generation. And the crown pays as the crown pays. In its own time. We've had to pay for the copper and the builders and the carting out of our own reserves, and we'll be paid back with interest, I don't doubt it, and in the meantime—"

"In the meantime," said the man on her left, "the reserves are gone."

He hadn't spoken until then. He was tall for a Debri, with Sara's fairness gone to sand-brown and a wide, square, practical face, and he had a pen behind his ear that he'd clearly forgotten was there. Kris. Her oldest brother, who would one day sit where their mother was sitting, and who had been doing sums since he was six and had never once in his life been able to stop himself doing them out loud.

"Your share on the shields alone," said Kris, to Tor, quite pleasantly, "comes to a little over four thousand gold, Master Baker. As of last week. The food dryers are another eleven hundred. And we have, at this table, at this moment, in coin, not quite enough to pay the wharf men on fifth day." He spread his hands. "So. That's where we are."

Tor looked at him. He looked as if Kris had said it in a language he only partly knew.

"Four thousand," said Tor.

"And some."

"For the *shields*."

"For the shields," said Kris.

"I didn't—" Tor stopped. He looked down at the copper square on its string, which had come back up the table to Sara and was lying by her plate. "They're only to keep the knives off," he said, a little helplessly, as though somebody had made a mistake in the arithmetic. "And the army. The army has— don't they have—"

"No," said Rolph.

He hadn't spoken since he sat down. He didn't raise his voice now. He didn't need to. Sara had known him two years at school, and she'd heard him laugh in every register a voice could laugh in, and she had never once heard this one: quiet, and smooth, and level, and very, very cold.

"Mistress Debri," said Rolph. "Forgive me. I want to be sure I understand. The Debri house has taken this man's work, and sold it, at a very great price, to the largest buyer in the kingdom. And has not paid him." He tilted his head. "Is that the reputation the house wants? In the city? *Debri doesn't pay its builders.* Because people will say it. I'd say it. I'd be sorry to."

Her mother had gone a little pale. "Your Highness. That isn't— nobody here wishes— the house has always—" She stopped, and steadied, and when she went on her voice was her own again. "We'll pay him. Every copper. With interest. I'd put that in writing before this lunch is over, and stand behind it in any court in the land. But the order was the crown's." She looked at Rolph then, straight at him, for the first time since the curtsy. "The *king's*, Your Highness. And one doesn't send the king a bill on the first of the month."

There was a silence at the table.

Sara looked at Tor. He was sitting very still between the two of them, her mother and Rolph, with his hands flat on the tablecloth on either side of his plate, and his face had gone the dreadful gray-white it went when people raised their voices near him, even when they didn't. She saw him look from one to the other. She saw him take in the length of the table, the twenty-six faces, the ledgers, the coin that wasn't there. And she saw him, quite clearly, decide that all of this was his fault.

"I don't need it," said Tor.

Everybody looked at him.

"The money. I don't need it. Not now." He was speaking quickly, to the tablecloth, the Hill vowels gone entirely and the wharf with them, just his own voice, the one from the woods, with flour on it. "I've got some. In a box. I've got more than I've ever had. I don't need any more. I'm not *doing* anything with it." He looked up at her mother. "If you could just— could you give me the things instead? To build with? Copper. Good copper, the thin kind, the sheets. And acid, and resist. And chains for these—" he touched the amulet by Sara's plate "—because the string rots, I found out this morning. In the bath. And then I can keep building, and you can make them, and when there's money you can pay me then, whenever, I don't mind. Really. It's only money."

Nobody said anything.

Then, from her mother's left, very quietly, Kris said, "Mother."

It was one word. It was the word their mother used herself, across a table, when someone had offered her a price that was too good. Sara knew exactly what it meant. *Careful. Where's the hook?* She watched her mother hear it, and look at Tor, and narrow her eyes very slightly, the way she narrowed them at a cargo that weighed light. She could see her mother doing the sum: a builder who wouldn't take his money. A debt that would sit there, growing, every month, every new device. A house that owed more and more to one man, until one day the man, or the man's friends, came and asked for something in return that wasn't money at all.

"Master Baker," said her mother slowly, "that is a very—"

"He means it," said Sara.

Her mother turned.

"He means it, Mother." Sara heard her own voice go flat and slow and even, her father's voice, the one for a table with paper on it, and for once she was glad of it. "There's no hook. I've watched him for a year. He gave away the copies he thought weren't good enough. He took pies for drying fields. He sent his first gold home in bags with his sisters' names on them. He doesn't *want* anything from us. He's not putting us in his debt. He's giving you the time you just asked him for. That's all." She put her hand flat on the ledger beside her mother's. "Accept it. Give him the copper. Give him whatever he needs. Chains, acid, sheets, the good resist, not the store stuff. Write it down, and write down what we owe, and pay it when we can. And thank him."

Her mother looked at her for a long moment. Then she looked at Tor.

"Is that what you're asking, Master Baker?"

"Yes," said Tor, with enormous relief. "Yes, please. That." He hesitated. "And— could some of it go home? Not much. To my mother. In Two Rivers. And— the village. They're building. Some of the houses are old, and the winters are bad, and there's never enough for the roofs, or the—" He stopped. "Timber, and nails, and slate, I suppose. For the winter. If it's not too much trouble. Only if there's any to spare."

Kris had taken the pen from behind his ear. He was writing. Sara saw what he wrote: *Two Rivers. Mother; village. Timber, nails, slate, glazing, stoves*, and then, after a moment, underlined, *whatever they ask*.

"It's not too much trouble," said Heather Debri. Her voice had gone a little rough. "Consider it done."

Sara let her breath out.

And because she'd been holding it for an hour, and because it was over, and because she was more tired than she'd known, she turned to her mother and said, under her breath, but not quite under enough:

"There. You see? I didn't even have to dress up."

It went down the table like a dropped glass.

Her mother went still. Kris put his pen down, very carefully. Aunt Merrin found something to look at in the fountain court.

And Rolph said, into the quiet, in that same smooth, cold, beautiful voice that wasn't his at all:

"*Really*, Heather?"

Her mother turned to him.

"Turning your own daughter on him." Rolph didn't move. He sat back in his chair with his hands on its arms, at his ease, enormous, his face perfectly pleasant, and every person at the table could feel the temperature of it. "Like that. To soften him up for the bad news. How very *noble* of you. I'd expected rather more of a trading house."

Sara wanted to sink through the floor.

Her mother had gone the color of the tablecloth. For a moment she said nothing at all. Then she lifted her chin, the way Sara lifted hers, and Sara saw the merchant come up through the mother like a fish through water.

"It wasn't to *soften* him," said Heather Debri. "Your Highness. Forgive me. But I won't have it said in my own hall that I'd use her like a lure." She looked across at Tor. "It would be a good match. A very good one. Sara isn't the heir; her brother is. She can marry where she likes, within reason, and there is no one in the city, no one, I'd sooner see her with than a man who'd give my house a template for nothing and ask for roofing for his village in return." She drew a breath. "Master Baker. Would it be agreeable to you if I sent someone to your mother? To open the conversation? Properly?"

Tor looked at her.

Sara watched it reach him. It took a while. It came all the way down from wherever he was, through the half-trance and the heat and the twenty-six faces, and arrived; and when it arrived, his face went through bewilderment, and then something like alarm, and then, last of all, a kind of tired, gentle understanding that was much worse than either, because she had seen it before, in a warm room in the winter, with the window open.

"Oh," he said. "Oh. That's very kind." He smiled at her mother. It was a lovely smile, and quite sincere, and it went right past everything she'd said. "But I don't think my mother would know what to do with someone coming. She'd want to feed them. She'd make them help with the bread." He looked down at his plate. "Is it— does everybody do this? Here? In the capital?" He glanced at Sara, quite without reproach. "Miss Sara did it too. In the winter. And Trice. I thought it was just school. I didn't know it was a— a city thing." He nodded slowly, as if fixing it in his mind. "I'll get used to it. I expect. Or I'll learn not to stand where people can do it. I'm a bit tired today, that's all."

Nobody laughed.

Sara found she couldn't look at her mother. She found she couldn't look at Tor either. She looked at Rolph, because there was nowhere else, and Rolph wasn't looking at her at all. He was looking at her mother, and his face had gone very still, and something moved under it that she couldn't read, and didn't, at that moment, try to.

"While Master Baker is in the capital," said Rolph, quietly, in his own voice now, "any proposals of marriage for him go to my mother. The Queen. In writing." He looked down the table, at all of them. "All of them."

"Of course, Your Highness," said Heather Debri.

And then it came to Sara, all at once, what she'd been too frightened to notice for the last half hour.

"Rolph," she said. "Tor's not— when Mother bowed, he didn't—" She turned to Tor. "You *know*?"

"About Rolph?" Tor looked up. "Oh. Yes. Since this morning. Sorry. I should have said."

"He had to find out," said Rolph. He sounded, suddenly, very tired himself. "There was a— situation. At home. This morning. I'll tell you about it later." He met her eyes for the first time. "Not here, Sara."

"Not here," Sara agreed, after a moment.

Her mother looked from one of them to the other, and back, and Sara saw her file it, all of it, the way she filed everything, and put it away for later, and pick up a ledger.

"Then perhaps," said Heather Debri, "we might talk about what the house would like to make next."

* * *

They talked about it for two bells.

It came from all round the table, once it started: from the factors, from the woman from the glass halls, from old Peder, from Aunt Merrin, who wanted something to keep the moth out of wool. Something for lamps. Something for ships. Something to keep milk sweet on the road, and fruit from bruising, and ink from freezing, and a hospital ward from smelling like a hospital ward. Sara watched it happen: watched the table forget, a little at a time, that it was talking to a builder from the woods in borrowed clothes, and start talking to him the way the house talked to itself, quickly and over each other, the way you talk to someone who might actually do the thing.

Tor said almost nothing. But at the first idea he'd turned to her and said, quietly, "Could I have some paper? And a pencil? Please?" and she'd sent for them, and he'd written down every single one, in his careful round hand, with the speaker's name beside it. When the stack got too tall he squared it. When it got taller he started a second.

It was Kris, near the end, who said the one that made Tor's pencil stop.

"Carts," said her brother, leaning back with his hands behind his head. "Wagons. That's the trouble with the flying rigs, if you ask the wharf. Nobody wants to go up in one. They're afraid of the height, and you need a shield on as well, and it's one man, one rig. One man can't carry a cargo." He shrugged. "If you could make a *cart* do it. Not up. Just— off the ground. Along the road. Ten bales, twenty, and a man walking beside it. That's what we'd buy. That's what every house on the river would buy."

Tor wrote it down. And then he looked at what he'd written, and didn't write anything else for a while.

* * *

The carriage was waiting in the forecourt in the long gold light, and Tor got into it as though the seat were the only solid thing for a mile. Rolph got in after him. The door shut. The wheels moved.

And Tor put his face in his hands, and his shoulders shook, once, and then again, and Rolph sat opposite him and let him, and said nothing, and looked out of the window at the white house going away behind them with the banners hanging still on its terraces and Sara a small figure in green on the steps, not waving.

After a while Tor took his hands away. His eyes were wet. He wiped them on the green cuff of the gray coat, carefully, as if the coat were someone else's, which it was.

"Sorry," he said.

"Don't be."

"There were twenty-six of them."

"I know. I counted."

"And her *mother*—"

"I know," said Rolph.

Tor sat for a moment. Then he reached into the coat and took out the stack of paper, the two stacks, and put them on his knees, and began to go through them, one sheet after another, the way you'd look through a basket of eggs for the cracked ones. His lips moved. Moth. Lamps. Milk. Ink. He turned the sheets over one by one and laid them face down on the seat beside him. And then he came to one, and stopped, and didn't turn it over.

"A cart," he said, to the paper.

Rolph looked at him.

"Not up," Tor said. "Kris is right. Nobody wants up. Up's frightening. But along. Like the luggage. The plates already follow, they already go along at knee height, they already *carry*, they did the whole way here with Trice's shoes in them. It's only bigger. It's only *more*." His hand had come up and was drawing in the air: a long flat shape, a little higher than a man's knee, and a stick figure walking beside it. "And you wouldn't need it to fly. Not really. It'd only need to float. A hand above the road. And you'd push it, or pull it, or it would follow you, like the plates, and a man could walk beside it all day with twenty bales on it and it'd be no heavier than—" He stopped. "Than nothing. It'd weigh nothing."

His lips went on moving after his voice had stopped.

Rolph let him go. He sat back and watched the city slide past, and found that what he wanted, very much, was to talk to someone. And the someone opposite him was halfway to a floating cart, and probably wouldn't hear, and that was perhaps the best kind of someone to talk to just now.

"It's a mess," said Rolph. "Ursula."

Tor's eyes came up, a little way.

"I can't marry her now. Father won't undo it; he can't. And I don't— I never wanted to, really, I mean, I like her, I've liked her since she was four and bit the dancing master, but that's not—" He stopped. "And then there's Ward. If Ward knew what that thing was, it's treason, and if he didn't, he's been made a fool of by Austra in front of the crown, and either way he's got a county full of men who'll take it very personally when they hear the crown called their count a spy. And somebody in this city will say County Ward's gone over. And then it's men in fields." He rubbed his face. "And in the middle of all that there's Urs. Who didn't do anything. And nobody will touch her now. Nobody."

"I offered," said Tor.

"I know you did." Rolph found he was smiling, a little, despite himself. "It was the best thing anyone's done all year. But you can't. And it's not you, it's the rule. She has to marry within three steps of her station. That's the law, for heirs. And she's heir to County Thorgood. That makes her as good as a countess, which means a baron at the very least. Or better." He shook his head. "And there isn't a baron in Noram who'll put his name next to hers this week. Not with the crown's decree on it. Not until somebody makes it safe."

Tor was quiet for a while.

"There's a dinner," he said.

"There's a dinner. Tonight. Her parents. Mother. Father. Ward, if he comes." Rolph looked out of the window. "Mother sent a letter to Trice this afternoon. She'll be there too. She'll want to be; she knows Urs, and she knows Ward's people, and she sees things at a table I don't." He paused. "And she'll want to know why she wasn't told sooner."

"Should we tell Tovey?" said Tor. "He'd want to help. He's— he'd be good. He's a count. He'd know what Ward—"

"No," said Rolph.

Tor looked at him.

"Not yet." Rolph shook his head slowly. "The fewer people know, the fewer people talk, and the fewer people talk, the easier it is to stop. Tovey would help. Tovey would help so hard that by tomorrow every count on the council would know exactly what he was helping with." He sighed. "Let's get through dinner first."

"All right," said Tor.

He looked down at the paper on his knees, the one he hadn't turned over. After a moment, Rolph saw his lips begin to move again.

The carriage went on up the long hill toward the palace, in the gold light, with its two passengers sitting very still: one thinking about war, and the other about carts.
