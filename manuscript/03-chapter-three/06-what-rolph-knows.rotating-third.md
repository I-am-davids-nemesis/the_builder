Rolph had dressed, in the end, because it was something to do. Then he'd gone down to the porters' lodge, and the porter had known nothing. Then he'd gone to the infirmary, where the sister had known something and wouldn't say it. Then he'd stood in the main court for some time, looking at a long dark scuff on the flagstones and a scatter of what might have been soot, until a second-year he didn't know had come up and told him, breathless, that somebody had *attacked Count Thompson in the night*, and did Rolph know who, and Rolph had looked at him until he went away.

So he was sitting on his bed, with the ten squares on his knees, wrapped in the rag, when the bar lifted and Tor came in.

Tor didn't say anything. He crossed the room, turned around, and fell backward onto his own bed, full length, like a tree going over. The bed made a noise. Tor lay there with his arms out and his eyes shut and his boots, filthy, hanging off the end.

Rolph looked at him.

"Are you," he said, "even alive?"

"I don't know," said Tor, to the ceiling. "I think so. I'm meant to go to breakfast."

"*Breakfast?*"

"The headmaster said." Tor opened his eyes. "Rolph. I hit Tovey."

And then it all came out.

He told it lying on his back, staring upward, in the flat careful voice he used to read out a sigil, and Rolph let him. The workroom, and the numbness, and his leg not working. Trice on the ground in the dark. The sack. Tovey turning round. Running, and flying, actually *flying*, Rolph, through the air, over the sheep, and the wood, and the bramble, and sitting in the leaves until dawn, and Tovey hanging there over the trees and then just *falling out of the air*, plop, into the mud, and getting up and walking home, as if he'd remembered he'd left a lamp burning. Then working it out. The two fingers. Then the dean, and the basement, and the door you couldn't look at, and a room with a fire in it that was colder than the corridor, and Trice and the headmaster and Wensa sitting there in chairs you weren't meant to be able to see. And Tovey coming in and *apologizing*. And everyone being very kind about it, really, except Wensa.

"I hit a count," said Tor, at the end of it. "In a battle rage. From behind. With fifty copper squares. And I'm not dead, and I'm not in a cell, and nobody even seems—" He turned his head on the pillow and looked at Rolph, and his face was honestly bewildered. "How? You know about nobles. How is that *possible*?"

Rolph thought about it. He thought about several things he knew about Tovey, from a long way back, and didn't say any of them.

"I know Tovey," he said instead. "A bit. Enough. He's not going to come after you, Tor. Not ever. Not for this." He turned the parcel over on his knees. "He thinks you went in to help his cousin. Which you did. That's the whole of it, to Tovey. And he hates it. The rages. He hates them worse than anything. If you'd done something to him while he was himself, maybe. But you stopped him doing something while he wasn't. He'll be grateful to you till he dies, and he'll never once be able to say so properly, and you'll have to put up with it."

Tor took this in. He didn't look as if he believed it, quite. He looked as if he'd file it to think about later.

"And Trice," Rolph went on, "is—" He stopped, and chose the word. "She's different."

"Different?"

"From the rest of them. From most nobles. That's why she's spent so many years at the academy."

Tor sat up on his elbows. "Nobles?"

"Tor."

"*Trice* is a noble?"

Rolph looked at him with something like wonder. "Tor. What do you think goes on at that academy?"

Tor thought about it. Rolph could watch him do it, the way you could watch him do anything: the frown, the faint movement of the lips. "Spies," said Tor at last, slowly, as if it were rude to say. "And— well. Assassins. Learning to be. That's what the second-years say. All the black, and the knife, and the powder, and—" He touched his own throat. "She had a knife, Rolph. In *unarmed*."

"It trains people who might *get* assassinated," said Rolph. "Mostly. That's the point of it. The children of houses that have enemies. Nobles. The big merchant families. You learn what the knife looks like coming, and how to get out of the way of it, and who to watch at dinner." He shrugged. "Some of the people who learn all that go on to use it. That's the bit the second-years talk about. But that's why Trice is there. She's a duke's daughter. And that's why Sara's there. She's Debri. You know that. The Debri house."

There was a silence.

Then Tor lay back down, slowly, on the bed, and put his arm across his eyes.

"She doesn't know who I am," he said, from under it. "Sara. She doesn't— When she finds out I'm nobody, she'll want to change the deal. She'll have to. You can't have a Debri buying from a baker's son from Two Rivers. She'll have to tell her father. And he'll—"

"Tor," said Rolph, "she knows exactly who you are."

The arm came off the eyes.

"She's a merchant," said Rolph. "A real one, from a real house. She doesn't put twelve gold a board on anything she hasn't had looked into. She came and found me, in the Barrow, last night. Before she went up to see you." He smiled, a little, remembering it: Sara in the doorway of the back room in her green, with her comb in, perfectly at ease among forty drunk second-years, and asking him questions as if she were reading them off a list. "She wanted to know everything. Your family. Your people. Whether you work. Whether you drink. Whether you pay your debts. I told her."

"What did you tell her?"

"That your father's a baker and your mother's a baker and your brothers and sisters are bakers, and there are more of them than is reasonable. That you'll work until you fall down, and then you'll get up and apologize to the floor. And that you can bake," said Rolph. "Actually bake. Bread. Pies. That thing with the apples. Well enough to open a shop of your own if the building ever went wrong." He grinned. "That went down very well. Trice was there by then. You should have seen her face."

Tor looked at him with total incomprehension. "Why would that go down well?"

"Because nobody can *do* it, Tor."

"Everybody can do it."

"I can't make toast," said Rolph, with some pride. "I've tried. I set fire to a fork. Neither can anybody I grew up with. Neither can Trice. Neither can Sara, I'd bet, or Tovey, or half the people you're frightened of. There's not one of us who could feed ourselves for a week if the kitchens shut. And there you are, a builder, a good one, and if they took it all away from you tomorrow you'd just shrug and go and make bread. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know how *safe* that looks, to people like—" He stopped. "To merchants."

Tor turned this over for some time.

"She's very pretty," he said at last, gloomily. "Sara. And she's rich. And now she's a *Debri*. That's like being told the moon has a family name. It's not as if—" He didn't finish. He looked up at the ceiling as if it had personally disappointed him. "It doesn't matter. Who's Trice, then? Really?"

Rolph straightened a little. It was hard not to; you were taught to, saying it.

"The Ducharina Patricia Alyson Morgan," he said. "Daughter of the Duke of Morgan. Patricia to the court. Tricia, if you're being polite and don't know her well. Only her friends call her Trice."

"Why Trice?"

"It's from when she was small. Some royal brat of a Prince couldn't get his mouth round Patricia, when he was a baby. He called her Trice instead. She bit him for it, I'm told. But it stuck." Rolph shrugged, and looked at the parcel on his knees. "She doesn't seem to mind it. She lets her friends use it. You're one, apparently."

Tor received this as if it were bad news.

"I called her *Miss Trice*," he said. "To her face. For days."

"She loved it."

"Oh, *no*."

Rolph laughed, and Tor put his arm back over his eyes, and for a while neither of them said anything. The bell went. Somewhere down the corridor a door slammed and someone shouted about a missing boot.

"You should send those off," said Tor, from under his arm. "To your mother. If you hurry, they'll make the express."

"I know." Rolph got up. He went to the lockbox at the foot of his bed, and laid his hand flat on the lid until it clicked, and lifted out what he'd put in there two days ago, when he'd first thought of this: a box. It was the length of his forearm, and of some dark red wood that took the light, with a lid inlaid in a pattern of pale leaves and a clasp shaped like a closed hand, which was a small piece of work in itself. Inside, it was lined with gray velvet. He'd had it a long time.

Tor had sat up to look at it. Rolph saw him look: the quick, assessing glance that slid over the inlay and the clasp and the velvet and arrived at a number. It was the look Sara had, sometimes. Then Tor looked away again, very politely, as if he'd caught Rolph undressing.

Rolph untied the rag from the ten squares, and chose four, the four best, and laid them in the velvet side by side.

"Only four?" said Tor.

"Four for her. In the box. The other six go loose, wrapped, so she can give them out." He closed the lid. "And this has to be wrapped properly, or I'll never hear the end of it. You've no idea what her friends are like. They notice paper. They'll look at the knot before they look at what's inside, and if it's a merchant's knot they'll say so, very sweetly, at dinner." He began to hunt along the shelf for string. "But it doesn't matter. Nobody's going to give her anything like this. Nobody. Not even Father, and he's—" Rolph found the string. "Well. He's giving her something large. It won't be as good."

He sat back down to wrap it. He did it carefully, with good paper, folding the corners under the way he'd been shown, while Tor watched him from the bed.

"When's the other one going to be ready?" said Rolph, intent on a corner. "The food drying one. For fruit. I want to write and tell her it's coming, if—"

"I can't," said Tor.

Rolph looked up.

Tor was sitting on the edge of the bed. He'd taken off one boot and was holding it in his lap, and looking at it, and his face had the gray, stretched look again.

"I can't do it now," he said. "I'm sorry. I'm trying to think about it, and I can't. I need to be able to see the whole of it, and I can't see anything. I haven't slept. I keep— everything keeps going round." He put the boot down. "I'm sorry. I'll do it. I will. Just not now."

Rolph put down the box.

He'd asked for ten. That was the thing that went through him, quite suddenly, looking at Tor on the edge of the bed with one boot off. He'd come up the stairs from the Barrow two nights ago with a head full of other people's money and asked for ten, by the express, and Tor had done fifty in a night, and then been hit across a lawn by a count of the realm, and Rolph was sitting here asking about the next one. Like a man ordering bread.

"Take your time," he said. "Tor. I mean it. There's no hurry. There was never any hurry."

"There's the shield," said Tor, not really listening. "That's twelve days. And there's the rest for Sara, the fifty on the desk, and classes, and the building master, and Kolb. And Wensa." He laughed, without much in it. "Wensa's got it in for me now. She'll be watching everything. The headmaster says to act normally, so she's got nothing to look at, but I don't see how I'm meant to. Did you know," he added, in the tone of somebody remembering an odd detail, "she's a royal guard? Some kind of secret one. She's been one nineteen years. She had a cutter."

Rolph was very still.

"Say that again," he said.

Tor looked up at the change in his voice.

"Wensa," said Rolph. "Mistress Wensa. Accounts." He heard his own voice, quite flat, quite clear, every consonant in its place, and could do nothing about it. "She told you that she is a member of the royal guard. In those words. Are you certain?"

"Yes," said Tor, a little alarmed. "She said it to the headmaster. She said she held a commission. She took the hilt out, Rolph, it was—" He stopped. "Rolph? Are you all right?"

Two years of accounts. Two years of Wensa at the front of the cold room on the second floor, with her ink-stained finger and her ledgers, marking his sums with small neat ticks and never once, not once, raising her eyes to his face for longer than it took to hand back a paper. He'd thought she disliked him. He'd thought it was rather restful.

He'd wondered, his first year, lying awake: *who*. He'd looked at every new face for a month. And then he'd stopped, because there was nobody, and it had been such a relief.

"I'm fine," he said. His voice sounded exactly like his father's. "Go on."

Tor looked at him uncertainly for a moment. Then, perhaps because it was the only thing he could think of, he tried to make a joke of it.

"It's a pity she's not younger, really," he said. "Isn't it? I've never been chased about by a woman before. I'd have thought I'd enjoy it more."

Rolph stared at him.

Then he saw it. He saw it entire, all at once, before he could stop it: Mistress Wensa, gray-streaked and upright, in her plain dark dress, pursuing Tor across the main court at a dead run with her skirts hitched up in one hand, a bunch of roses in the other, and the cutter in her teeth.

He laughed so hard he had to put the box down on the bed.

Tor looked pleased and anxious at once, which made it worse. Rolph laughed until he had to hold onto the bedpost, and then until his eyes ran, and then, gradually, until he could breathe again, and wipe his face, and sit up.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's— Thank you. I needed that." He wiped his eyes again. "What else did she say?"

Tor's face fell a little. "That I might be an Austran spy."

Rolph stopped wiping his eyes.

"She didn't say I *was*," said Tor quickly. "She said she didn't know what I was. Because of the wards, and the fifty. And because all the nobles keep going into rages, she said, more and more of them, all year, and nobody knows why, and I was there. So she thought maybe I'd done it. To Tovey. With some sort of magic that makes nobles angry. To get Trice—" He swallowed. "To get Trice hurt. Or killed. She didn't say killed. But she meant it. And she wanted me in a cell. Master Kolb came, though," he added, brightening a little. "He laughed at her. He said I take direction well. I think it was the nicest thing he's ever said to me."

Rolph stood up.

He didn't decide to. He was standing, and the laughter had gone out of him entirely, as though it had never been there, and in its place was something very cold and very clear that he hadn't felt since he was fourteen.

"She said," he said, "that you tried to have Trice killed."

"Not *said*—"

"You. You walked into a battle rage with a bag of copper to get a girl you thought was nobody out from under a count, and that woman stood up in front of the headmaster and called you an *assassin*." He found he'd picked up his coat. "For that. For *that*."

"Rolph—"

"Get your boot on."

He went out. He didn't slam the door; he was past slamming doors. He walked out through it and down the corridor toward the stair, and heard behind him, after a moment, a frantic scuffling, and a thump, and Tor's voice saying "Wait—sorry—wait," and then the uneven sound of someone running down a corridor with one boot on and the other in his hand.

Tor caught him at the top of the stair. He was hopping, trying to get the second boot on and keep up at the same time, and Rolph slowed for him without looking round.

"Rolph," said Tor, breathless. "You know I didn't. Don't you? I didn't try to hurt anybody. I wouldn't. I didn't even know she was—"

"I know." Rolph stopped on the landing. He made himself unclench his hands. "Tor. I know. Of course I know."

Tor got the boot on, finally, and stood up, and looked at him.

"But listen," said Rolph. "Listen to me. The royal guard don't—" He chose it carefully. "They don't play by the same rules as the rest of us. They aren't *meant* to. They're there to keep one thing safe, and they don't care what it costs anyone else, and nobody's allowed to ask them to. If one of them's decided she's worried about you, then you be careful. You be very, very careful. Do you understand me?"

Tor looked at him for a long moment. Something in his face changed: not fear, quite. Attention.

"How do you know that?" he said.

"Everybody knows that," said Rolph. "Come on. They'll have eaten all the eggs."

He went on down the stairs, and after a moment, Tor followed him.
