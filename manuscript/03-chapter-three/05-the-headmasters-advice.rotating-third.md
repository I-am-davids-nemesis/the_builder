The headmaster took the thirty-one steps slowly, because his knees required it, and the boy took them slowly beside him, because the boy, it seemed, would not go up a stair ahead of anyone.

At the top, the back hall was full of ordinary morning. Someone had opened the shutters. The light came in long and pale across the boards, and there was a smell of porridge from somewhere and the sound, far off, of the kitchen boys arguing. The headmaster stood a moment in it and let his hands warm. He was always surprised, coming up out of that room, to find that the world had gone on without him. He supposed that was the point of the room.

He looked at the boy.

In the daylight Baker looked worse. The scratches had gone dark, and there was a bruise coming up along his jaw that hadn't been there in the firelight, and the leaf was still in his hair. He was standing very straight, hands clasped, with the air of someone waiting to be told which way the cells were.

"Shall we go out?" said the headmaster.

They went out by the side door, into the yard behind the administration building. The air was cold and clean and smelled of wet stone. Over the roofs the bell tower stood up against a sky going from gray to blue, and as they came out it rang: six strokes, slow and heavy, rolling out over the court and down the hill. Somewhere in the dormitories a window banged open. A student in brown went past the end of the yard at a run, with his tunic on backward, late for something. He didn't look at them.

The boy watched him go as if he were a bird of some rare kind.

"What do you generally do, Mr. Baker," said the headmaster, "at this hour of the morning?"

The boy turned. "Sir?"

"On an ordinary day. At the sixth bell."

"I—" Baker thought about it, visibly. "I'd be getting up, sir. And then breakfast. And then I'd go to the building master. He likes us early." He frowned. "Then meditation. Then theory. Then—"

"Then that," said the headmaster, "would seem a good thing to do today. Wouldn't it?"

The boy looked at him.

The headmaster watched him take it in. He watched him take it in, and turn it over, and come to the end of it and find nothing there that made sense. It was very like watching someone pick up a field they'd been told was finished and find no sigil on it.

"Sir," said Baker, carefully. "I don't— Mistress Wensa wanted me in a cell."

"Yes."

"She thinks I'm a spy. She thinks I— with the nobles getting angry, she thinks it's me somehow. That I did something to Count Thompson. That I'm trying to—" He stopped. "And I'm meant to go to breakfast?"

"Do spies," said the headmaster, "generally go to breakfast?"

"I don't know, sir. I've never met one." The boy's hands had come apart. He was counting on his fingers, the headmaster saw, quite unconsciously, the way a child counts sums. "And there's the shield, sir. Master Kolb's. That's due in twelve days. And it has to let air through, and I haven't worked out how yet. And I promised Rolph the food dryer, the one for fruit, and I've only got the design half done. And there's the rest of the drying fields for Miss Sara, the fifty on the desk, they're only etched. And classes. And she'll be *watching*, sir. She'll be watching all of it." He looked at his fingers, which had run out. "How am I meant to do all that normally?"

The headmaster considered him.

There were a great many things he might have said. He knew most of them already, and had known them for some time, longer than the boy had been at Lairdgren; and he said none of them. He said instead what he'd have said to any student, standing in the yard at the sixth bell with leaves in their hair and too much to do.

"One thing at a time, Mr. Baker," he said. "Isn't that how you build?"

The boy opened his mouth. Then he shut it, slowly. Something went across his face, the ghost of the calm the headmaster had seen in the warded room, and was gone.

"Yes, sir," he said.

"Breakfast first, I think. Then the building master." The headmaster turned to go back in. At the door he paused. "And Mr. Baker."

"Sir?"

"You have a leaf in your hair."

He went in. He didn't look back to see the boy find it. He'd seen a good deal this morning already, and he thought he'd better leave something for later.
