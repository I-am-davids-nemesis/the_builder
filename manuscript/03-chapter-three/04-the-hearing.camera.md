Tor sat on the edge of the chair by the fire with his hands clamped between his knees. His trousers were black with wet to the thigh. In the firelight, faint threads of vapor rose off his shoulders. There was a leaf in his hair.

Trice watched him from her armchair, the wet blue cloth against her cheek. Across the hearth, Wensa sat with her weight forward on the balls of her feet, one hand by her belt.

The door opened.

Campbell stepped aside from it. Kolb came in first, big and bald, the scar pale on his face, his glance going once around the room and the door and the corners. Behind him, ducking under the lintel, came Tovey.

He wore a clean coat. His hair was brushed and his brooch was pinned on straight. He was very pale. He stopped just inside the door, upright, his hands at his sides, and his eyes went round the room and found Trice and stayed on her.

"Patricia," he said.

"Good morning, Tovey."

"Are you hurt?" He took one step toward her and stopped. "Did I—"

Trice took the cloth away from her face. The right side of it was swollen out, purple going to yellow at the edges, the eye shut.

"Hurt *me*?" she said. "You? Honestly, Tovey, the conceit of the man." She put the cloth back. Water ran out of it and down her wrist. "You did knock the side of my head about a bit. I'll give you that. It's not my best side now. But Tor came along and gave you something else to think about, so that was all right." She considered. "It was closer than I'd like. I'll say that too."

Tovey looked at her face for a long moment. His jaw set. His throat moved. Nothing else in his face changed at all.

Then he turned, all of a piece, and faced Tor.

Tor shot up off the chair. He stood with his hands clasped in front of him and his eyes on the floor near Tovey's boots.

"Scholar Baker," said Tovey.

"Sir."

"I struck you. I—" Tovey's gaze was fixed on a point on the wall above Tor's head. "I have no memory of it. That is not an excuse. I know what I am when I'm like that. I know what I would have done. I hunted you, I'm told, the whole of the night." A muscle moved in his jaw. "I am more sorry than I can say."

Tor looked up. His face went blank with bewilderment; then something at the corner of his mouth twitched, and was gone.

"Oh," he said. "That's all right, sir. Really. What are friends for?"

He said it perfectly straight.

Nobody in the room moved. Tovey stood as if he'd been struck. The headmaster's eyebrows went up. Kolb folded his arms and looked at Tor. Wensa's mouth thinned.

Trice lowered the cloth an inch.

"There," she said. "You heard him, Tovey. You should thank your lucky stars we've friends like that. Because if you *had* killed me, I'd have haunted you. I'd have done it properly. Under the bed. In the wardrobe. Every night, for the rest of your life." She flapped the wet cloth at him. "Stop *looming*. Sit down. You're making the room cold."

"The room is always cold," said Tovey. But he sat, in the empty chair by the fire, carefully, and when he looked at Tor again it was steadily, and for a long time.

Tor sat down again on the edge of his chair.

Wensa stood up.

"Headmaster," she said. "I'm sorry to interrupt. But we seem to be forgetting what this boy did."

"I don't think anybody's forgotten, Wensa," said the headmaster mildly.

"Then let me say it so that it's said. Last night Scholar Baker assaulted a peer of the realm. He struck Count Thompson, repeatedly, from behind, with a weighted bag." She looked at the headmaster as she said it, and once at Tovey, and not at Tor. "There's a word for that. The word is treason."

Tor had gone white. He nodded slowly, twice, and said nothing.

"I don't remember Scholar Baker assaulting me," said Tovey.

Wensa turned to him.

"I remember," said Tovey slowly, "very little of last night at all. I'm told I was in a rage. I'm told I struck my cousin, who is half my size, and knocked her down, and stood over her. If that's what I was doing," and his hands closed on his knees, "then I'd thank anybody who came to her aid. I would have begged him to. I would have begged anyone."

"He tapped you on the shoulder," said Trice. "To get your attention. And then he ran away." She smiled at Wensa with half her face. "It was a very sensible tap."

"Several taps," said Tor, to his knees. "With the sack. I'm sorry. I should say."

"Hush, Tor."

"And the reason," said Wensa, "that the two of you were in the main court at the second bell of the morning?"

Trice looked at Tovey. Tovey looked at Trice.

"A family matter," said Trice.

"A private one," said Tovey. He went paler still.

"Cousin?" said Tor.

They all turned to him. He was looking from Trice to Tovey and back, slowly.

"You're cousins? I didn't know you were related."

"First cousins," said Trice. "His mother and my father. Close enough that nobody's mother has ever once suggested we marry, thank goodness." She frowned at him. "You didn't know? Then why on earth did you *help*?"

Tor opened his mouth, and closed it. He frowned at her, hard, as though she'd asked him why water ran downhill.

"You were losing," he said at last. "And you're my friend. And we're in business, you and Miss Sara and me. And you were—" He made a small helpless gesture toward her face. "I don't know what else I was meant to do."

Then he yawned: enormously, without warning, so that he had to hide it behind both hands.

"Sorry. Sorry. I finished the first fifty," he said through his fingers. "Of the drying fields. Last night. Before." He rubbed his eyes. "That's why I was out. I'd been in the workroom. I should have said."

Trice didn't answer. She sat quite still in the big chair, the cloth forgotten against her cheek, looking at him. After a moment the tips of her ears, under the dark hair, went pink.

"Fifty?" said Tovey.

He was staring at Tor.

"Fifty," he said again. "In one night. Yourself."

"Yes, sir. Sorry. It's only copying. I'd have done more, but they're boring, and I kept losing count."

"Scholar Baker," said Tovey, with great care, "my mother buys lamps for the county from the Arlen works. The *whole* works. Forty journeymen. She's told me they can't do a hundred in a day between them."

Tor nodded politely, the way a guest nods at his host's kindness.

"That," said Wensa, "is exactly my point."

She hadn't sat down. She stood by her chair with the firelight on one side of her face, and looked at Tor directly.

"Headmaster. This boy walked through the outer ward this morning as if it weren't there. He saw through the ward on the door. He saw through the wards on these chairs, which were laid by the same hands that laid the wards on the—" She stopped. "Which are very good wards. He is a scholarship boy from nowhere, who builds in a night what a manufactory builds in two days. And last night, when one more noble child in this realm fell into a rage, as they have been falling into rages all year, more and more often, with no one able to tell us why, he was there." She drew a breath. "An Austran agent could not have been better placed. I'm not saying he is one. I am saying I don't know what he is, and neither do you, and that I am not prepared to take the chance."

"Mistress Wensa," began the headmaster.

"I want him removed from the school. Today. Out of caution."

"On what authority?"

Wensa looked at him. Then her hand went to her belt, and came away holding a short, dark, cord-wrapped hilt a hand and a half long, with small regular marks around the guard.

Kolb's arms came unfolded.

"On mine," said Wensa. "I hold a commission in the royal guard. I have done for nineteen years." She held the hilt low, at her side. "I'll take him to the holding cells myself, and he can stay there until people in this room come to their senses."

Tor stood up at once.

"Yes, ma'am," he said. His face was white, and frightened, and relieved. "Of course. I'll come. I'm sorry for the trouble."

As he spoke, his feet moved. His left foot slid back half a pace and turned out. His knees softened. His weight settled over the balls of his feet, and his clasped hands came apart and rose an inch, open, loose, at the height of his belt. His eyes were on Wensa's hands, not her face. His mouth went on apologizing.

Wensa's hilt came up a quarter of an inch. Her weight shifted. Her face went quite still.

In the armchair, Trice took the cloth away from her cheek.

Kolb laughed.

It was loud and sudden and it went off the vaulted ceiling like a dropped pan. Everyone in the room jumped but Kolb.

"Oh, look at him," said Kolb, grinning, the scar pulling up the corner of his mouth. "Look at that. Feet. Hands. Watching the weapon. I taught him that, Mistress. Every bit of it. Three years I've been beating that into him and I didn't think any of it had gone in." He looked at Tor. "Stand easy, Baker. You'll frighten the lady."

Tor looked down at his own feet, startled. "Oh," he said. "Sorry."

"He takes direction, that's his trouble," Kolb told the room. "I tell him: Baker, you're small, if a big man comes for you, hit him once to make him look and then run. And what does he do? He hits him to make him look, and then he runs. All night. From a count in a rage. And here he is, not a mark on him that a week won't fix." He shook his head. "I've had captains who couldn't follow an order that well."

"Mistress Wensa," said Tovey quietly, from his chair. "Please."

He didn't stand, or raise his voice. He only looked at her.

"Do keep on, Wensa," said Trice comfortably. "Only I'd think about it. If you frighten him off now, he'll break his contract with Sara and me. And then he'll take his drying fields to the Sorveys, and the whole of the realm will be buying its laundry from Douglas Sorvey, and *then* where will any of us be?"

There was a pause.

Wensa looked at Trice; at Tovey; at Kolb, grinning by the door; at the headmaster, who had taken his spectacles down from his forehead and was polishing them on his sleeve. Last, she looked at Tor, standing before the fire with his hands clasped again and his feet carefully together, steaming.

The hilt went back to her belt.

"I would like it noted," she said, "that I objected."

"So noted," said the headmaster, putting his spectacles on. "Thank you, Wensa. Mr. Baker, will you walk with me?"
