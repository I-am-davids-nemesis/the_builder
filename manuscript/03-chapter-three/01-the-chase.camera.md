The fist came down out of the dark, and Tor was not under it.

He went left. It wasn't quick, and it wasn't graceful; his legs were stiff from the hip and one foot dragged. But he went, early, before the fist had properly started, and it passed his ear with a sound like a door swinging shut and moved his hair.

He bent and picked up the sack.

Then he turned and ran.

It wasn't running yet. His left leg swung out stiffly at every stride and his right did all the work. The sack banged against his knees. Behind him the big shape came on across the flagstones, slow and heavy, with sparks coming off its head and shoulders in a crackling stream that lit the stones blue and threw Tor's shadow out ahead of him, jumping.

He reached the corner of the long hall.

The blow caught him between the shoulders. He left the ground. He went up, and over, sprawling, past the corner of the hall and out over the lower lawn, and came down on grass, and rolled, and rolled again, and fetched up against the low wall of the kitchen garden, face down in a row of cabbages.

He lay still a moment. Then his fingers moved. His left leg drew up under him, and bent at the knee.

Across the lawn, the sparks turned slowly, as the big shape turned with them, searching.

The sack was still in Tor's hand.

He got up and ran properly.

He went over the kitchen garden wall and through a gap in the hedge, and down across the sheep field beyond. His breath went up white. His stride lengthened, and evened; his arms settled; he ran the way a runner runs on a long road, in for three and out for three. Behind him, the heavy steps began again, very fast.

He didn't look back.

The steps came on. Tor ran. Gradually the sound of them grew less: slower, further off. At the bottom of the field he went over a stile by the ditch without breaking stride.

Behind him, the footsteps stopped.

He ran a dozen strides. Then a dozen more. The field was quiet. There was the ditch water and his own breathing and, somewhere, a sheep.

He looked back.

In the middle of the sheep field Tovey stood quite still, a black shape against the gray grass, with sparks fizzing off him. His heels came up off the ground. His toes followed. He rose, without effort, without hurry, a foot, three feet, the height of a man, and hung there over the field with sparks dropping off him like rain off a roof, while the sheep ran away beneath him.

Then he leaned forward and came on, through the air, faster than he had run.

"Oh, *no*," said Tor, and fled.

There was a wood along the bottom of the valley: oak and holly and old dark beech. Tor reached its edge and went in, and the dark closed over him.

For a while there was only noise. Branches snapping. Holly tearing at cloth. A splash and a gasp, as he went into a stream he hadn't seen, to the knee. Scrabbling, on a bank. Then a long, frantic rustling, and his voice, low and desperate: "Please. Please, *please*." A tearing sound; a bramble letting go of a sack. A thump, and, "Sorry," to a tree.

Over the wood, the light moved.

It went back and forth above the canopy, blue-white, crackling, slow. Where it passed, the bare tops of the beeches stood out like the ribs of a roof, and went dark again. It stopped once directly over the place where the rustling had been.

Underneath, between two roots, Tor sat in the leaves with his arms wrapped around the sack and his face pressed into the moss on a trunk, and did not move.

The light went on.

It came back twice, a little further off each time. Tor stayed where he was. His teeth chattered once, and he clamped them shut.

Slowly, the sky through the branches went from black to dark gray.

A bird spoke. Another answered. Tor lifted his head.

High over the edge of the wood, in the gray of the morning, Tovey hung in the air. No sparks came off him now. His arms hung at his sides, and his head was bowed a little, like a man who has walked into a room and forgotten why. Then he raised his head, and looked down.

He dropped: a lurch, a slither, like a man missing the last step of a stair, and then the rest of the way at once. He landed beyond the last trees in the wet grass at the edge of the sheep field, on his hands and knees, and stayed there.

He stayed there a long time.

At last he got up. It took him two tries. He stood swaying, mud to the elbows, and turned all the way around, looking at the valley: the wood, the ditch, the sheep field, the school on its hill with the bell tower black against the dawn, a long way off. He stayed facing the school for some time.

Then he put his head down and walked toward it, slowly and stiffly, one boot after the other, up the sheep field toward the stile. A sheep watched him go. He didn't look back.

Tor let his breath out.

He sat in the leaves. His face was scratched in a dozen places, his trousers soaked to the thigh, one sleeve torn open from wrist to elbow. He began to shake, and went on shaking for some while.

Then he stopped.

"You hit him," he said.

A bird went off through the branches with a clatter.

"You hit a count." He held up one finger and looked at it. "With a sack. Several times. Tor. Striking a noble: that's one." A second finger. "A count. Not a younger son. A count. That's two." He looked at the two fingers for a long time and didn't hold up a third.

"They hang you for that," he said, very quietly. "Everybody knows that. Even at home they know that."

He sat with the two fingers up.

"Miss Trice hit him too," he said. "Before me. With the rod thing." His hand came down slowly. "And she's only with Miss Sara. She's not anybody either. They'd hang her first."

His hands had stopped shaking.

"No," he said, quite calmly. "No, that's all right. I did it. She was on the ground. I did it." He nodded, to himself, several times. "That's easy."

He shifted to get up, and the sack under his arm went *clank*.

Tor looked down at it.

He picked it up by the neck. He weighed it in his hand, a long time, as if he had never seen it before.

He began to laugh. It wasn't loud. It went on, helplessly, for some while, and he wiped his face on his good sleeve.

"You could have *dropped* it," he said. "You could have dropped it at any time. Honestly." He looked up at the branches, where the light had been. "I argued with a *bramble*."

Then he stopped laughing, and stood up, carefully, and put the sack over his shoulder.

"Right," he said. "Rolph's ten. Then the rest, for home. Then—" He thought. "Then whoever it is you go to. The headmaster. And say it was me."

He nodded once more. Then he set off up through the wood toward the school, limping, with the birds getting louder all around him, and the sack clanking at every step.
