The headmaster climbed the thirty-one steps slowly, one hand on the wall. Tor climbed beside him, a step behind, and did not pass him.

At the top, the back hall of the administration building was full of morning. Someone had opened the shutters; pale light lay in long bars across the boards. There was a smell of porridge, and the far-off sound of kitchen boys arguing. The headmaster stood in the light a moment, flexing his fingers.

Tor stood beside him with his hands clasped. In daylight the scratches on his face had darkened, and a bruise was coming up along his jaw. The leaf was still in his hair.

"Shall we go out?" said the headmaster.

They went out by the side door into the yard behind the building. The air was cold and smelled of wet stone. Over the roofs, the bell tower stood against a sky going from gray to blue, and as they came out it rang six slow, heavy strokes that rolled out over the court and down the hill. A window banged open somewhere in the dormitories. A student in brown ran past the end of the yard with his tunic on backward, and didn't look at them.

Tor watched him go.

"What do you generally do, Mr. Baker," said the headmaster, "at this hour of the morning?"

Tor turned. "Sir?"

"On an ordinary day. At the sixth bell."

"I—" Tor's brow creased. "I'd be getting up, sir. And then breakfast. And then I'd go to the building master. He likes us early. Then meditation. Then theory. Then—"

"Then that," said the headmaster, "would seem a good thing to do today. Wouldn't it?"

Tor looked at him for some time.

"Sir," he said carefully. "I don't— Mistress Wensa wanted me in a cell."

"Yes."

"She thinks I'm a spy. She thinks I— with the nobles getting angry, she thinks it's me somehow. That I did something to Count Thompson. That I'm trying to—" He stopped. "And I'm meant to go to breakfast?"

"Do spies," said the headmaster, "generally go to breakfast?"

"I don't know, sir. I've never met one." Tor's hands had come apart. He was counting on his fingers. "And there's the shield, sir. Master Kolb's. That's due in twelve days. And it has to let air through, and I haven't worked out how yet. And I promised Rolph the food dryer, the one for fruit, and I've only got the design half done. And there's the rest of the drying fields for Miss Sara, the fifty on the desk, they're only etched. And classes. And she'll be *watching*, sir. She'll be watching all of it." He looked down at his fingers, which had run out. "How am I meant to do all that normally?"

The headmaster looked at him for a long moment. He drew a breath, as if to say something, and let it out again.

"One thing at a time, Mr. Baker," he said. "Isn't that how you build?"

Tor opened his mouth, and shut it slowly. For an instant his face went smooth and still, as it had in the warded room; then it was only tired.

"Yes, sir," he said.

"Breakfast first, I think. Then the building master." The headmaster turned back toward the door, and paused with his hand on it. "And Mr. Baker."

"Sir?"

"You have a leaf in your hair."

He went in, and the door closed.

Tor stood alone in the yard. Slowly he put up a hand, felt about in his hair, and found the leaf. He held it out and looked at it: a beech leaf, brown, wet, perfectly whole.

He put it carefully in his pocket, and went to find Rolph.
