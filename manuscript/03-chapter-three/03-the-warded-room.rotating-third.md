The stair down from the administration building's back hall had thirty-one steps, and Campbell counted them, because he always did. At the bottom there was a lamp in a niche, already lit, and beyond the lamp the corridor began.

He didn't look at it. There was no point. Twenty-two years at Lairdgren, and he'd been down here perhaps forty times, and he had never once managed to look at it properly. You came off the bottom step and your eyes simply slid. They went to the lamp, or to your own boots, or back up the stair behind you, and if you made them go forward they went forward and came back with nothing: a wall, and more wall, and a floor that was a floor, and the thing you'd meant to think about was gone again, and you were thinking about breakfast. The cold was the only part of it that stayed. It came up through the soles of his boots and lay along the backs of his hands, the dead, still cold of a cellar in deep winter, though it was barely autumn outside.

So Campbell did what he always did. He kept his eyes on his boots and counted flagstones. Seventeen from the bottom step, on the left: that was the door. He'd never seen it either. He'd been told the number, his first year, and had written it down, and had been very glad since that he had.

"Come along, Scholar."

The boy came along. Campbell had a hand on his arm still, more from habit than need; Baker hadn't pulled against it once, all the way across the court and through the back hall. He'd said "sorry" four times. Campbell had stopped counting those.

Three flagstones. Four. The boy's steps beside him had begun to drag.

Six. Seven.

The boy stopped.

Campbell turned to tell him to come along, and looked at him, and didn't.

Baker was standing quite still in the middle of the corridor with his eyes half closed. He was frowning: not frightened, not confused, but the frown of somebody trying to read small writing in bad light. His lips were moving. Campbell bent his head to hear.

"Focus," the boy was saying, under his breath. "Focus now. There's something. There. Think, Tor."

Then he let his breath out, a long slow breath, and the frown smoothed away, and his eyes opened all the way, and Campbell found that he had taken a step back.

He couldn't have said why. Nothing about the boy had changed. He was the same small, filthy, scratched scholarship student who'd just made his will in the dormitory, with a leaf in his hair. But he was standing differently. His shoulders had come down from around his ears. His hands, which had been knotted together in front of him all the way across the court, hung loose at his sides. And he was looking down the corridor, straight down it, steadily, the way nobody ever looked down this corridor, as if it were simply a corridor and he was simply looking.

"There's a door," Baker said.

He didn't say *sir*. Campbell noticed that. He noticed it a long time after, too, lying awake.

"There," said the boy. "On the left. And another one further down, on the right, and there's something at the end, but I can't—" He narrowed his eyes. "That one's harder." He turned his head, and looked straight at a patch of wall ten flagstones off, where Campbell had never seen anything in twenty-two years, and nodded. "That one's ours. Isn't it. It's warm on the other side of that one. Well. It's lighter."

Campbell opened his mouth, and found he had nothing to put in it.

He knew what the corridor was. He didn't know how it was done, and had never been told, and had never asked, but he knew the principle, as every senior member of the staff knew it: there were wards on this place, very old ones and very good ones, laid by people who had known exactly what they were doing, and they turned the mind aside. Not the eyes. The mind. You could look all you liked. You just couldn't *keep* anything. That was the point of it. Nobody got through it who hadn't been let through.

He looked at the boy, standing loose and easy in a corridor that had been built to turn away a king's spies.

Then he looked at the patch of wall where the boy was looking, and tried, and his eyes went to the lamp.

Well.

It was, Campbell decided, above his pay. Very much above it. He was the dean of students. He did discipline, and dormitories, and letters to parents, and he had been woken in the middle of the night and told to fetch a boy, and he had fetched the boy, and what the boy could or couldn't see was a matter for people with larger offices than his. He took his hand off Baker's arm. He reached into his coat, found the cord around his neck, and drew out the token: a small disc of dark metal, warm from his skin, with a pattern on its face like frost.

"Come along," he said again. It came out rougher than he'd meant.

He walked, and the boy walked with him. Eleven flagstones. Fourteen. Seventeen. Campbell put out his left hand, flat, to where he knew the plate was, and touched cold metal, and pressed the token against it.

There was a sound like a held breath let out.

And there was the door. Just like that, for the space of a breath: an ordinary door, oak, iron-studded, with a ring for a handle, and a little brass plate beside it at shoulder height, as plain and present as a door in any corridor in the world. Campbell took hold of the ring before it could go, and turned it, and pushed.

Heat came out to meet them, or the look of it.

The room beyond was not large. It had a low vaulted ceiling, whitewashed, and a stone floor with a worn rug, and on the far wall a great fireplace in which a fire was roaring: a real fire, a big one, logs the size of a man's leg banked high and blazing, throwing orange light all over the walls. Campbell felt the light of it on his face. He felt nothing else. The cold in the room was the same cold as the corridor's, the same dead stillness laid along his hands; his breath came out in smoke in front of the fire. He'd never got used to it. He didn't expect to.

The door swung shut behind them. Campbell put the token away.

There were chairs. He knew there were chairs; there were always chairs. Two on the near side of the fire, empty, with their backs to the door. And on the other side, the left side, the side he never could look at, there were—

He looked at the fire instead. It was easier.

Beside him, Baker had gone still again.

Campbell glanced down. The boy's eyes were half shut. His lips were moving, and the frown had come back, deeper this time, the frown of a man working hard at something heavy. He was looking at the left side of the room. He was looking at it steadily, unhurried, and as Campbell watched, the frown went, and the boy's face went smooth, and then, of all things, he smiled: a small, startled, pleased smile, the smile of somebody who has walked into a room full of strangers and seen a face he knows.

"Miss Trice," said Baker. "You're all right."

Something on the left side of the room made a noise. It might have been a laugh. It sounded like it hurt.

"And sir. Good morning." The boy inclined his head, quite properly, to a place by the left side of the fire where Campbell could see nothing but firelight. "And—"

He stopped. The pleased look went out of his face, and something else came into it: not fear, Campbell thought, but attention. Cool attention. The look of a man who has found something on a shelf that shouldn't be there.

"Mistress Wensa?" said Baker. "From accounts?"

There was a silence in the room.

Then a chair creaked, on the side Campbell couldn't see, as if someone had sat forward in it very suddenly.

"Oh, let it go," said the headmaster's voice, from beside the fire. He sounded tired, and a little amused. "There's no point now, is there?"

Something clicked, small and metallic, like a snuffbox shutting.

And there they were, all at once, as if they had been there all along, which of course they had. Three chairs on the left side of the fire, drawn up in a half circle. In the nearest, the headmaster, gray and spare in his long gray gown, with his spectacles pushed up onto his forehead and his hand just leaving the brass stud set into the arm of his chair. Beyond him, curled sideways in a big leather chair with her boots tucked up under her, the girl from the special school, the one they called Trice, still in her black: the whole right side of her face swollen out and gone purple and yellow, the eye on that side shut, and a bundle of soft pale blue cloth held against her cheek. She lifted her free hand a little, at Baker. It was very nearly a wave.

And in the third chair, upright, with both feet flat on the floor and both hands on her knees, a neat, gray-streaked, sharp-faced woman in a plain dark dress, with ink on the second finger of her right hand: Mistress Wensa, who taught accounts to the third years, and who had no business at all, so far as Campbell had ever known, being in this room at this hour.

She was looking at Baker. She wasn't looking at him the way the others were. She had one hand off her knee, and it had gone to the side of her belt, and stayed there.

Baker blinked.

He blinked again, and gave a little shake of the head, like a swimmer coming up, and Campbell watched it happen: the shoulders creeping back up toward the ears, the hands finding each other in front of him, the whole of him somehow getting smaller. He looked around the room as if seeing it for the first time, the fire, the vault, the chairs, the people in them, and his face went from calm to anxious to appalled in the space of a breath.

"Oh," he said. "Sorry. I'm sorry. I didn't— Was I meant not to see? I'm sorry, sir. Ma'am. I didn't mean to look."

"Sit down, Mr. Baker," said the headmaster. "No, not there. By the fire. You look frozen."

The boy sat, on the very edge of the chair, with his hands between his knees. Campbell stayed by the door, where he belonged.

On the far side of the fire, Mistress Wensa had not taken her hand from her belt.
