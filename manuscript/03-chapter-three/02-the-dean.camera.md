The fourth door on the top corridor of the dormitory stood shut. Behind it, in the gray of very early morning, Rolph sat on the edge of his bed with a blanket around his shoulders and his bare feet on the boards.

A man stood at the window with his back to the room. He was narrow and gray-jawed, and dressed in black from his collar to his boots. His hands were clasped behind him. One button of his coat was done up in the wrong hole.

On the desk, fifty bare copper squares lay in rows of ten.

The other bed had not been slept in.

"Sir," said Rolph. "Dean Campbell. Sir. If you could tell me only whether—"

"You'll wait, Scholar."

Rolph waited. He looked at the dean's back. He looked at the button. He looked away from the button.

The tower bell rang. Neither of them counted it aloud.

The bar in the door lifted by itself with a small wooden knock. The door opened, and Tor came in sideways, because of the sack on his shoulder.

Rolph stood up. The blanket fell off him.

Tor was soaked to the thighs, his trousers black with wet and mud. One sleeve of his tunic hung open from wrist to elbow, and the arm inside was scratched in long red lines, and there were more scratches on his face. A leaf was caught in his hair. He limped. His face was gray, and quite calm.

He saw Campbell, and stopped.

"Oh," said Tor. "Good morning, sir. I'm sorry. I was coming to you. I only had to—" He looked at Rolph. "I won't be a minute. I'm sorry. May I?"

Campbell said nothing.

Tor set down the sack between the beds. It clanked. He crouched, undid the neck, and took out ten copper squares, tied in a stack with a strip of rag. He stood and put them into Rolph's hands.

"These are your mother's," he said. "They're the good ones. I did them first, before I got tired. You paid fifty-five, so if you send the other fifty-five to my mother, that's square. Only you needn't if you'd rather not, honestly. I know it was the friend price."

"Tor—"

"The other forty are in the sack." He spoke quickly and evenly, as though reading from a list. "They all work. I tested every tenth one. Miss Sara said twelve, so that's four hundred and eighty, which is a lot. If you'd please sell them to her and send it home. To my mother. Baker, at the bakery, Two Rivers. Anyone there will know." He frowned. "Not the ones on the desk. Those aren't done. They're only copper. Don't let her pay for those."

Rolph looked down at the squares in his hands, and the strip of rag. Its edge was torn and grimy, the same brown as Tor's shirt.

"Tor," he said. "What *happened*?"

"Scholar Baker," said Campbell.

"Yes, sir. Sorry. Nearly done." Tor turned back to Rolph. "Thank you for everything. Really. You were very kind to me. I'm sorry about the mess."

"What mess? Tor, *what*—"

Campbell crossed the room in three strides, took Tor by the upper arm, and walked him out through the door. Tor went without resisting.

"Sorry, sir," he said, on the threshold.

The door shut. The bar dropped into its bracket.

Rolph stood in the middle of the room in his nightshirt, holding the ten squares. He looked at the door, and at the sack sagging open on the floor, mud-slick copper showing inside it.

"No," he said, to the empty room. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a much older man. "No. I don't think so."
