The fist came down out of the dark, and Tor wasn't there.

He couldn't have said how. His legs had stopped being legs some time ago and become two posts he was balanced on, and posts don't step aside. But Kolb had spent three years throwing things at his head, and something lower down than thinking had learned the lesson whether Tor liked it or not. He went left. He always went left. The blow went past his ear with a sound like a door swinging shut, and the wind of it moved his hair, and he was already bending.

He picked up the sack.

He didn't decide to. It was there by his feet, it was Rolph's, it was ten for Rolph's mother and forty more at twelve gold each, and his hands had closed on the neck of it before the rest of him had finished being frightened. Then he turned around and ran.

*Run,* Kolb said, in his head, as Kolb always did. *You're small. Be gone.*

It wasn't running. Not at first. It was the cart with the wheel off again: his left leg swinging stiff from the hip, his right doing all the work, the sack banging against his knees with every lurch. The cold in him was worst at the back of his neck, as though somebody were standing very close behind him, breathing ice. Somebody was. He could hear it: the big, heavy, uneven steps on the flagstones, and over them a fizz and crackle like fat in a pan, and the light from it throwing Tor's own shadow out ahead of him across the court, jumping, blue.

He got as far as the corner of the long hall.

Then something hit him in the back, between the shoulders, very hard, and the ground went away.

He was in the air. That was clear enough. He saw the bell tower go by at an angle he'd never seen it from, and the lamp at the gate lodge, and a lot of sky. He thought, quite distinctly, *oh, that's what the pell feels like*, and then he came down on grass, and rolled, and rolled again, and stopped against something that turned out to be the low wall around the kitchen garden, and lay there with his face in a row of cabbages.

He was alive. He checked. He moved his fingers and they moved. He moved his left leg and it moved too, more than it had in a while.

He was a long way from Tovey.

That was the thing he understood next, lying in the cabbages. The blow had thrown him clear across the lower lawn. The cold at the back of his neck had thinned, the way a draft thins when someone shuts a door. Across the grass, in the dark, the sparks were turning, slowly, as the big shape turned with them, looking for where he'd gone.

The sack was still in his hand.

Tor got up and ran properly.

This part he knew. He'd done it twice this week already, down the hill and along the Hollin road and back, with Kolb's voice in the back of his head and the carter laughing at him from her wagon, and a hundred times before that. He went over the kitchen garden wall and through the gap in the hedge where the scholarship boys went out at night to buy pies, and down across the sheep field. The grass was soaking. His breath came up white in front of him. Behind him, after a moment, the steps began again: huge, heavy, very fast.

He didn't look back. Kolb said not to look back. Looking back was how you fell over.

The steps came on. Tor ran. The sack swung. The cold came and went at his neck, came and went, and then, after a while, began, very slightly, to fade. His left knee bent. It bent again. With every stride he got a little more of himself back: an ankle, a hip, the feeling in the fingers clenched on the neck of the sack. That meant distance. He knew that without working it out. He lengthened his stride and settled his breathing, in for three, out for three, the way he did on the road, and the heavy steps behind him grew slower, and further off, and he crossed the bottom of the sheep field and went over the stile by the ditch with something that was almost, nearly, a feeling of competence.

Behind him, the footsteps stopped.

Tor ran on a dozen strides. Then a dozen more. It was very quiet. There was the ditch water, and his own breathing, and a sheep somewhere complaining about him.

He looked back.

Tovey was standing in the middle of the sheep field, a black shape against the paler gray of the grass, with the sparks coming off his head and shoulders in a slow fizzing stream. He was standing quite still. Then his heels came up off the ground. Then his toes did. He rose, without hurry and without any effort Tor could see, as if he were being lifted on a string, a foot, three feet, the height of a man, until he hung there over the sheep field with the sparks falling off him like rain off a roof, and the sheep running away underneath.

Then he leaned forward and came on, through the air, faster than he had run.

"Oh, *no*," said Tor, and fled.

He ran the way he had never run on the Hollin road. The wood was ahead, past the ditch, along the bottom of the valley: Lairdgren's wood, the one the second-years told stories about, oak and holly and old dark beech. He got to the first trees with the cold coming back up his spine like water rising in a well, and went in, and the light went out.

After that it was branches. Branches in his face and roots under his feet and holly that went straight through his sleeve. A stream he didn't see until he was in it to the knee. A bank he went up on his hands. The sack caught on a bramble and wouldn't come, and he stood there tugging it in the pitch dark with a giant somewhere overhead and said, "Please, please, *please*," to a bramble, until it gave. He ran into a tree and apologized to it. He went on.

Overhead, there was light.

It came and went through the leaves: blue-white, crackling, moving slowly above the canopy, back and forth. Looking. Where it passed, the bare tops of the beeches stood out for a moment like the ribs of a roof, and then went black again. Once it stopped right above him, and the cold came down through the branches and into his shoulders so hard that he sat down without meaning to, in the leaves, between two roots, and stayed there with his arms around the sack and his face pressed into the moss of a trunk, and didn't breathe.

The light moved on.

He didn't move for a long time after that. He didn't know how long. The bell at the school was too far off to hear down here, or he was too frightened to count it. The light came back twice more, and went away twice more, a little further off each time. The cold came and went. His legs began to feel like legs again, and then like legs that had been sitting on wet ground for a very long time.

The sky through the branches went from black to dark gray.

Somewhere close, a bird said something. Another answered. Tor lifted his head.

He could see the trunk in front of him now, and the moss, and his own hands on the sack, filthy and scratched. He could see, between the branches, a long way up, the gray of the coming morning, and in it, high over the edge of the wood, a black shape that wasn't throwing off any sparks at all.

Tovey hung there in the air. He wasn't searching. He was quite still, with his arms at his sides and his head bowed a little, the way a man stands when he's walked into a room and can't remember why. Then he lifted his head. He turned slowly, all the way around, as if seeing the valley for the first time: the wood, the ditch, the sheep field, the school up on its hill with the tower black against the dawn. He stayed facing the school for some time.

Then he went toward it, not fast, low over the fields, and was gone behind the trees.

Tor let out his breath.

He sat in the leaves and listened to the birds. The cold had gone completely. He could feel everything: every scratch on his face, every bruise on his back, the place on his hip where he'd landed in the cabbages, the wet all the way up to his knees. He started to shake, and let himself, for a while, since there was nobody to see.

Then he stopped.

"You hit him," he said.

It came out loud in the quiet of the wood, and a bird went off with a clatter. Tor didn't notice. He was looking at his hands.

"You hit a count," he said. "With a sack. Several times. Tor." He held up a finger, and looked at it, as if it might explain itself. "Striking a noble. That's one." Another finger. "A count. Not a third son. A count, with a county. That's two." He didn't hold up a third. He didn't need to. Everybody knew the third one. Even in Two Rivers, where the nearest noble was four days off and nobody had ever seen one, they knew it. You didn't strike a noble. Not ever. Not for anything. Because if you did, they could hang you for it, and they did.

He sat there with his two fingers up.

Then he thought of Trice.

She'd been on her knees on the flagstones with half her face gone dark, holding that rod up at a man twice her size. She'd hit him with it. Tor had seen her do it. She'd been hitting him with it for some time before Tor ever came out of the east stair door. And Tor knew, he'd heard, he knew, that she wasn't from anywhere very much, because she was always with Sara, and Sara was a merchant's daughter, and girls who went to the special school in black were there because somebody had paid for them to learn to look after themselves. If they could hang a baker's son for it, they could hang a girl from nowhere much. They could hang her first.

His hands had stopped shaking.

"No," he said, quite calmly. "No, that's all right. That's easy. I did it. She didn't do anything. She was on the ground. I did it."

He nodded, to himself, several times.

Then he moved, to get up, and something under his arm went *clank*.

Tor looked down.

The sack. The sack was still there. He'd carried it out of the court and across the lawn, he'd had it in the cabbages, he'd taken it over the wall and through the hedge and down the sheep field and over the stile, he'd had it in the stream and on the bank and he'd *argued with a bramble* over it, with a raging count flying over his head. Fifty copper squares. Five inches on a side. He picked it up and felt the weight of it. It was very heavy. It had been heavy all night.

He began, helplessly, to laugh.

"You could have *dropped* it," he told himself. "You could have dropped it at any time. Honestly. Tor."

But he hadn't, and now it was here, and it was ten for Rolph's mother, and the express went when it went. He stopped laughing. He stood up, carefully, in the leaves, and put the sack over his shoulder, and stood a moment working it out, the way he worked out a field: what came first, what had to be finished before the next thing could start. Rolph's ten. Then the rest, for home. Then the porters' lodge, or the headmaster, or whoever it was you went to, to say you'd done a hanging thing and would they please not trouble anyone else about it.

That was all right. That was clear. It had an order to it.

He went back up through the wood toward the school, limping, in the gray light, with the birds getting louder all around him, and the sack clanking at every step.
