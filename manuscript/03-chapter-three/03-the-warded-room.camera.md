A stair went down from the back hall of the administration building, thirty-one steps of worn stone. At the bottom, a lamp burned in a niche.

Beyond the lamp there was a corridor. It was cold. It had walls, and more wall, and a floor that was a floor.

Campbell came off the bottom step with his hand on Tor's arm and his eyes on his own boots. His lips moved as he walked. "One," he said, under his breath. "Two. Three."

Tor came along beside him. His steps were dragging. He looked up the corridor, and his eyes came back down to the lamp; he looked again, and they went to the floor. He shivered. Their breath went up white.

"Five," said Campbell. "Six. Seven."

Tor stopped.

Campbell turned to him. He opened his mouth, and shut it again.

The boy was standing still in the middle of the corridor with his eyes half closed, frowning, the way a man frowns at small writing in bad light. His lips were moving.

"Focus," he was saying. "Focus now. There's something. There. Think, Tor."

He let out a long, slow breath. The frown smoothed away. His eyes opened fully.

His shoulders, which had been up around his ears since the dormitory, came down. His hands, knotted in front of him, came apart and hung loose at his sides. He looked straight down the corridor, steadily, and the corridor was there: long, stone-flagged, lamplit at the near end, with doors set into its walls at intervals, oak, iron-studded, dark with age.

Campbell took a step back from him.

"There's a door," said Tor.

He didn't say *sir*.

"There, on the left. And another further down, on the right, and there's something at the end, but I can't—" He narrowed his eyes. "That one's harder." He turned his head and looked at the third door on the left, ten flagstones off, and nodded. "That one's ours. Isn't it. It's warm on the other side of that one. Well. It's lighter."

Campbell looked at the boy, and at the place the boy was looking. His eyes slid off it to the lamp.

He stood a moment. Then he took his hand off Tor's arm, reached inside his coat, and drew out a cord from around his neck. On the end of it hung a small disc of dark metal, with a pattern on its face like frost.

"Come along," he said. His voice was rough.

He walked on, counting under his breath, and Tor walked beside him, looking at the door the whole way. At seventeen, Campbell put out his left hand flat against what looked like wall. His palm met a small brass plate set into the stone at shoulder height. He pressed the disc to it.

There was a sound like a held breath let out.

Campbell took the iron ring of the door in his hand at once, turned it, and pushed.

The room beyond had a low vaulted ceiling, whitewashed, and a worn rug on a stone floor, and on the far wall a great fireplace with a fire roaring in it, logs as thick as a man's leg banked high and blazing. Orange light moved over the walls. In front of the fire, Campbell's breath came out in smoke.

The door swung shut. Campbell put the disc away and stayed beside the door.

There were two chairs on the near side of the fire, empty, their backs to the door. To the left of the fire there was firelight, and a wall, and firelight.

Tor had gone still again. His eyes were half shut. He was looking at the left of the room, and frowning, harder this time, his lips moving. Then the frown went, and his face smoothed, and he smiled: small, startled, pleased.

"Miss Trice," he said. "You're all right."

To the left of the fire, there was a noise. It might have been a laugh. It sounded like it hurt.

"And sir. Good morning." Tor inclined his head politely toward the firelight on the left. "And—"

He stopped. The pleased look left his face. What came into it wasn't fear. He looked at a spot just to the left of the fire with cool attention, the way a man looks at something on a shelf that shouldn't be there.

"Mistress Wensa?" said Tor. "From accounts?"

Silence.

A chair creaked, to the left of the fire, as if someone had sat forward in it very suddenly.

"Oh, let it go," said a man's voice, tired, a little amused. "There's no point now, is there?"

Something clicked, small and metallic, like a snuffbox shutting.

There were three chairs to the left of the fire, drawn up in a half circle.

In the nearest sat an old man, gray and spare, in a long gray gown, with spectacles pushed up onto his forehead. His hand was just leaving a brass stud set into the arm of his chair. In the next, curled sideways in a big leather armchair with her boots tucked under her, sat Trice, still in black. The right side of her face was swollen out and gone purple and yellow, the eye on that side shut. She held a bundle of soft, pale blue cloth against her cheek. She lifted her free hand an inch toward Tor.

In the third chair a woman sat upright, feet flat on the floor, both hands on her knees. She was neat and sharp-faced, with gray in her hair, in a plain dark dress; the second finger of her right hand was stained with ink. She was looking at Tor. As Campbell watched, one of her hands left her knee and went to the side of her belt, and stayed there.

Tor blinked.

He blinked again, and gave a small shake of his head, like a swimmer coming up. His shoulders crept back up. His hands found each other in front of him. He looked around at the fire, the vault, the three chairs and the people in them, and his face went from calm to anxious to appalled.

"Oh," he said. "Sorry. I'm sorry. I didn't— Was I meant not to see? I'm sorry, sir. Ma'am. I didn't mean to look."

"Sit down, Mr. Baker," said the headmaster. "No, not there. By the fire. You look frozen."

Tor sat on the very edge of the nearer chair, his hands between his knees.

By the door, Campbell stood with his own hands behind him.

On the far side of the fire, Wensa's hand stayed at her belt.
