He looked dreadful. Trice made a note of it, the way she noted everything, and then found that she wasn't filing it anywhere, only looking.

He was sitting on the very edge of the chair with his hands clamped between his knees, as if the chair might object to being sat on. Wet to the thighs. Scratched to ribbons down one arm. A leaf in his hair, which nobody had told him about and which she wasn't going to. And steaming, very faintly, in the firelight, as the warmth that wasn't warmth got at him: little threads of vapor going up off his shoulders like a loaf just out of the oven.

He'd looked straight at her. Through the wards on the chairs, which she'd been told nobody could see through, and which she'd been sitting behind for an hour feeling invisible and rather pleased about it. He'd looked at her, and smiled, and said *you're all right*, as though that were the first thing anybody would want to know.

She put that in the account too. She wasn't sure which column.

Across the hearth, Wensa sat like a drawn bow. Trice had been watching her since she arrived, with what attention she could spare from the throbbing in her face, and she had come to some conclusions. One was that accounts mistresses did not, in Trice's experience, sit with their weight forward on the balls of their feet. Another was that they didn't keep a hand by the belt for a full hour, in a warded room, with no one in it but the headmaster and a girl with a broken face. A third, which she had come to only in the last few minutes, while Tor was walking through the wards as if they were curtains, was that whatever Wensa was, she was now very frightened, and very good at not showing it, and both of those were worth knowing.

The door opened.

Campbell stood aside from it at once. Kolb came in first, which was like Kolb: big and bald and unhurried, his scar pale in the firelight, glancing round the room the way he glanced round the yard, counting exits. Then, ducking under the lintel, Tovey.

He'd changed his coat. That was the first thing Trice noticed, and it told her more than anything else could have. Somebody, at some point in the last hour, had found Tovey somewhere (on a roof, she'd heard; on the roof of the chapel, sitting with his arms around his knees), and talked him down, and taken him to his rooms, and he had *changed his coat*, and brushed his hair, and pinned his brooch on straight, before coming here to be looked at. He was very pale. He stood just inside the door as if he'd been placed there, perfectly upright, with his hands at his sides, and his eyes went round the room and found her and stopped.

"Patricia," he said.

"Good morning, Tovey."

"Are you hurt?" He took one step toward her and then didn't take another, as though he didn't trust what the next step might do. "Did I—"

Trice took the cloth away from her face and looked at him with the eye she still had.

"Hurt *me*?" she said. "You? Honestly, Tovey, the conceit of the man." She put the cloth back. The ice inside it had mostly gone to water and was running down her wrist. "You did knock the side of my head about a bit. I'll give you that. It's not my best side now. But Tor came along and gave you something else to think about, so that was all right." She considered. "It was closer than I'd like. I'll say that too."

Tovey looked at her face. She watched him look. She saw the exact moment at which he took in all of it, the swelling and the shut eye and the colors, and saw his jaw set, and his throat move, and nothing whatever happen anywhere else in his face. It was a remarkable thing to watch. She'd been watching him do it since they were six.

Then he turned, stiffly, all of a piece, and faced Tor.

Tor got up. Of course he did. He shot up off the edge of the chair as if it had bitten him, and stood there with his hands clasped in front of him and his eyes on the floor somewhere around Tovey's boots, and every line of him said that he had been waiting for this since dawn and was ready, and was sorry, and would go quietly.

"Scholar Baker," said Tovey.

"Sir."

"I struck you. I—" Tovey stopped. He was looking, Trice saw, at a point just above Tor's head, as though the rest of his sentence were written on the wall there and he was having trouble reading it. "I have no memory of it. That is not an excuse. I know what I am when I'm like that. I know what I would have done. I hunted you, I'm told, the whole of the night." The muscle in his jaw moved again. "I am more sorry than I can say."

Tor looked up.

Trice saw his face, and saw the thing that went across it: bewilderment, and something underneath the bewilderment that was nearly, very nearly, a laugh. It was the look of a man who has come to the gallows with his speech prepared and been offered a cup of tea instead.

"Oh," said Tor. "That's all right, sir. Really. What are friends for?"

He said it perfectly straight. He said it so straight that it took Trice a moment to hear the joke in it, and by then she'd seen that nobody else had heard it at all.

The room had gone still.

Tovey stood as if struck. Beside the fire the headmaster's eyebrows had climbed into his hair. Kolb, by the door, had folded his arms, and was looking at Tor with an expression Trice couldn't read. And Wensa's mouth had gone very thin.

It was there, on the table, in front of everyone. A count of the realm had apologized, and a commoner had answered him as a friend, in front of the headmaster of Lairdgren and witnesses, and Tovey hadn't contradicted him.

Trice thought about it for perhaps half a breath. Then she took it, before anyone else could.

"There," she said. "You heard him, Tovey. You should thank your lucky stars we've friends like that. Because if you *had* killed me, I'd have haunted you. I'd have done it properly. Under the bed. In the wardrobe. Every night, for the rest of your life." She flapped the wet blue cloth at him. "Stop *looming*. Sit down. You're making the room cold."

"The room is always cold," said Tovey. But he sat, in the empty chair by the fire, very carefully, as though he might break it. And when he looked at Tor again, Trice saw that something had shifted in him, the way a weight shifts in a cart. He looked at Tor the way he looked at Trice: as somebody who was his.

Tor, who had noticed none of this, sat down again on the edge of his chair.

Wensa stood up.

"Headmaster," she said. Her voice was quite level. "I'm sorry to interrupt. But we seem to be forgetting what this boy did."

"I don't think anybody's forgotten, Wensa," said the headmaster mildly.

"Then let me say it so that it's said. Last night Scholar Baker assaulted a peer of the realm. He struck Count Thompson, repeatedly, from behind, with a weighted bag." She didn't look at Tor while she said it. She looked at the headmaster, and once at Tovey. "There's a word for that. The word is treason."

Tor had gone quite white. He didn't say anything. He just nodded, slowly, as though she had read out a sum he'd already done himself, and got the same answer.

"I don't remember Scholar Baker assaulting me," said Tovey.

Wensa looked at him.

"I remember," said Tovey, slowly, picking his way, "very little of last night at all. I'm told I was in a rage. I'm told I struck my cousin, who is half my size, and knocked her down, and stood over her. If that's what I was doing," and his voice didn't change at all, but his hands, on his knees, closed, "then I'd thank anybody who came to her aid. I would have begged him to. I would have begged anyone."

"He tapped you on the shoulder," said Trice. "To get your attention. And then he ran away." She smiled at Wensa with the half of her face that worked. "It was a very sensible tap."

"Several taps," said Tor, unhappily, to his knees. "With the sack. I'm sorry. I should say."

"Hush, Tor."

"And the reason," said Wensa, ignoring this, "that the two of you were in the main court at the second bell of the morning?"

Trice looked at Tovey. Tovey looked at Trice.

"A family matter," said Trice.

"A private one," said Tovey, stiffly, and went, if possible, paler. Trice could see him thinking it, every word: that the whole school was going to hear he'd lost himself over a family argument. That the whole school was going to think *that* was what had done it. She didn't help him. It was a private matter, and it was going to stay that way.

"Cousin?" said Tor.

They all looked at him. He'd looked up from his knees, and was looking from Trice to Tovey and back with a sort of slow dismay, as though a sum he'd been confident of had just come out wrong.

"You're cousins?" he said. "I didn't know you were related."

"First cousins," said Trice. "His mother and my father. Close enough that nobody's mother has ever once suggested we marry, thank goodness." Then the rest of it caught up with her, and she frowned at him. "You didn't know? Then why on earth did you *help*?"

Tor opened his mouth, and closed it.

She watched him try. He honestly tried; she could see him going about it, the way he went at a puzzle, frowning, looking for the place to start. He looked at her as if she'd asked him why water ran downhill.

"You were losing," he said at last. "And you're my friend. And we're in business, you and Miss Sara and me. And you were—" He made a small helpless gesture at her face. "I don't know what else I was meant to do."

Then he yawned. It took him completely by surprise: an enormous, jaw-cracking yawn that he tried to swallow and couldn't, so that he ended up hiding it behind both hands and apologizing through them.

"Sorry. Sorry. I finished the first fifty," he said, as if that explained it. "Of the drying fields. Last night. Before." He rubbed his eyes. "That's why I was out. I'd been in the workroom. I should have said."

Trice sat very still.

*He didn't know.*

She went back over it, quickly, the way she'd been trained to. The laundry. The yard. The evening in his room with Sara, when he'd called her *Miss—Trice*, and stopped in the middle, as though he weren't sure what he was allowed to call her. He'd thought she was nobody. A friend of Sara's. A girl in black from the special school, who'd been sent there to learn to look after herself because nobody else was going to. He'd come out of the dark at the second bell, numb to the knees, with no rod and no rank and no idea who she was, at a count of the realm in a battle rage the size of a cart horse, because she was losing. Because she was his friend. Because they were in *business*.

Nobody had ever done anything for her without knowing who she was. She'd never had the opportunity to find out what it would feel like. It felt, it turned out, rather warm, around the ears.

She found she had nowhere to put it. There wasn't a column.

"Fifty?" said Tovey.

Trice came back to the room. Tovey was staring at Tor.

"Fifty," said Tovey again. "In one night. Yourself."

"Yes, sir. Sorry. It's only copying. I'd have done more, but they're boring, and I kept losing count."

"Scholar Baker," said Tovey, with great care, "my mother buys lamps for the county from the Arlen works. The *whole* works. Forty journeymen. She's told me they can't do a hundred in a day between them."

Tor looked at him with polite interest, and Trice could see that he thought Tovey was being kind.

"That," said Wensa, "is exactly my point."

She hadn't sat down. She was standing by her chair with the firelight on one side of her face, and she was looking at Tor now, directly, for the first time.

"Headmaster. This boy walked through the outer ward this morning as if it weren't there. He saw through the ward on the door. He saw through the wards on these chairs, which were laid by the same hands that laid the wards on the—" She stopped. "Which are very good wards. He is a scholarship boy from nowhere, who builds in a night what a manufactory builds in two days. And last night, when one more noble child in this realm fell into a rage, as they have been falling into rages all year, more and more often, with no one able to tell us why, he was there." She drew breath. "An Austran agent could not have been better placed. I'm not saying he is one. I am saying I don't know what he is, and neither do you, and that I am not prepared to take the chance."

"Mistress Wensa," began the headmaster.

"I want him removed from the school. Today. Out of caution."

"On what authority?"

Wensa looked at him for a long moment. Then she put her hand to her belt, finally, the hand that had been waiting there for an hour, and when it came away there was something in it. It was short, and dark, cord-wrapped, a hand and a half long. Trice knew it at once. Everyone in the room knew it. Every student at Lairdgren who'd ever stood in Kolb's yard knew exactly what a cutter's hilt looked like.

"On mine," said Wensa. "I hold a commission in the royal guard. I have done for nineteen years." She didn't raise it. She didn't have to. "I'll take him to the holding cells myself, and he can stay there until people in this room come to their senses."

Tor stood up.

He did it at once, as though he'd been waiting to be told, and his face, Trice saw, was relieved: white, and frightened, and relieved, because at last someone was saying the thing he'd known all along was coming. "Yes, ma'am," he said. "Of course. I'll come. I'm sorry for the trouble."

And as he said it, his feet moved.

Trice saw it, because she'd spent an afternoon trying to kick him. His left foot slid back half a pace and turned out. His knees softened. His weight dropped and settled over the balls of his feet, and his hands, which had been clasped meekly in front of him, came apart and rose an inch or two, open, loose, at the height of his belt. It was Kolb's guard. It was the first thing Kolb taught you. Tor was standing in it perfectly, with his chin up and his eyes on Wensa's hands, not her face, while his mouth went on apologizing.

Wensa saw it too.

Trice watched it land on her. The hilt came up a quarter inch, no more, and Wensa's own weight shifted, and something went out of her face and something else came into it, very cold and very quiet, and in that instant Trice was quite certain that the woman was going to do it. Here, in front of the headmaster. She was going to do it, and Tor was going to stand there in his perfect guard saying *sorry*, and—

Kolb laughed.

It filled the little vaulted room, big and sudden and real, and it went off the walls like a dropped pan. Everybody jumped, except Kolb.

"Oh, look at him," said Kolb. He'd unfolded his arms. He was shaking his head, grinning, with the scar pulling up the corner of his mouth. "Look at that. Feet. Hands. Watching the weapon. I taught him that, Mistress. Every bit of it. Three years I've been beating that into him and I didn't think any of it had gone in." He looked at Tor with something very like fondness. "Stand easy, Baker. You'll frighten the lady."

Tor looked down at his own feet, startled, as though they'd done it without asking him. "Oh," he said. "Sorry."

"He takes direction, that's his trouble," Kolb told the room. "I tell him: Baker, you're small, if a big man comes for you, hit him once to make him look and then run. And what does he do? He hits him to make him look, and then he runs. All night. From a count in a rage. And here he is, not a mark on him that a week won't fix." He shook his head again. "I've had captains who couldn't follow an order that well."

"Mistress Wensa," said Tovey quietly. "Please."

That was all. He didn't stand. He didn't raise his voice. He only looked at her, very straight, and said it, in the voice his father had used, and Trice saw it reach her.

So she added her own weight. One more push on the scale; that was all it needed.

"Do keep on, Wensa," she said, comfortably, from behind her cold cloth. "Only I'd think about it. If you frighten him off now, he'll break his contract with Sara and me. And then he'll take his drying fields to the Sorveys, and the whole of the realm will be buying its laundry from Douglas Sorvey, and *then* where will any of us be?"

There was a pause.

Wensa looked at Trice. She looked at Tovey, and at Kolb, grinning by the door, and at the headmaster, who had taken his spectacles down off his forehead and was polishing them on his sleeve with great attention. Last, she looked at Tor, who was standing in front of the fire with his hands clasped again and his feet very carefully together, steaming gently.

The hilt went back to her belt.

"I would like it noted," she said, "that I objected."

"So noted," said the headmaster, putting his spectacles on. "Thank you, Wensa. Mr. Baker, will you walk with me?"
