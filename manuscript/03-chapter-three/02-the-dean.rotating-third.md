Rolph had been awake for most of an hour, and he still wasn't sure he was.

He was sitting on his bed with his blanket around his shoulders and his feet on the cold boards, because the dean of students had told him to sit up, and then had told him nothing else at all. The dean of students was standing by the window. He had been standing by the window for most of an hour. He was a narrow, gray-jawed man named Campbell, all in black from collar to boots, and he had the look of someone who'd been pulled out of his own bed in the dark and intended for the rest of the world to know it. One of his coat buttons was in the wrong hole. Rolph had noticed it in the first minute, and had been trying not to look at it ever since.

"Count Thompson," Campbell had said, coming in, "was attacked last night. Where is Scholar Baker?"

And Rolph, who had been asleep, had said, "What?" and then, "Tovey?" and then, "*Tor?*" and then, in the order in which they'd occurred to him, a number of other things, none of which Campbell had answered. He'd simply looked at Tor's empty bed, and at the desk with its fifty bare copper squares laid out in rows of ten, and then gone to the window to wait.

So Rolph waited too. He found that the waiting had a shape, if you watched it. Campbell had been sent. That was the first thing. He stood the way men stood who had been sent, and not the way they stood when they'd sent themselves: weight back on his heels, hands behind him, not quite at ease. Somebody else was waiting on the other end of this. Somebody senior. Rolph turned that over, slowly, with a mind still full of sleep, and didn't like any of the answers.

The tower bell rang. Rolph didn't count it.

Then the bar lifted in the door, by itself, with its little wooden knock, and Tor came in.

He came in sideways, because of the sack. Rolph saw the sack first, over his shoulder, and then the rest of him, and then he stood up without meaning to and let the blanket fall.

Tor was soaked to the thighs. His trousers were black with wet and mud. One sleeve of his tunic hung open from wrist to elbow, and the arm inside it was scratched in long red lines, and there were more on his face, and a leaf in his hair, and what looked like a smear of cabbage on his neck. He was limping. He was gray with tiredness, under the scratches, the way he sometimes was after a build, and his eyes had the very clear, very calm look in them that Rolph had learned, over two years, to be afraid of.

He looked at Campbell and stopped.

"Oh," said Tor. "Good morning, sir. I'm sorry. I was coming to you. I just had to—" He looked at Rolph. "I won't be a minute. I'm sorry. May I?"

Campbell said nothing. Tor seemed to take that as a yes.

He set the sack down on the floor between the beds. It clanked. He crouched and undid the neck of it, and took out a stack of copper squares, ten of them, tied together with a strip of rag, and stood up and put them into Rolph's hands.

"These are your mother's," he said. "They're the good ones. I did them first, before I got tired. You paid fifty-five, so if you send the other fifty-five to my mother, that's square. Only you needn't if you'd rather not, honestly, I know it was the friend price."

"Tor—"

"The other forty are in the sack." He was talking fast now, low and orderly, the way he talked when he was going through a field in his head and wanted to get all of it out before he lost the thread. "They all work. I tested every tenth one. Miss Sara said twelve, so that's four hundred and eighty, which is a lot, so if you'd please sell them to her and send it home. To my mother. Baker, at the bakery. Two Rivers. Anyone there will know." He frowned. "Not the ones on the desk. Those aren't done. They're only copper. Don't let her pay for those, they don't do anything."

Rolph stood holding the ten copper squares. They were cold. They smelled faintly of acid, and very strongly of wet leaves.

"Tor," he said. "What *happened*?"

"Scholar Baker," said Campbell.

"Yes, sir. Sorry. Nearly done." Tor turned back to Rolph, and for a moment he looked, not frightened, but apologetic, in a deep and settled way, as if he'd broken one of Rolph's cups and was very sorry about it but had already worked out how to replace it. "Thank you for everything," he said. "Really. You were very kind to me. I'm sorry about the mess."

"What mess? Tor, *what*—"

Campbell crossed the room in three strides, took Tor by the upper arm, and steered him out through the door. Tor went without any resistance at all. He said, "Sorry, sir," on the threshold, and then the door shut behind them, and the bar dropped into its bracket with its little wooden knock.

Rolph stood in the middle of the room in his nightshirt.

He looked at the door. He looked at the sack, sagging open on the floor, with forty copper squares inside it, slick with mud. He looked down at the ten in his hands, and the strip of rag around them, which had been torn, he saw, from the bottom of Tor's own shirt.

*Thank you for everything.*

"No," Rolph said, aloud, to the empty room, in a voice that was very nearly formal. "No. I don't think so."
