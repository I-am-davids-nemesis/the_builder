Rolph sat on his bed, dressed, with his coat beside him and the ten squares on his knees, still tied in their strip of rag. The sack lay open on the floor where Tor had left it. On the desk, fifty bare copper squares lay in their rows.

The bar lifted. Tor came in.

He crossed the room without a word, turned around, and fell backward onto his bed, full length, like a tree going over. The bed frame cracked. He lay with his arms flung out and his eyes shut and his filthy boots hanging off the end.

Rolph looked at him.

"Are you," he said, "even alive?"

"I don't know," said Tor, to the ceiling. "I think so. I'm meant to go to breakfast."

"*Breakfast?*"

"The headmaster said." Tor opened his eyes. "Rolph. I hit Tovey."

He told it lying on his back, staring upward, in a flat, careful voice. Rolph sat and listened. The tower bell rang once during it, and neither of them looked up.

"—and then he just fell out of the air," Tor was saying. "Plop. Into the mud. And got up and walked home. As if he'd left a lamp burning. And then the dean, and the basement, and the door you can't look at, and a room with a great fire in it that's colder than outside, and Trice and the headmaster and Wensa all sitting there in chairs you're not meant to be able to see. And then Tovey came in and *apologized*. To me. And everybody was very kind about it, really. Except Wensa."

He turned his head on the pillow and looked at Rolph.

"I hit a count," he said. "In a battle rage. From behind. With fifty copper squares. And I'm not dead, and I'm not in a cell, and nobody even seems— How? You know about nobles. How is that *possible*?"

Rolph looked down at the parcel on his knees for some time.

"I know Tovey," he said. "A bit. Enough. He's not going to come after you, Tor. Not ever. Not for this." He turned the parcel over. "He thinks you went in to help his cousin. Which you did. That's the whole of it, to Tovey. And he hates it. The rages. He hates them worse than anything. If you'd done something to him while he was himself, maybe. But you stopped him doing something while he wasn't. He'll be grateful to you till he dies, and he'll never once be able to say so properly, and you'll have to put up with it."

Tor said nothing. He frowned at the ceiling.

"And Trice," said Rolph, "is—" He paused. "She's different."

"Different?"

"From the rest of them. From most nobles. That's why she's spent so many years at the academy."

Tor came up on his elbows. "Nobles?"

"Tor."

"*Trice* is a noble?"

Rolph stared at him. "Tor. What do you think goes on at that academy?"

Tor's lips moved slightly. "Spies," he said at last, slowly, as if it were rude to say. "And— well. Assassins. Learning to be. That's what the second-years say. All the black, and the knife, and the powder, and—" He touched his own throat. "She had a knife, Rolph. In *unarmed*."

"It trains people who might *get* assassinated," said Rolph. "Mostly. That's the point of it. The children of houses that have enemies. Nobles. The big merchant families. You learn what the knife looks like coming, and how to get out of the way of it, and who to watch at dinner." He shrugged. "Some of the people who learn all that go on to use it. That's the bit the second-years talk about. But that's why Trice is there. She's a duke's daughter. And that's why Sara's there. She's Debri. You know that. The Debri house."

Tor lay back down slowly and put his arm across his eyes.

"She doesn't know who I am," he said, from under it. "Sara. When she finds out I'm nobody, she'll want to change the deal. She'll have to. You can't have a Debri buying from a baker's son from Two Rivers. She'll have to tell her father. And he'll—"

"Tor," said Rolph. "She knows exactly who you are."

The arm came off his eyes.

"She's a merchant," said Rolph. "A real one, from a real house. She doesn't put twelve gold a board on anything she hasn't had looked into. She came and found me, in the Barrow, last night. Before she went up to see you." He smiled slightly. "She wanted to know everything. Your family. Your people. Whether you work. Whether you drink. Whether you pay your debts. I told her."

"What did you tell her?"

"That your father's a baker and your mother's a baker and your brothers and sisters are bakers, and there are more of them than is reasonable. That you'll work until you fall down, and then you'll get up and apologize to the floor. And that you can bake," said Rolph. "Actually bake. Bread. Pies. That thing with the apples. Well enough to open a shop of your own if the building ever went wrong." He grinned. "That went down very well. Trice was there by then. You should have seen her face."

Tor looked at him blankly. "Why would that go down well?"

"Because nobody can *do* it, Tor."

"Everybody can do it."

"I can't make toast," said Rolph, with some pride. "I've tried. I set fire to a fork. Neither can anybody I grew up with. Neither can Trice. Neither can Sara, I'd bet, or Tovey, or half the people you're frightened of. There's not one of us who could feed ourselves for a week if the kitchens shut. And there you are, a builder, a good one, and if they took it all away from you tomorrow you'd just shrug and go and make bread. Do you know how rare that is? Do you know how *safe* that looks, to people like—" He stopped. "To merchants."

Tor said nothing for a while.

"She's very pretty," he said at last, gloomily. "Sara. And she's rich. And now she's a *Debri*. That's like being told the moon has a family name. It's not as if—" He didn't finish. He scowled at the ceiling. "It doesn't matter. Who's Trice, then? Really?"

Rolph sat up a little straighter.

"The Ducharina Patricia Alyson Morgan," he said. "Daughter of the Duke of Morgan. Patricia to the court. Tricia, if you're being polite and don't know her well. Only her friends call her Trice."

"Why Trice?"

"It's from when she was small. Some royal brat of a Prince couldn't get his mouth round Patricia, when he was a baby. He called her Trice instead. She bit him for it, I'm told. But it stuck." Rolph looked down at the parcel. "She doesn't seem to mind it. She lets her friends use it. You're one, apparently."

Tor's face fell.

"I called her *Miss Trice*," he said. "To her face. For days."

"She loved it."

"Oh, *no*."

Rolph laughed, and Tor put his arm back over his eyes. Down the corridor a door slammed and somebody shouted about a missing boot.

"You should send those off," said Tor, from under his arm. "To your mother. If you hurry, they'll make the express."

"I know." Rolph got up and went to the lockbox at the foot of his bed. He laid his hand flat on the lid; it clicked. He lifted out a box the length of his forearm, of dark red wood that took the light. Its lid was inlaid in a pattern of pale leaves, and its clasp was shaped like a closed hand. Inside, it was lined with gray velvet.

Tor had sat up to look. His eyes went over the inlay, the clasp, the velvet; then he looked away, very politely, at the wall.

Rolph untied the rag, chose four of the ten squares, and laid them side by side in the velvet.

"Only four?" said Tor.

"Four for her. In the box. The other six go loose, wrapped, so she can give them out." He shut the lid. "And this has to be wrapped properly, or I'll never hear the end of it. You've no idea what her friends are like. They notice paper. They'll look at the knot before they look at what's inside, and if it's a merchant's knot they'll say so, very sweetly, at dinner." He hunted along the shelf. "But it doesn't matter. Nobody's going to give her anything like this. Nobody. Not even Father, and he's—" He found the string. "Well. He's giving her something large. It won't be as good."

He sat down and began to wrap it, folding the corners under, neatly and with care.

"When's the other one going to be ready?" he said, intent on a corner. "The food drying one. For fruit. I want to write and tell her it's coming, if—"

"I can't," said Tor.

Rolph looked up.

Tor sat on the edge of his bed with one boot off, holding it in his lap. His face was gray.

"I can't do it now," he said. "I'm sorry. I'm trying to think about it, and I can't. I need to be able to see the whole of it, and I can't see anything. I haven't slept. Everything keeps going round." He put the boot down. "I'm sorry. I'll do it. I will. Just not now."

Rolph put the box down on the bed beside him. He looked at Tor for a long moment.

"Take your time," he said. "Tor. I mean it. There's no hurry. There was never any hurry."

"There's the shield," said Tor, not really listening. "That's twelve days. And there's the rest for Sara, the fifty on the desk, and classes, and the building master, and Kolb. And Wensa." He laughed, without much in it. "Wensa's got it in for me now. She'll be watching everything. The headmaster says to act normally, so she's got nothing to look at, but I don't see how I'm meant to. Did you know," he added, "she's a royal guard? Some kind of secret one. She's been one nineteen years. She had a cutter."

Rolph went still.

"Say that again," he said.

Tor looked up.

"Wensa," said Rolph. "Mistress Wensa. Accounts." His voice was flat and clear, every consonant in its place. "She told you that she is a member of the royal guard. In those words. Are you certain?"

"Yes," said Tor, a little alarmed. "She said it to the headmaster. She said she held a commission. She took the hilt out, Rolph, it was—" He stopped. "Rolph? Are you all right?"

Rolph sat looking at the wrapped box beside him for some time.

"I'm fine," he said. "Go on."

Tor looked at him uncertainly. Then he tried a smile.

"It's a pity she's not younger, really," he said. "Isn't it? I've never been chased about by a woman before. I'd have thought I'd enjoy it more."

Rolph stared at him.

Then he laughed. He laughed so hard he had to catch the box before it slid off the bed. Tor looked pleased and anxious at once, and Rolph laughed harder, and held onto the bedpost, and then wiped his eyes, and at last sat up.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, the *roses*. In her teeth." He wiped his eyes again. "Thank you. I needed that. What else did she say?"

Tor's face fell a little. "That I might be an Austran spy."

Rolph's hand stopped halfway to his face.

"She didn't say I *was*," said Tor quickly. "She said she didn't know what I was. Because of the wards, and the fifty. And because all the nobles keep going into rages, she said, more and more of them, all year, and nobody knows why, and I was there. So she thought maybe I'd done it. To Tovey. With some sort of magic that makes nobles angry. To get Trice—" He swallowed. "To get Trice hurt. Or killed. She didn't say killed. But she meant it. And she wanted me in a cell. Master Kolb came, though," he added, brightening. "He laughed at her. He said I take direction well. I think it was the nicest thing he's ever said to me."

Rolph stood up.

His face had gone quite white and quite still.

"She said," he said, "that you tried to have Trice killed."

"Not *said*—"

"You. You walked into a battle rage with a bag of copper to get a girl you thought was nobody out from under a count, and that woman stood up in front of the headmaster and called you an *assassin*." He had picked up his coat. "For that. For *that*."

"Rolph—"

"Get your boot on."

He went out. He didn't slam the door. His footsteps went away down the corridor, quick and hard.

Tor sat a moment, staring at the open door. Then he grabbed the boot, and lunged for the door with it, and there was a scuffle and a thump in the corridor, and his voice, "Wait—sorry—wait," and the uneven sound of someone running with one boot on and the other in his hand.

He caught up with Rolph at the top of the stair, hopping, trying to get the second boot on as he went. Rolph slowed without looking around.

"Rolph," said Tor, breathless. "You know I didn't. Don't you? I didn't try to hurt anybody. I wouldn't. I didn't even know she was—"

"I know." Rolph stopped on the landing. His hands were fists; he opened them. "Tor. I know. Of course I know."

Tor got the boot on at last, and straightened, and looked at him.

"But listen," said Rolph. "Listen to me. The royal guard don't—" He paused. "They don't play by the same rules as the rest of us. They aren't *meant* to. They're there to keep one thing safe, and they don't care what it costs anyone else, and nobody's allowed to ask them to. If one of them's decided she's worried about you, then you be careful. You be very, very careful. Do you understand me?"

Tor looked at him for a long moment.

"How do you know that?" he said.

"Everybody knows that," said Rolph. "Come on. They'll have eaten all the eggs."

He went on down the stairs. After a moment, Tor followed him.
