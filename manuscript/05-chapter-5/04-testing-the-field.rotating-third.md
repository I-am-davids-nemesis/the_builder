The candles had burned down to two stubs in two puddles, and the window had gone from black to gray, when Tor sat back from the green tablecloth and found that it was morning.

He knew the feeling. He'd been somewhere else, and now he wasn't. His neck had set in one position like a pot left to dry, and his eyes felt as if somebody had rubbed sand into them very gently, all night, out of kindness. He sat for a while and let the room come back: the moss-green cloth, the cooling plate square in the middle of it, the white flowers in their bowl, the curtains hanging quite still in the cool.

In front of him, in a neat row, were ten small copper squares, each with a hole punched at the top, and on each one, etched fine and dark, a line that went round in a loop like an egg and then down, out of the bottom of the egg, in a hook.

He picked one up. It was cool to touch, which meant nothing; copper always was. He put it down again.

"Ten," he said. His voice came out like a door that hadn't been opened in a while.

He counted them anyway, with one finger, to be sure. Rolph's mother. Karina. Varley. Rolph. Trice, Sara, Tovey. Rolph's father. One for him to test. One spare.

He looked at the spare. Then he thought about how many people he'd met yesterday, at the gate and in the corridor and at dinner, and how many he hadn't met yet, and how many more there would be at the luncheon, and how many of them would be standing about in the heat in their good things, and how *one spare* was what you said when you hadn't thought it through.

He sighed, and reached for the copper.

The second ten went faster. They always did; by the second batch your hands knew where they were going and only needed you to keep them company. The window went from gray to white while he did it. Somewhere a long way off a cock crowed, and was answered, and a bell he didn't know rang the fifth hour, not the school's bell, a thinner one, from somewhere down the hill. Then a nearer one, deeper, that made the glass in the window hum. Tor stopped and listened to it all the way through, five strokes and their echoes, out of habit, and then went on.

When he'd finished there were twenty. He squared them into two stacks of ten and sat looking at them, and his head nodded forward once, by itself, and he caught it.

"No," he told it. "Not yet. You haven't *tried* it."

He found the hemp string in the bottom of his trunk, in the bag with the spare buttons, a whole hank of it, gray and hairy and smelling faintly of the store. He cut a length with his knife and threaded the first square from the top of the left-hand stack and knotted it, the way he'd knotted his shield cord, and put it over his head, and tucked it in under his shirt, against his chest, beside the shield.

Then he laid his hand flat on it and told it what *right* was.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened; the room was already right. He'd made it right himself, last night. He stood there in the cool with his hand on his chest, feeling nothing at all, and felt rather foolish.

"Well, you'll have to go *out*, then," said Tor, and went to find his boots.

* * *

He tried the corridor first.

The corridor was warm already, though it was barely past the fifth bell; the long carpet held the heat the way bread holds it, and the tall windows at the end were full of white sky. Tor walked the whole length of it, slowly, with his hands held out a little from his sides, palms down, like a man feeling for rain.

The warmth came at him and didn't arrive. That was the only way he could put it. He could see it was hot: a maid on her knees at the far end with a brush and pan had her sleeves pushed up past the elbow and a dark patch down the back of her dress. He could feel, on his face, on the backs of his hands, the faint thick touch of it, the way you feel the heat from an open oven door across a kitchen. But it stopped there. It stopped a finger's width off his skin, and went round.

He stopped in front of the maid.

"Good morning," said Tor.

The maid looked up at him: at his school brown, his uncombed hair, his eyes with no sleep in them, his hands held out palm-down in the air. She looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, "Morning, sir," very carefully, and went back to her brushing without taking her eyes off him.

"Is it hot?" said Tor. "In here. Would you say?"

"It's—" The maid considered this, as if it might be a trick. "It's warm, sir."

"Thank you," said Tor warmly, and went on down the stairs, muttering.

He tried the stairs, which were warmer. He tried a passage at the bottom of the stairs that ran past a row of doors and smelled of beeswax and boot blacking, which was warmer still. He found a door at the end of it standing open on a yard, and went out into the yard, and the sun hit him.

It hit him the way it had hit him getting out of the carriage the day before: like a hand. The yard was all pale flagstones and whitewashed walls, and the light came off every surface of it at once, and he screwed up his eyes. And then he stood there in the very middle of it, in the full sun, with his eyes screwed up and his arms out, and waited to start sweating.

He didn't.

He could feel the sun. He could feel it on his hair, on the top of his head, warm, the way the hearthstone is warm on your feet. But under his shirt, and down his back, and in the creases of his elbows, where yesterday the sweat had started before he'd crossed the river gate, it was cool. Not cold. Not even quite cool, if he was honest. It was *nothing*: the temperature of nothing. The temperature of not noticing. Like the cellar on a summer afternoon, when you go down for the butter and stand there a moment longer than you need to, for no reason you could say.

Tor stood in the middle of the yard and laughed out loud, once.

A boy came out of a door on the far side with a bucket in each hand, saw him, and stopped dead. Tor waved. The boy went back in, with both buckets.

"Right," said Tor, to himself. "Right. That's sun. What about—"

He went round the side of the house. There was a long path there, paved, between two rows of trees with broad dark leaves and fat green fruit on them, with the sun coming through in coins, and an old man in an apron raking the gravel at the edge of it. The old man watched Tor walk the whole length of the fig walk and back again, very slowly, stopping every twenty paces to hold out his hands and look at them and frown, and occasionally to say something to them. He raked the same patch of gravel the whole time.

"It's not *working*," Tor was saying, to his left hand, as he came back past. "It's working, but it's not *working*. It's keeping me where I am, but it doesn't know where I am, it's only guessing. If I was cold, it wouldn't— and the ground, it's taking it *all* down, it's not giving any back, so in a month you'd have a little hot place under wherever you'd been standing, like a—" He stopped. He looked down at the gravel. "Oh, that's *interesting*."

He crouched down, and put his hand flat on the path, and kept it there.

The old man stopped raking.

Tor stood up again. "No," he said. "No, it's fine. It's spreading. It's fine." He looked round, and saw the old man looking at him, and went pink, and said, "Good morning. Sorry. It's very hot, isn't it?"

"It is, sir," said the old man. He was sweating so hard his collar was dark with it.

"Oh," said Tor, and looked suddenly stricken, and patted his pockets, and found nothing in them, and looked back toward the house where both stacks of ten were waiting on the green cloth upstairs. "Oh, I'm sorry, I haven't— Would you wait there? Just a— no, you're busy, you're working, I'll—" He stopped. He made himself stop. He stood on the fig walk with his hands at his sides and his mouth shut, and then he said, very carefully, "I'll bring you something. Later. If that's all right," and went back into the house before the old man could say whether it was or not.

The old man watched him go. Then he took off his hat, and wiped his head with his sleeve, and put his hat back on, and went on raking.

* * *

Upstairs, Tor sat down at the green table to write it down, all of it, the yard and the stairs and the fig walk and the hot place under the path, before it went.

He wrote half a page. Then he put his head down on his arms beside it, just for a moment, to rest his eyes.

When he lifted it again, the light had moved right across the table and the noon bell was ringing, the big one, the one that made the window hum, and somebody was knocking at the door.

* * *

Quavel had been carrying the clothes himself.

That was the thing he would remember afterwards, when he told it in the servants' hall, which he did not do, because he was the head server, and the head server did not tell things in the servants' hall. Master Alphonse had come down to the pantry at half past the eleventh bell, himself, in his shirtsleeves, with his arms full, and had said, "Quavel, could you take these up to the scholar? Some of my old things. He's going out this afternoon and he's terrified of the brown," and Quavel had said, "Of course, sir," and had taken them, because what else did you do, even though there were four footmen and a valet in the house whose business it was to carry clothes up stairs and not one of them was the head server.

So he'd been carrying them along the second-floor corridor, a summer coat in dove-gray linen and two shirts and a waistcoat with a pattern of small leaves, all of them a good four years too small for Master Alphonse and very likely a good deal too large for anybody else, when the Count came round the corner at the far end.

Quavel stopped walking.

There was nobody in the house who quite knew what Count Lairdgren was. He came and went. He'd come and gone, as far as Quavel could tell, for the whole of Quavel's twenty years in the house and for a good while before, and he looked, as far as Quavel could tell, exactly as he had on the first day: a small, neat man, quiet, in a plain dark coat, with a face you'd have passed in the street and a way of standing in a room that made you forget you'd ever been in one without him. He wasn't on any list Quavel had ever seen. He didn't need to be. When he was in the house, the house knew it, the way a pond knows when there's a heron at the edge of it.

The Count looked at the clothes in Quavel's arms. He looked at the door at the end of the corridor. He looked back at the clothes.

"For the scholar?" he said.

"Yes, my lord. From Master Alphonse."

"I'll take them," said Count Lairdgren.

And he did. He took them out of Quavel's arms, the gray coat and the shirts and the leaf waistcoat, and settled them over his own arm, quite expertly, the way a man does who has done it before, though Quavel could not for his life imagine when.

"You may come and hold the door," he added.

Quavel came and held the door.

The Count knocked. There was a pause, and a thump, as if someone inside had sat up very suddenly, and a voice said, "Yes? Sorry. Come in."

The Count went in. Quavel held the door, and saw, past the Count's shoulder, the guest.

He was sitting at the long table in the first room in yesterday's brown, the green cloth in front of him covered at one end with papers and at the other with two neat stacks of small copper squares, and a coil of gray string, and a knife. His hair stood straight up on one side where his head had been lying on it. There was the mark of a cuff pressed into his cheek. He looked about fifteen, and as if he'd been up all night, and he was getting to his feet with the hurried politeness of a boy who has been found asleep in church.

"Sir?" said the Count. His voice was quite low and quite level, a servant's voice, and Quavel, who had never heard him use it before, felt something go cold down the back of his neck. "Master Alphonse suggested that these clothes be brought to you. For your outing later this afternoon?"

"Oh!" said the guest. "Oh, that's very kind. Thank you. You didn't have to bring them up yourself."

"It was no trouble, sir."

The Count walked into the room. And then he stopped, a few paces in, and looked through the open doorway into the bedroom beyond, at the great bed with its pale green curtains, and Quavel looked with him, and saw what the Count was looking at.

The bed was made. It was made the way the maids had made it yesterday afternoon, sheet folded down over the coverlet in a perfect band, pillows plumped and square. Nobody had sat on it. Nobody had so much as leaned on it.

The Count looked at it for some time. He looked at it, Quavel thought, the way he'd once seen the Count look at a letter on a salver, a long time ago, before he picked it up: as if he already knew what it said, and was interested only in how it had been folded.

Then he turned back to the guest, and laid the clothes over the back of a chair, and said nothing at all about it.

The guest hadn't noticed. The guest was looking at the copper.

"Can I show you something?" he said. "Only I've been up— I made something last night. I've been trying it. I've been all round, the yard and the stairs and the fruit trees, the long path with the—" He gestured. "But I've only tried it on me. And you can't really tell, on yourself, can you, whether it's working, or whether you only want it to." He picked up one of the squares from the top of a stack and held it out on his palm. "It's for the heat."

The Count looked at the square on the boy's palm. He didn't take it. He leaned forward, a little, from the waist, and looked at it, and Quavel saw his eyes go along the etched line, round the loop and down into the hook, and stop there, and go back, and go round again.

"For the heat," he said.

"It's the same as the plate. That one." The guest pointed at the green cloth, where a bigger square of copper lay in the middle like a dish. "It takes the warm out of the room and puts it down in the ground. Only this one doesn't do the room. It just does you. Just the bit of air right next to you, a finger's width. The shield holds it on, like an egg, I made it so it'd sit in the shield, and then this keeps the egg right. Cool when it's hot and warm when it's cold. And it puts the warm down into the ground, wherever you are, it reaches, the way the plate does, only I've found out this morning it doesn't give it back properly, it just piles it up underneath you, so I need to—" He stopped himself. He went a little pink. "Sorry. It's for the heat."

The Count straightened up.

"Most interesting, sir," he said.

"Would you try it?" said the guest. "Would you mind? Only you've just come up the stairs, and it's hot on the stairs, and if you could tell me whether—"

He was already cutting string. He cut a length off the gray hank with his knife and threaded the square and knotted it, quickly, without looking, as if his hands had done it twenty times since dawn, and held it out by the string.

Count Lairdgren took it.

He held it up and looked at it, turning on its gray hemp, catching the light from the window. Then he put it over his head. He did it quite simply, the way you'd put on a scarf, and tucked it inside his plain dark coat, and laid his hand flat on it, over the coat, where it lay against his chest.

Quavel, at the door, held the door, and did not breathe.

They stood there, the three of them, in the cool room.

"Hm," said the guest, after a while. He sounded disappointed. "It's no good in here, is it? It's already right in here. You can't tell." He brightened. "You'd want to go somewhere hot. Outside. Or—" His face lit. "The kitchen. Would you? The kitchen'd be the hottest place in the house, wouldn't it, at noon, with the ovens. You could walk through the kitchen and see."

The Count inclined his head.

"I can do that, sir," he said. "Would you care for breakfast first, sir? Or a bath?"

The guest looked down at himself, at yesterday's brown and the cuff-mark on his hand and the faint gray shadow of copper filings in the creases of his fingers. "A bath," he said. "I think. I'd better. Before I touch Rolph's— before I put those on." And then, anxiously, "If that's not too much trouble. I can find something to eat myself. I don't want to put anyone out."

"It's no trouble at all, sir." The Count turned. "Quavel. Breakfast for the scholar, if you would. In half an hour."

"My lord," said Quavel.

The guest glanced at him, and then at the Count, and smiled at both of them with the vague goodwill of a boy who has not slept, and went off to find the bath.

The Count came out past Quavel into the corridor, with the copper square on its string inside his coat. He didn't look at Quavel. He went away along the corridor, toward the back stairs, toward the kitchens, at an even, unhurried pace, a small neat man in a dark coat going to see if it was hot.

Quavel shut the door very quietly.

* * *

The water was wrong.

Tor lay in it with his eyes half shut and his chin on his chest and the brass taps gleaming at the end of the bath, and tried to think how. He'd turned the warm tap all the way, and let it run, and it had come out steaming, he'd seen it steam. And now he was lying in it and it felt like—

Like nothing. Like the cellar. Like the yard had felt, in the full sun, like the fig walk had felt. Not cold. Not warm. Just there, all round him, and not touching him. Like lying in air.

He frowned at his knees. He put a hand into the water, and lifted it out, and watched the drops run off it, and put it back. Steam was still coming up off the surface, quite plainly, in the bars of light from the window.

"That's *hot*," Tor told the bath. "You're hot. I can *see* you being hot."

And then he looked down at his chest, at the gray string round his neck and the small copper square lying flat on his breastbone under the water, and closed his eyes.

"Oh," said Tor. "Oh, you *idiot*."

He sat up, and the water slopped, and he got the string over his head and held the amulet up out of the bath, dripping, and the heat arrived. It arrived all at once, everywhere, like a door being opened, so that he gasped and nearly dropped the copper and sat there, pink to the shoulders, in a bath that was suddenly quite as hot as he'd run it.

"It works," he said, faintly, to the ceiling. "It works on water. It works on *bathwater*." He looked at the string. The string was soaked, and already going limp and dark, the hemp swelling. "And you'll rot," he told it. "You'll rot in a week. Wool. Or a chain. Or something that doesn't mind." He reached out of the bath and laid it carefully on the tiles, out of the wet. "And it'll want a stop. For baths. So you can tell it *leave this one alone*."

He lay back in the hot water, and let it come, and found it very nice indeed.

He very nearly fell asleep again. He caught himself with his nose an inch off the surface, and sat up, and scrubbed.

* * *

When Quavel came in with the tray, the guest was still in the bath; Quavel could hear splashing, and once, distinctly, a voice saying *no, but if you did it with a stop* to nobody.

He laid the breakfast on the end of the green table, away from the copper. He laid it properly, because he was the head server, and because it was the first time in twenty years he had been told by Count Lairdgren to lay anything. Bread from the morning baking, still warm in its cloth. Butter in a dish of chipped ice. A cold fowl, sliced. Peaches. Honey. A pot of coffee and a jug of milk and a jug of the cold mint water the young ladies took in summer.

He stood back and looked at it, and moved the peaches an inch.

The door opened behind him, and the Count came in.

He had been to the kitchen. Quavel could tell, because he smelled faintly of it, of bread and smoke and onions, and because he'd stopped on the way back to take his coat off, and was carrying it over his arm, which Quavel had never seen him do either. He was in his shirtsleeves and a plain waistcoat. The gray string showed at his collar.

He was not sweating.

Quavel looked at that, and then looked away, because it was not his business to look, and then looked again, because the kitchen at noon on a day like this was like the inside of a bread oven, and the cooks went through it in their shifts with wet cloths tied round their necks, and you could not walk across it, not even in your shirt, without the sweat starting on your lip. The Count's face was dry. His collar was crisp. He looked as if he had been for a walk along the fig walk in the cool of the evening.

The bathroom door opened, and the guest came out, wrapped in a towel to the chin, with his hair in wet spikes, carrying the copper square on its sodden string in front of him like a dead mouse.

He saw the breakfast and stopped.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, *look* at that."

"Your breakfast, sir," said Quavel.

"Thank you. Thank you so much. That's— there are peaches." He looked at the peaches as if they'd been laid out especially for him, which they had. Then he saw the Count, and remembered, and his whole face changed. "Oh! Did you try it? In the kitchen? Did it—"

"I did, sir," said the Count. "I walked through the kitchen twice, from the ovens to the yard door, and stood for some minutes beside the great range, where the bread is baked." He paused. "And outside, in the yard, in the sun. And on the back stairs, which are close."

"And?"

The Count considered his answer for a moment.

"Most effective, sir," he said.

The guest let out a breath. He let it out the way a man does who has been holding it since the fifth bell. His shoulders came down. He smiled, not at the Count, not at anything, a quick, private, delighted smile that went across his face and was gone again before he could catch it.

"Good," he said. "Oh, good. It did it in the bath, too, you know. I forgot I had it on. I lay there for ages thinking the water was wrong." He held up the dripping string. "So it needs a stop. And a different string. But it *works*." He set the amulet down on the corner of the table, on a napkin, carefully. And then he looked at the Count, and frowned a little, and seemed to come to some decision.

"I'm sorry," he said. "You keep calling me sir. I'm not anybody. I'm Tor. Torrance, really, Torrance Baker, but everybody says Tor." He put out his hand, the one that wasn't holding the towel. "I'm at school with Rolph. Alphie." He made a small face. "Alphonse. I'm going to get that wrong all summer. What's your name?"

The Count looked at the hand.

Quavel, by the window, had a coffee pot in his hands. He found that he was holding it very tightly.

The Count took the hand, and shook it, once, a plain firm shake. Up close, side by side, with the window light on both of them, there was something about the two faces that Quavel's eye caught on and could not let go of: something in the set of the brows, or the jaw, or the quick watchful stillness of the eyes, so that he looked from one to the other and back without meaning to, and couldn't have said what he was looking for.

"Burks," said the Count.

The coffee pot did not fall. Quavel would remember that, too, afterwards, with some pride.

"Burks," said Tor, warmly. "Thank you, Burks. For the clothes, and for testing it. And for breakfast." He looked round. "And you, sir. I'm sorry, I didn't—"

"Quavel, sir."

"Thank you, Quavel."

Burks had put his hand inside his collar, and drawn out the gray string, and lifted it over his head. He held the amulet out, on its hemp, toward Tor.

"Yours, sir, I believe."

Tor looked at it, and then at him, as if he'd said something very strange.

"Oh, no," he said. "No, that's yours. Keep it. That's what it's *for*." He waved a hand at the two copper stacks. "I made extra. I made twenty. I didn't know how many people there'd be." He looked at the string. "Only change that, if you can. The hemp. It'll rot if you get it wet, and you will, it's summer. Wool's better. Or a chain."

Burks stood holding the amulet for a moment longer. Then he put it back over his head, and tucked it back inside his collar, and laid his hand on it, flat, the way Tor did.

"Thank you, Tor," he said.

He said the name quite plainly. It was the first time he'd said it. Quavel heard him say it, and could not have said why the back of his neck went cold again.

"And one for you," said Tor, to Quavel, and before Quavel could step back the boy had picked a square off the top of the second stack and cut a length of string and knotted it and held it out, in one movement, beaming. "You've been running up and down stairs all morning. I've seen you. You can tell me if it works on you as well."

Quavel looked at the Count.

The Count, very slightly, inclined his head.

Quavel put the coffee pot down, and took the amulet, and said, "Thank you, sir," and felt for one dizzying moment like a boy at a fair, and put it in his pocket, and resolved to think about it later, somewhere quiet, for a long time.

* * *

After breakfast, which Tor ate standing up, in his towel, before Burks gently suggested he sit, Burks helped him with the clothes.

Quavel stayed. Nobody told him to go, and he did not offer.

There were two shirts, and Burks held them both up against the window and chose the softer. There was the gray summer coat, which went on, and hung, and was too long in the sleeve by a hand's breadth; Burks turned the cuffs back twice, quickly, so that the lining showed, pale green, and stood back, and it looked as if it had been meant. There was the waistcoat with the small leaves, which Tor looked at doubtfully and said, "Isn't that a bit much?" and Burks said, "Not for luncheon, sir," and Tor put it on.

And there was a stock, a long strip of fine white linen, which Tor held up in both hands with an expression of deep alarm.

"I don't know how," he said. "We don't— at home, you just do your top button."

"Allow me," said Burks.

He stood in front of Tor and tied it. He did it without hurrying, his small neat hands going round and through and under, the way the cook's go when she's plaiting a loaf. Tor stood very still with his chin up and his eyes on the ceiling, the way a boy stands to have his hair cut. Quavel, watching from beside the window, thought he had never in his life seen anything so peculiar as this, and could not have said what was peculiar about it, except everything.

"There," said Burks.

He turned Tor by the shoulders, quite gently, toward the long glass between the windows.

Tor looked at himself. He looked for quite a long time. A small young man in a gray linen coat with the cuffs turned back to show green, and a leaf-patterned waistcoat, and a white stock tied in a way that was perfectly plain and somehow exactly right, with damp hair beginning to dry into its usual disorder, and behind his shoulder, a little taller, a small neat man in shirtsleeves, looking at the glass too.

"Oh," said Tor, to the glass, quietly. "I look like somebody."

Burks didn't answer. He looked at the glass a moment longer, at the two of them in it. Then he stepped back, out of it, and took up his coat from the chair, and put it on.

"You'll do very well, sir," he said. "Master Alphonse will come for you at the second bell, I believe. The carriage is ordered for half past."

"Thank you, Burks. Really. Thank you."

"Sir." Burks went to the door. He stopped there, with his hand on it, and looked back once, not at Tor, at the green table: at the papers, at the bigger plate on the cloth, at the stack of copper squares with their etched loops and hooks, waiting in a row. He looked at them for perhaps the space of a breath.

Then he went out, and Quavel, after a moment, followed him, and shut the door.

* * *

In the corridor, Count Lairdgren walked away toward the back stairs without a word, at his even pace, his hand resting, briefly, on the front of his coat.

Quavel stood outside the guest's door with a copper square in his pocket and listened to the house. Below, faintly, the kitchens. Outside, the rake on the fig walk. Behind the door, a chair scraping, and then nothing, and then, very softly, the slow, even breathing of a young man who has sat down in a borrowed coat, very carefully so as not to crease it, and gone to sleep sitting up.

* * *

Quavel had a house to run, and he went and ran it. But at the second bell he found that he had business on the second floor, a matter of the linen press at the end of the corridor, which he had been meaning to look into for some years.

He was looking into it when Master Alphonse came up the main stair.

He wasn't alone. There was a young lady on his arm. She was tall, taller than any merchant's daughter Quavel had ever served, and came up nearly to Master Alphonse's ear, which few people did now that he had begun, at last, to grow into his father's height; and she walked as if she had been told at an early age to take up exactly the room she was owed and not an inch less. She was very fair, her hair the pale yellow of new straw, put up close and plain under a small hat, and she wore a walking dress the gray-blue of a pigeon's wing. It was a strong face, long and level, with a straight nose and a wide, steady mouth. Nobody, Quavel thought, would ever have called it pretty. Nobody who had seen it walk down a room would have forgotten it, either.

He knew the face. Every servant in the house knew it. He set down the stack of pillowcases he had not been counting and stood very straight.

"Quavel," said Master Alphonse. He was in his good blue coat, the one for calling, and he was smiling the smile Quavel had last seen on him at nine years old, when they'd put him up on a horse a hand too tall for him in front of half the city and told him to look pleased. "Is he up?"

"The scholar is resting, sir. He was up all night." Quavel paused. "Working."

"Of course he was." Master Alphonse let out a breath that was nearly a laugh. He looked at the door. He looked, for a moment, at the young lady beside him, who was looking at the door too, with polite and complete attention, the way a buyer looks at a horse before the dealer has said a word. "Well. Open it, would you?"

Quavel opened it.

The guest was asleep in the chair by the green table, upright, his hands folded in his lap, in the gray coat with the green cuffs and the waistcoat with the small leaves, his chin sunk on the white stock and his hair gone back to its own opinions. The copper squares lay in their two stacks beside him. The room was cool. The young lady stopped on the threshold as the cool came round her, and her eyebrows went up a fraction, and she looked at the plate on the green cloth for some time.

Master Alphonse crossed the room and put his hand on the guest's shoulder.

"Tor. Tor. Wake up."

The guest woke all at once, the way children and soldiers do, and was on his feet before he was entirely awake, one hand going flat to his chest. "Sorry. Sorry, is it the bell? I'm ready. I'm dressed, look, Burks did the—" He saw the young lady in the doorway, and stopped, and his back straightened, and his chin came up a hair, and he bowed from the shoulders, quite correctly, in Count Thompson's very voice. "I beg your pardon, my lady. Good afternoon."

"Good afternoon," said the young lady. Her voice was low and clear and gave nothing away.

Master Alphonse stood between them. He looked from one to the other. He put his hands behind his back, and took them out again, and Quavel, at the door, saw him do it and wished, for no reason he could have given, that he had stayed with the linen.

"Tor," said Master Alphonse, "I'd like you to meet Ursula Benevolence Matilde Thorgood." He took a breath. "My fiancée."

The guest looked at him.

He looked at Master Alphonse for a long moment, with his mouth a little open and his hand still flat on his chest, over the copper, as if something had just been thrown at him and he hadn't yet worked out whether it had hit.

"Your," said Tor.
