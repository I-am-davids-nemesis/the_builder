At the fifth bell on the last morning of term the Thompson traveling coach stood in the main court: dark green, the arms painted small on the door, four bays in the traces, two men on the box and one up behind. The luggage rail on the roof was empty. Tovey stood beside it in his plain dark cloak with his gloves in one hand, while the court filled up around him with trunks bumping down stairs, first-years crying, and somebody's mother in a hat.

Trice arrived first, with three trunks on a porter's barrow.

"Three," said Tovey.

"Three," said Trice. "One for clothes, one for the other clothes, and one for the things I'm bringing back for people who will be cross if I don't. Is there a problem?"

"No."

"Good."

Rolph came next, with four, and Sara with one and a satchel. The men on the box climbed down and began to lift the first of Trice's trunks toward the rail.

Then Tor came round the corner of the long hall, and the men stopped lifting.

He walked briskly, in his brown, with his old belt round his middle and a stuffed satchel on his back. Behind him, at the height of a man's knee, came ten flat copper plates in a line, a little apart, bobbing. Nine were empty. They swung wide round the corner after him, one after another. The tenth carried an old brown trunk with its corners mended in a different leather, and rode lower than the rest.

He stopped in front of the coach. The plates stopped behind him.

"Good morning," said Tor. "Sorry I'm late. I had to finish the—" He looked at the coach, the four bays, the men on the box, and then at Tovey, and went pink. "Oh. Oh, I'm sorry. I should have said. I thought Rolph had said."

"Said what?" said Rolph.

"We're flying," said Tor.

The nearest people in the court stopped what they were doing. Then the people beyond them stopped. A first-year stopped crying to look.

"I made plates," said Tor. "For the luggage. That's what they're for. You put the trunk on, and you keep the button, and it follows you. It'll follow you up. I tried it on the stair." He was untying a small cloth bag from his belt. "I thought we'd fly. It's quicker. And you all have your belts. Unless—" He looked round at them. "Unless you'd rather not. I don't mind the coach. I just thought."

Tovey looked at the coach, at the luggage rail, at the ten plates waiting at knee height, and at Trice, who had turned to regard her three trunks.

"Well," said Trice. "In that case, Master Baker, you'll be carrying all three of mine."

"Of course."

"All three. All the way."

"I don't carry them. They carry themselves. You just have to keep the—"

"I heard *carry*," said Trice. "I'm holding you to *carry*. Two of them are mostly shoes."

Tor knelt in the dust of the court and began to strap trunks to plates. He worked fast and without looking up: Trice's three, Rolph's four, Sara's one, and Tovey's, which his man fetched down off the coach's boot and handed over without a word; a buckle each side and one across. Each plate sank an inch as the weight came onto it and rose again to knee height. When each trunk was fast he took a copper disc from the bag, no bigger than a coat button, pressed his thumb to it and then to the plate, and handed the button to the trunk's owner.

"Keep it on you," he said to each of them. "In a pocket. If you put it down, it'll stay with the button, so don't put it down anywhere you're not going to be."

He gave Trice hers last. She weighed it in her palm, and then it was gone somewhere inside her black.

"And what's in yours?" said Rolph, nodding at the old brown trunk. "Stones?"

"Copper. Mostly. Things I've made. For your mother." Tor went pink again. "And some spares. In case anybody needs anything. You never know, at a place you haven't been."

"It's riding low."

"It's heavy," Tor told the trunk.

Tovey said four words to the man on the box. The man touched his hat, and the coach went away round the long hall at a walk, empty, the bays shaking their heads.

Tovey turned back. He fixed his eyes on a point a little above Tor's head.

"Scholar Baker. I've tried it fast."

Tor looked up from the last buckle.

"On the belt. Over the river, ten days ago. It gets cold. Very cold. You can't breathe; the air comes at you too quickly to take any. And my eyes watered until I couldn't see where I was going. I came down in a field I didn't know." A pause. "I'm not afraid of it. I'd like to know how far it is to the capital, at a speed where I can see."

"Oh!" said Tor, and his face cleared entirely. "Oh, no, it's all right. I know. I did that too, the first time. I couldn't breathe all the way to Hollin." He was digging in his satchel. "That's the shield. It lets the air through, so it lets the wind through, all of it, straight at you. So I made a new one." He brought out a handful of palm-sized copper plates on cords, like the shields, but with a long sweeping line etched across each. "It's the same as the old one at the back. But at the front it's—" He drew it in the air with one finger: round at the front, long and narrow behind. "It's the shape of a drip. Off the pump spout. You know how a drip goes, when it falls, it's round at the bottom and it trails off at the top? It's that. Lying down. The front's round, and it keeps the wind off, and the wind goes round it, along the sides, and off the back. And you're inside, in the still part. You can breathe. You can see." He held one out. "I tried it on fourth day. To Hollin and back, as fast as I could go. My eyes didn't water at all."

Tovey took it and turned it over.

"You made this," he said, "because I'd have asked."

"Well, I'd have asked," said Tor. "If I were you. And it was annoying me." He handed out the rest, one to each of them. "Put it on over the other one. They don't mind each other."

Tovey hung it inside his shirt and pressed his palm flat on it. He stood still a moment with his head slightly lifted, like a man listening for rain.

Half the court was watching now. A boy on the dormitory stair held a trunk in his arms and had forgotten to put it down.

Tor looked round at the four of them, and up at the sky, and settled his satchel.

"Right," he said, to himself.

He lifted his right hand and rose straight up, and the brown trunk rose after him on its plate. After a moment Tovey lifted his own hand and followed. Then the others. Below them, on the stair, the first-year began to cheer.

* * *

Three hours south they came down in a field outside a town called Ashby Market and walked in through the gate on a market day, five of them, with ten trunks following at knee height in a line.

The market stopped. A woman at a cheese stall put her hand over her mouth. A man selling ribbons sat down on his own stool. A boy ran shouting up an alley, and two streets away a bell began to ring, too fast for the hour. Somebody behind a cart said, "Austrans," and somebody else said, "*Austrans*, in brown?" A dog barked.

"Ah," said Rolph.

Tor's right hand went flat to his chest, over the shield, and stayed there. He looked at the cobbles in front of his feet.

A big red-faced man in an apron came out of the inn on the corner, wiping his hands, and strode halfway across the street before he saw Tovey and stopped. He looked at the cloak and the brooch, and at Tovey's face. Then his eyes moved on to Rolph, and stayed on him. His mouth opened slightly and shut. He looked back at Tovey, quickly. Tovey nodded, very slightly.

"My lord," said the innkeeper, and bowed to Tovey, and then turned and bowed to Rolph, a little lower. Sara watched him do it. "My lords. Ladies. You'll be wanting the back parlor. It's cool. I'll have it cleared." He turned and raised his voice to the street. "*It's Count Thompson.* Go and stop that bell, somebody. Go on."

The bell stopped. The street started up again, slowly. The ribbon-seller stayed sitting down.

"See?" said Rolph to Tor. "Nothing to it. Everybody knows Tovey."

Tor took his hand off his chest. "Yes," he said. "Of course."

In the back parlor Tor ate everything put in front of him, with his eyes on the window, where children stood three deep with their noses on the glass. Each time one pointed, he looked down at his plate. When the innkeeper came in with more cider, Tor stood up. When the girl came for the plates he stood up again, said "Thank you" three times, and helped her stack them. She went out scarlet and came back with a pie nobody had ordered.

"Tor," said Sara. "Sit down."

"Sorry."

"You don't have to stand up for the plates."

"I know. Sorry. It's only—" He sat, and lowered his voice. "They're all looking."

"They're looking at the trunks."

"They're looking at me *and* the trunks."

"Well," said Sara, "you brought them."

They stopped twice more. At a river town with a stone bridge, Tor stood in the square with his head on one side, looking at the bridge, until Trice walked back and took him by the sleeve.

"It's just stone," he said. "There's nothing holding it up. It's just *stone*, and it's the shape that does it."

At a posting house on the high road he came out of the privy looking stunned. "There was a *chain*," he told Rolph quietly. "You pull it, and the water—"

"Yes."

"Where does it *go*?"

Rolph said he had honestly never wondered. Tor looked at him for a long moment.

At every stop it went the same way: the bell or the shout or the barking, *Austrans*; then Tovey's face and brooch, and the faces round them changing; and then a second look, a longer one, at Rolph. Rolph did not appear to notice the second looks. Sara watched each one.

* * *

The high road led them south all morning. In the early afternoon it swung east into low hills, and Rolph said the quick way was to pick up the river on the far side and follow it down, because the capital sat on the river and you couldn't miss it.

They found a river and followed it for most of an hour. It widened and browned and then bent north.

"The capital," said Trice, "is not north."

"It's a different river," said Rolph.

"There's only one river."

"There's clearly two."

They hung over a reedy bend in a loose ring, with the trunks hanging below them, and argued. Tor said nothing. He was looking down. After a while he pointed. "There's a road."

Along the far bank, half hidden in poplars, ran a road, wide and new-graveled, with clean-cut ditches on either side. At its edge, at intervals, stood white stones. They landed beside one. It came to Tor's waist, square-cut, chamfered on top, and on the river side, in letters cut deep and filled with black, it said:

*THE CITY — 22*

Tor touched the letters and looked at Rolph.

"Twenty-two," he said.

"I told you it was the river," said Rolph.

"You said it was a different river."

"I said it was *a* river. I was right about it being a river."

They followed the road. The milestones counted down. Barges appeared on the river, and then many barges; farms gave way to market gardens, then to walled gardens, then to houses built on the walls. Tor flew at the front of the line, as he had since the hills, with the others close behind him in the still air.

At the twelve-mile stone, without turning round, Tor said, "What if they don't like them?"

"Like what?" said Sara.

"The things. In the trunk." He glanced back at it. "For Rolph's mother. They're only—they're just things I made. They're homemade. And she lives in the capital, she'll have all sorts, and I'll come in with a trunk of copper like a tinker—"

"Tor," said Rolph.

"—and they're not even *finished*, some of them, they're more like trials—"

"*Tor*. You made a room cold in the summer. She's going to eat you alive with questions. In the nicest way. You'll be a—" He waved a hand, and his trunk swung below him. "You'll be a sensation."

"As long," said Trice, "as you remember to talk."

"Talk?"

"To people. When they speak to you. You can't just stand at the back looking at the ceiling, working out what's holding it up."

"I don't do that."

"You did it to a *bridge*."

"As long as you talk," said Sara, "you'll be fine. You'll be more than fine. Just say good evening and answer what you're asked."

"And don't stand up every time somebody takes a plate," said Rolph.

"They'll like you," said Tovey, from the back of the line. "They'll like what you've made. And they'll like that you made it." A pause. "I'd be careful how much you give away, at the first house you go to. That's all."

Tor flew on in silence for some way.

"Good evening," he said under his breath, to the river. "Good evening. Thank you. Good evening."

* * *

The city came up out of the haze as a long low line of white along the river. The line grew towers, and the towers grew roofs, and the roofs went on up the hills on both banks and over them: white walls, red tile, green copper, gold, banners, smoke, the glitter of windows in the afternoon sun. Round all of it ran a wall, white, thick as a house, with a tower every hundred yards and a flag on every tower, down to the water on both sides.

Tor stopped in the air. His trunk swung gently behind him. His mouth was a little open. Rolph, beside him, was not looking at the city. He was looking at Tor.

"It's," said Tor, and stopped.

"Big," said Rolph.

"It's—how many people *live* in it?"

Rolph opened his mouth, and shut it again. "Lots."

"Two Rivers would go in it—" Tor counted on one hand. "Ten times. More. In one *corner*. Is it all stone?"

"Most of it. The old part is. It's white because the quarry's white. They're not trying to show off. Well." He considered. "They're trying a bit."

Closer, the color came out of it: blue and saffron and green awnings over the streets, window boxes, washing strung across the alleys, a whole hillside on the far bank painted pink and yellow and sky-blue. Tor began to smile, at nothing in particular, at all of it.

"Rolph," said Sara.

Six men were coming up off the wall from the nearest tower, fast and level, in a tight wedge. They wore red and white: white tunics, red sashes, red caps with white cockades. Each held a force lance in his left hand, a long black shaft with copper fittings at the head. Each man's right hand was closed on something small, strapped to hand and wrist with a crossing of buckled leather, like a falconer's glove.

Tor made a small noise. "That's—" he said. "Those are *mine*."

Sara kept her eyes straight ahead. Trice said, "Mm."

The guards halted in the air twenty yards off and the wedge opened smartly into a line. The man at the front was older, gray at the temples, with a captain's gold on his sash and a weathered face. He looked them over: five young people in school brown and school black and a noble's cloak, with ten trunks hanging behind them.

"Oh, look at the *trunks*," said the youngest guard, at the end of the line. "Sir, look, the trunks are—"

"Thank you, Daws," said the gray man, without turning his head. Daws went red and looked at the horizon.

The guards saluted. Lances went into the crooks of their arms, and six left fists came up to six right shoulders, and down. It was a salute done in a mirror. They did it together, crisply.

Tovey nodded. Trice nodded. Rolph nodded. Sara attempted a curtsey: a dip of the knee and the head that, with nothing under her feet, made her sink a foot and bob up again like a cork, so that she had to catch her balance with her free hand. Her ears went pink.

Tor watched each of them in turn. Then he drew himself up in the air and nodded, carefully, exactly as Tovey had. Then he added, clearly and courteously:

"Greetings."

The gray man's face did not move.

"Good afternoon," he said. "Curtis, captain of the river gate. I'm sorry to stop you. I'll need to know who you are, if you'd be so good."

Tovey moved a little forward in the air. Rolph stayed where he was, his face pleasant and slightly bored.

"Thompson," said Tovey. "Count. These are my party. The Ducharina Patricia Morgan, my cousin. Miss Sara Debri, of the Debri house. Scholar Torrance Baker, of Lairdgren." He turned his head slightly toward Rolph. "And Alphonse."

Rolph's face did not change. His hand, on the control, tightened.

"But we all call him Rolph," said Tovey smoothly, "at school, and when we're traveling." A pause. "For safety. You understand."

Captain Curtis looked at Rolph for one heartbeat. His back straightened by a hair. His left hand began to rise toward his shoulder again, and stopped.

"Of course, my lord," he said, and let the hand fall. "I understand entirely." He looked round at all of them. "Welcome to the capital. All of you. Welcome—" He paused, very slightly. "Welcome."

At the end of the line, Daws was staring at Rolph with his mouth open. The man beside him trod on his foot, which took some doing in the air.

Tor looked from the captain to Rolph and back, with a small line between his brows.

"Alphonse?" he said under his breath.

"Family name," said Rolph. "Awful, isn't it? Don't you dare."

"I think it's nice."

"You would."

"I'm afraid," said Captain Curtis, a little louder, "I'll have to ask you to land outside the wall. There's no flying permitted inside the city. Not yet." He lifted his strapped right hand, a little ruefully. "These are very new, my lord. There aren't any rules for them, and until there are, there's an order. We've had accidents already. A man went through the roof of the chandlers' hall on the second day. Another one put a chimney through a lady's bedroom on the Hill, and the lady was in it." His face did not change. "She has made a complaint."

"Was anyone hurt?" said Tor at once.

The captain looked at him properly for the first time.

"The chimney," said Captain Curtis, "was a total loss. If you'd follow me down to the river gate, my lord. Ladies. Sirs. You'll find a carriage."

* * *

In the shade of the gate arch three carriages were waiting, clean, with fresh horses: one for passengers and two long open wagons for luggage. Tor looked at them, and at the gate, and up at the wall, where a man in red and white was watching them through a spyglass. Then he climbed in.

It was hot. Inside the white walls, out of the wind, the air was close and heavy and smelled of river, horse, hot tile and frying. Every window in the carriage was down. Sara fanned herself with her hand. Trice sat bolt upright and did not fan herself. Tor sat at the window, his hair stuck to his forehead, and turned his head to follow everything they passed: awnings, fountains, a man selling ices from a barrow, a stone horse in a square, a woman with a birdcage on her head that turned out to be a hat.

At a crossroads they waited five minutes behind a dray.

"Flying in," said Tovey, "was far superior."

"To this?" said Trice. "A cart would be superior to this."

"To riding in. Every summer of my life I've come in by the road, and it's a week of dust and bad inns and losing a wheel at Barford. And today I've eaten lunch in three towns and arrived before the dinner bell." He looked out at the dray with deep personal grievance. "They'll all want to do it. Every house on the Hill. By the end of the summer, you mark me, there won't be a coach-maker in the city who isn't frightened."

"They'll have to change the order first," said Rolph.

"They'll change the order," said Tovey.

Rolph looked out of the other window and said nothing.

* * *

The Thompson house stood at the top of a steep street on the Hill: white, enormous, with a portico and a pair of stone dogs on the steps and a great many shuttered windows. Tor got down onto the hot pavement and tipped his head right back to look at it. His lips moved slightly; his eyes went along the rows of windows.

"It's not much," said Tovey. "It's only the town house. The third one. I'm hardly ever here. We open it in the autumn for the council of counts and shut it again when it's over." He was already climbing the steps. "It'll be cool inside."

Tor looked at the portico, and at Tovey's back, and then at Trice, who looked back at him with no expression at all.

The door opened before Tovey reached it. A tall, old, silver-haired man in black bowed perfectly. "My lord."

Behind him the hall was dim and cool and half the furniture was still under dust sheets. From somewhere deeper in the house came the sound of doors, running feet, a voice saying "*Today?*" in a strangled whisper, and something falling over. A maid crossed the far end of the hall at a run with her arms full of curtains. A gong began to sound, stopped, and began again more steadily.

"Dallow," said Tovey, peeling off his gloves. "We've come early."

"So I perceive, my lord," said Dallow. "How very pleasant."

He took their cloaks. A footman arrived, still buttoning his coat, to receive them.

Tor had gone pink. He stepped close to Trice and said, very low, "They didn't know."

"They didn't know *today*," said Trice. "They knew this week."

"But it's my fault. I said fly. If we'd come in the coach they'd have had a week. They'd have taken the sheets off." He looked at the dust sheets, and at Dallow. "I should apologize to him."

"You will *not*."

"I'd want somebody to apologize to me."

"You're not a butler," said Trice. "You're a guest. If you apologize to him it'll be the worst thing that's happened to him all year. Worse than the gong." She took his elbow. "Leave it. Dallow has been butler of this house since before Tovey could walk. He can survive five schoolchildren and a lot of trunks."

Tor kept looking at Dallow. The pink went out of his face, and the anxious line between his brows went with it. He tilted his head a little to one side.

"Oh," he said quietly. "He's *pleased*."

Trice looked at Dallow, who was directing the footman with a face like a closed door.

"He is not," she said. "He's furious. He's hiding it beautifully."

"No, he's pleased. He's ever so pleased." Tor was frowning, but not unhappily. "He's glad Tovey's back early. Underneath the—underneath. He's—I don't know. He's been worried. About the road. And now Tovey's here, and he's not on the road, and he's so pleased he can hardly—" He stopped and looked at Trice. "How do I know that?"

"You tell me."

"I *can* feel it. Like a—like warm. Coming off him." He glanced at Dallow again, quickly. "They talked about it in theory. In the second year. Some people can feel what someone's feeling, if it's strong. By the field. It's just the field. Everybody's got one, and if it's strong enough you can feel it, if you're the sort who can." He rubbed the back of his neck. "I never have. I always do the—the things. Things you can pick up. Copper. I never tried the other sort."

Trice looked at him for some time.

"Well," she said. "Don't tell Dallow."

* * *

A footman brought water into a long shuttered room at the back of the house, where the light came through the slats in bars across a cool stone floor and a fountain ran somewhere outside. A great glass jug sat on a silver tray, beaded with wet, with five glasses. In the jug, knocking against the glass when it was set down, were pieces of ice.

Tor stared at it. When Dallow had filled his glass he held it up to the barred light and watched the ice turn, and put a finger in, and took it out, and looked at the finger.

"Where do they get *ice*?" he said. "In the summer."

"Ice house," said Tovey. "Under the garden. They cut it off the lakes in the north in the winter and bring it down by barge and pack it in straw."

"All the way from the north?"

"All the way."

"And it lasts?"

"Most of it. Till about now." Tovey drank. "It gets expensive toward the end."

Tor looked at the ice. His eyes stopped moving. His glass tilted slowly in his hand, and water ran over his knuckles onto the floor.

"It's only the plate backwards," he said, to no one. "It's just the plate. The one on the wall. That takes the warm out and puts it in the ground. If you took it out of a box, a small box, and you didn't put it in the ground, you put it—it doesn't matter where you put it, you put it *out*—and you kept on, and there was a stop, so it didn't—then the box would go cold, and colder, and if you put water in it—" His free hand came up and drew a box in the air. "And the other way. If you took the warm out of the ground and put it all in a box, a small one, a closed one, and kept on—" His eyes widened. "That's an *oven*. That's an oven with no fire. You'd never have to—Mother'd never have to get up at the third bell to light the—"

"Tor," said Sara.

"—and you'd want two, side by side, one takes and one gives, then you'd only have to feed it the *difference*—"

"Tor."

He didn't answer. His hand drifted down to the arm of his chair and stayed there. His shoulders settled. His breathing slowed.

Trice leaned across, took a piece of ice out of the jug with her fingers, pulled out the back of his collar, and dropped it down his neck.

Tor made a noise like a kettle. He came out of the chair in one movement, clawing at the back of his tunic, and hopped across the stone floor while the ice went down his spine. Water went everywhere. His glass hit the floor without breaking and spun. Rolph put his face in his hands. Tovey looked at the ceiling. Sara coughed into her fist.

"*Trice!*"

"You were going," said Trice. "I brought you back."

"You could have *said*!"

"Sara said. Twice." She sat back with her own glass. "You didn't hear. You were making an oven."

Tor stood in the middle of the floor, wet down the back, the ice somewhere in his belt. His face was pink. He opened his mouth, shut it, and opened it again.

"Are all—" He stopped and began again, carefully. "I'm only asking. Are all noble ladies like this?"

"Like what?"

"*Violent*."

Rolph lifted his face from his hands. Tovey stopped looking at the ceiling. Sara stopped coughing.

"Because my sisters aren't," Tor went on earnestly. "Not really. Not most of them. Terlee shouts. But she doesn't *do* anything. And Tilda wouldn't hurt a fly, and Tessa's only little." He thought. "Teira hits. Teira hits quite a lot. But she's Teira. And none of them hits me as much as you do."

"I hit you in combat class," said Trice. "That's what combat class is *for*."

"You hit me in combat class," Tor agreed. "And at breakfast. With apples. And with a boot. And now ice."

"You *asked* me to throw things."

"I asked you to throw things. You did it with *enthusiasm*."

Trice looked at him over the rim of her glass for a moment. The tips of her ears had gone pink.

"Only the ones who like you," she said, and drank.

Tor looked at her, puzzled, and then laughed uncertainly, and sat down on the edge of his chair and fished the ice out of his belt.

Trice set her glass down. "Right," she said. "Tovey's here. The rest of us aren't stopping. Rolph's taking Tor home, and Sara and I are going to the Debri house, and Tovey can take his own sheets off." She stood. "Tomorrow. Luncheon. At the Debri house, all of us. At the third bell after noon, which is luncheon in the capital, Master Baker, so don't come at noon and stand on the step." She looked at him. "And don't make an oven before then."

"I wasn't going to *make* it."

"Then don't think about it."

Tor opened his mouth, and closed it again, and said nothing.

Trice went to see about the trunks.
