Tovey had the coach brought round to the main court at the fifth bell, because the roads south were good and the inns were better if you reached them before dark, and he had been reaching them before dark every summer since he was eleven.

It was the Thompson traveling coach, dark green, with the arms on the door painted small, the way his father had liked them. Four bays. Two men on the box and one up behind. The luggage rail on the roof was empty and waiting. Tovey stood beside it in his plain dark cloak, in the cool gray of the morning, and watched the court fill up with the end of term: trunks being dragged down stairs, first-years crying, somebody's mother in a hat. He had his gloves in one hand. He had his list in his head: Patricia's trunks, Rolph's trunks, Miss Debri's trunk, Scholar Baker's trunk, if Scholar Baker owned a trunk, which he doubted.

Patricia arrived first, with three.

"Three," said Tovey.

"Three," said Patricia. "One for clothes, one for the other clothes, and one for the things I'm bringing back for people who will be cross if I don't. Is there a problem?"

"No."

"Good."

Rolph came next, with four, and Miss Debri with one and a satchel, and they stood about in the court in the early light the way people stand about at the beginning of a long journey, saying nothing much, while the men began to lift the first of Patricia's trunks to the rail.

Then Scholar Baker came round the corner of the long hall, and the men stopped lifting.

He was walking quite briskly, in his brown, with his old belt round his middle and a stuffed satchel on his back. Behind him, at the height of a man's knee, in a line, came ten flat copper plates. Nine were empty. They bobbed along after him in perfect order, a little apart, like a string of ducklings, and when he came round the corner they came round it too, one after the other, swinging wide on the turn. The tenth carried a trunk. It was an old trunk, brown, with the corners mended in a different leather, and the plate under it rode a little lower than the others and seemed, if a plate could, to be working harder.

He stopped in front of the coach. The plates stopped behind him.

"Good morning," said Scholar Baker. "Sorry I'm late. I had to finish the—" He looked at the coach, and at the four bays, and at the men on the box, and then at Tovey, and his face went pink and apologetic all at once. "Oh. Oh, I'm sorry. I should have said. I thought Rolph had said."

"Said what?" said Rolph.

"We're flying," said Scholar Baker.

There was a pause in the court. It spread outward. A first-year stopped crying to look.

"I made plates," said Scholar Baker, in case anybody had missed them. "For the luggage. That's what they're for. You put the trunk on, and you keep the button, and it follows you. It'll follow you up. I tried it on the stair." He was undoing a small cloth bag from his belt. "I thought we'd fly. It's quicker. And you all have your belts. Unless—" He looked round at them, suddenly anxious. "Unless you'd rather not. I don't mind the coach. I just thought."

Tovey looked at his coach. He looked at the luggage rail. He looked at the ten plates, waiting at knee height in a patient line, and at his cousin, who had already turned to look at her three trunks with an expression of great calm.

"Well," said Patricia. "In that case, Master Baker, you'll be carrying all three of mine."

"Of course."

"All three. All the way."

"I don't carry them. They carry themselves. You just have to keep the—"

"I heard *carry*," said Patricia. "I'm holding you to *carry*. Two of them are mostly shoes."

Scholar Baker looked at her with the faintly hunted expression he wore whenever Patricia was being Patricia, and then knelt in the dust of the court and began, very quickly and very neatly, to strap trunks to plates.

He did it the way Tovey had seen him do everything: as if it were the only thing in the world. Patricia's three. Rolph's four. Miss Debri's one. Tovey's own, last, fetched down off the coach's boot by his man, who handed it over as though surrendering a sword. A buckle each side and one across, and the plates sank a little as the weight came onto them and then rose again to knee height and stayed. When each trunk was fast he took a small copper disc from the bag, no bigger than a coat button, with a hole for a cord, and pressed his thumb to it, and to the plate, and then handed the button to the trunk's owner.

"Keep it on you," he said to each of them. "In a pocket. If you put it down, it'll stay with the button, so don't put it down anywhere you're not going to be."

He gave Patricia hers last. She weighed it in her palm, and then put it away somewhere inside her black so fast Tovey didn't see where.

"And what's in yours?" said Rolph, looking at the old brown trunk on its laboring plate. "Stones?"

"Copper," said Scholar Baker. "Mostly. Things I've made. For your mother." He went pink again. "And some spares. In case anybody needs anything. You never know, at a place you haven't been."

"It's riding low."

"It's heavy," said Scholar Baker, apologetically, to the trunk.

Tovey dismissed the coach. He did it in four words to the man on the box, who had been his father's man and touched his hat and showed nothing at all, and the coach went away round the long hall at a walk, empty, with the luggage rail empty, and the bays shaking their heads.

Then he turned back to Scholar Baker and said the thing he had been deciding whether to say.

"Scholar Baker. I've tried it fast."

Scholar Baker looked up from the last buckle.

"On the belt. Over the river, ten days ago." Tovey kept his eyes on a point a little above Scholar Baker's head, which was where he kept them when he was admitting something. "It gets cold. Very cold. You can't breathe; the air comes at you too quickly to take any. And my eyes watered until I couldn't see where I was going. I came down in a field I didn't know." He paused. "I'm not afraid of it. I'd like to know how far it is to the capital, at a speed where I can see."

"Oh!" said Scholar Baker, and his whole face cleared, as if Tovey had handed him a present. "Oh, no, it's all right. I know. I did that too, the first time. I couldn't breathe all the way to Hollin." He was rummaging in his satchel. "That's the shield. It lets the air through, so it lets the wind through, all of it, straight at you. So I made a new one." He came up with a handful of copper plates on cords, the size of a palm, like the shields, but with a different line etched across them, long and sweeping. "It's the same as the old one at the back. But at the front it's—" He drew it in the air with one finger: round at the front, long and narrow behind, like a seed. "It's the shape of a drip. Off the pump spout. You know how a drip goes, when it falls, it's round at the bottom and it trails off at the top? It's that. Lying down. The front's round, and it keeps the wind off, and the wind goes round it, along the sides, and off the back. And you're inside, in the still part. You can breathe. You can see." He held one out. "I tried it on fourth day. To Hollin and back, as fast as I could go. My eyes didn't water at all."

Tovey took it. He turned it over. The new line ran across the copper like the stroke of a brush.

"You made this," he said, "because I'd have asked."

"Well, I'd have asked," said Scholar Baker. "If I were you. And it was annoying me." He handed out the rest: one to Rolph, one to Miss Debri, one to Patricia. "Put it on over the other one. They don't mind each other."

Tovey put it on over the other one. He hung it inside his shirt, against his chest, and pressed his hand flat on it, and felt it come on: the faint, close stillness of the air drawing in round him, and then something else, a sort of leaning, a sense that the air in front of him had been asked to step aside and was thinking about it.

The court was entirely silent now. Half of it had stopped to watch. A boy on the dormitory stair had a trunk in his arms and had forgotten to put it down.

Scholar Baker looked round at all of them, and then at the sky, and settled his satchel, and said, quite quietly, mostly to himself, "Right."

Then he lifted his right hand, and went up, and the brown trunk went up after him on its heavy plate, and after a moment Tovey lifted his own hand and followed, and the court fell away under his boots, and somewhere below him, very small, the first-year on the stair began to cheer.

* * *

They stopped, the first time, at a town called Ashby Market, three hours south, because Rolph said that if he didn't eat something soon he would fall out of the sky and it would be on Tor's conscience. Sara rather thought it would have been, too.

It was a market town on a market day. They came down in a field outside it, all five of them and their trunks, and walked in, and the trunks followed, in a line, at knee height, through the gate, and the market stopped.

Sara had been stared at before. She was a Debri, and she had walked through the glass halls on her father's arm since she was nine, and she knew what it was to have a room turn round. This was different. This was a whole street turning round. A woman at a cheese stall put her hand over her mouth. A man selling ribbons simply sat down. A boy ran, shouting, up an alley, and two streets away a bell began to ring, not the hour, something faster, and somebody behind a cart said quite clearly, "Austrans," and somebody else said, "*Austrans*, in brown?" and a dog began to bark.

They stood in the middle of the street with their trunks behind them.

"Ah," said Rolph.

Tor had gone very still. Sara saw his hand go to his chest, flat, over the shield, and stay there. He was looking at the ground in front of him. He looked like a boy who had walked into the wrong house and was waiting for someone to tell him so.

Then a big red-faced man in an apron came out of the inn on the corner, wiping his hands, very much on the warpath, and got halfway across the street, and saw Tovey, and stopped.

He took in the cloak. He took in the brooch. He took in the height, and the face, and something in his own face changed gear. And then his eyes went on, to Rolph, and stayed there. They stayed there quite a long time. The man's mouth opened a little. He shut it. He looked at Tovey again, quickly, as if for permission, and Tovey gave him the smallest nod in the world.

"My lord," said the innkeeper, to Tovey, and bowed, and then turned and bowed to Rolph too, a little lower, which Sara noticed, and filed, and put away. "My lords. Ladies. You'll be wanting the back parlor. It's cool. I'll have it cleared." He turned to the street and raised his voice. "*It's Count Thompson*," he told it, in the tone of a man saying *it's only the cat*. "Go and stop that bell, somebody. Go on."

The bell stopped. The street started again, slowly, though nobody went back to quite what they'd been doing. The ribbon-seller was still sitting down.

"See?" said Rolph, cheerfully, to Tor. "Nothing to it. Everybody knows Tovey."

Tor took his hand off his chest. "Yes," he said. "Of course."

In the back parlor Sara watched him not eat. That wasn't fair: he ate. He ate everything put in front of him, the way he always did, with the care of a boy who had grown up knowing what bread cost. But he ate it with his eyes on the window. There were faces at the window. Children, mostly, three deep, with their noses on the glass, and every time one of them pointed, Tor's eyes went down to his plate. When the innkeeper came in with more cider, Tor stood up. When the girl came for the plates, he stood up again, and said "Thank you" to her three times, and helped her stack them, and she went out scarlet and came back with a pie nobody had ordered.

"Tor," said Sara. "Sit down."

"Sorry."

"You don't have to stand up for the plates."

"I know. Sorry. It's only—" He sat. He looked at the window, at the noses, and then at her, and lowered his voice. "They're all looking."

"They're looking at the trunks."

"They're looking at me *and* the trunks."

"Well," said Sara, "you brought them."

They stopped twice more. Once at a river town with a stone bridge and a church with a gold weathercock, where Tor stood in the square and stared at the bridge for so long, with his head on one side, that Trice had to go back for him. "It's just stone," he said, when she took his sleeve. "There's nothing holding it up. It's just *stone*, and it's the shape that does it." And once at a posting house on the high road where they used the facilities, and Tor came out of them looking stunned, and said to Rolph, very quietly, "There was a *chain*. You pull it, and the water—" and Rolph said, "Yes," and Tor said, "Where does it *go*?" and Rolph said he had honestly never wondered, and Tor looked at him as though he'd confessed to never having wondered where the sun went at night.

At every stop it was the same. The bell, or the shout, or the barking. *Austrans.* And then Tovey, and the brooch, and the faces changing. And then, every time, after Tovey, a second look, a longer one, at Rolph.

Sara watched the second looks. She watched Rolph not notice them, with the ease of long practice. She said nothing at all, and wrote nothing down, and kept them.

* * *

The last stretch was the long one.

The high road had led them south all morning, which was easy; you followed the road. But in the early afternoon it had swung east to cross a range of low hills, and Rolph said the road went the long way round and that the quick way was to pick up the river on the far side and follow it down, because the capital sat on the river, everybody knew that, you couldn't miss it.

They found a river. They followed it for most of an hour. It grew wider and browner and more pleased with itself, and then it turned north, and Trice said, "The capital is not north."

"It's a different river," said Rolph.

"There's only one river."

"There's clearly two."

They hung in the air over a bend full of reeds, five of them in a loose ring with the trunks hanging below them like the weights of a clock, and argued about rivers. Tor didn't join in. He was looking down. After a while he said, "There's a road," and pointed, and there was: a road along the far bank, half hidden in poplars. They went down to look. It was a good road, wide and new-graveled, with ditches cut clean on both sides and not a single rut, and at the edge of it, every so often, there stood a stone.

They landed beside one. It came up to Tor's waist, white, square-cut, with the top chamfered, and on the river side it said, in letters cut deep and filled with black:

*THE CITY — 22*

Tor touched the letters. He looked at Rolph.

"Twenty-two," he said.

"I told you it was the river," said Rolph.

"You said it was a different river."

"I said it was *a* river. I was right about it being a river."

They followed the road. The milestones went down: twenty, eighteen, fifteen. The river went wide and slow and gray-green, with barges on it, and then more barges, and then a great many. The land on either side began to fill up, as land does near a city: first farms, then market gardens, then walls round the market gardens, then houses on the walls.

At twelve, Tor said, without turning round, "What if they don't like them?"

He said it to the air in front of him. They were flying in a line, and he was at the front, as he had been all day, so that the rest of them could fly in the still air behind him, the way geese do; he'd worked that out somewhere over the hills, and been pleased about it for nearly an hour.

"Like what?" said Sara.

"The things. In the trunk." He glanced back at it, riding low behind him. "For Rolph's mother. They're only—they're just things I made. They're homemade. And she lives in the capital, she'll have all sorts, and I'll come in with a trunk of copper like a tinker—"

"Tor," said Rolph.

"—and they're not even *finished*, some of them, they're more like trials—"

"*Tor*. You made a room cold in the summer. She's going to eat you alive with questions. In the nicest way. You'll be a—" He waved a hand, and the trunk under him swung. "You'll be a sensation."

"As long," said Trice, "as you remember to talk."

"Talk?"

"To people. When they speak to you. You can't just stand at the back looking at the ceiling, working out what's holding it up."

"I don't do that."

"You did it to a *bridge*."

"As long as you talk," said Sara, "you'll be fine. You'll be more than fine. Just say good evening and answer what you're asked."

"And don't stand up every time somebody takes a plate," said Rolph.

"They'll like you," said Tovey, from the back of the line. It was the first thing he'd said for half an hour. "They'll like what you've made. And they'll like that you made it." He paused. "I'd be careful how much you give away, at the first house you go to. That's all."

Tor flew in silence for a while.

"Good evening," he said, at last, under his breath, to the river. "Good evening. Thank you. Good evening."

* * *

Rolph saw it before Tor did, because he knew where to look.

It came up out of the haze ahead the way it always did, as a line first: a long, low line of white along the river, too straight to be cliff and too long to be anything else, and then the line grew a top, and the top grew towers, and the towers grew roofs, and the roofs went on and on and on, up the hills on both sides of the river and over them, roofs on roofs, white walls and red tiles and green copper and gold, and banners, and smoke, and the glitter of a thousand windows in the afternoon sun.

Rolph didn't look at the city. He'd seen it. He looked at Tor.

Tor had stopped. He hung there in the air at the front of the line with his trunk swinging gently behind him and his mouth a little open, and he didn't say anything at all. Rolph watched his eyes go from one end of it to the other, and back, and then up, and then very slowly along the wall: the great white wall, thick as a house, that ran round the whole of it and down to the river on both sides and had towers on it every hundred yards, with flags.

"It's," said Tor. He stopped.

"Big," said Rolph.

"It's—how many people *live* in it?"

Rolph opened his mouth to give him the figure from the last count, and shut it again. "Lots," he said.

"Two Rivers would go in it—" Tor was counting, with one hand. "Ten times. More. In one *corner*." He looked at the white of it. "Is it all stone?"

"Most of it. The old part is. It's white because the quarry's white. They're not trying to show off. Well." He considered. "They're trying a bit."

It wasn't all white, close to. As they came on, and down, the color came out of it like a painting being finished: awnings over the streets in blue and saffron and green, red tile, window boxes, washing hung across the alleys in a hundred colors, a whole hillside of houses painted pink and yellow and sky-blue on the far bank where the dyers lived, and over all of it, the flags.

Tor was smiling. Not at anything. Just smiling, in the air, at the whole of it, the way he'd smiled at Hollin, Rolph supposed, though Rolph hadn't been there for that.

Then Sara said, "Rolph," in her careful voice, and Rolph looked where she was looking, and saw the men coming up off the wall.

There were six of them. They came up from the nearest tower in a tight wedge, fast and level and well-handled, and they were in red and white: the city guard's summer uniform, white tunic, red sash, red cap with a white cockade. Each man carried a force lance in his left hand, a long black shaft with the copper fittings at the head that Rolph had last seen on a parade ground and had not much wanted to see again. Each man's right hand was closed round something small, bound to the hand and wrist with a sturdy crossing of leather, buckled, like a falconer's glove.

Rolph knew what was in those right hands. So did Tor; Rolph heard him make a small noise.

"That's—" Tor said. "Those are *mine*."

Nobody answered him. Sara was looking straight ahead. Trice said, "Mm."

The guards came to a halt in the air some twenty yards off, all together, smartly, and the wedge opened into a line. They were good. Somebody had drilled them; not for long, but hard. The man at the front was older, gray at the temples, with a captain's gold on his sash and a face that had been out in a lot of weather. He looked them over: five young people in school brown and school black and a dark noble's cloak, and a train of trunks hanging in the air behind them like washing.

"Oh, look at the *trunks*," said one of the guards at the end of the line, a young one, in pure delight. "Sir, look, the trunks are—"

"Thank you, Daws," said the gray man, without turning his head, and Daws went red and looked at the horizon.

Then the guards saluted.

They did it all together, and they did it with their left hands, because their right hands were spoken for: the lance shifted to the crook of the arm and the left fist brought up to the right shoulder, smartly, and down. It looked wrong. It looked like a salute in a mirror. Rolph saw that they knew it looked wrong, and had practiced it anyway, and were proud of it.

Tovey nodded, gravely. Trice nodded. Rolph nodded. Sara, beside him, did something Rolph had never seen anyone do before, which was attempt a curtsey in the air: a little dip of the knee and the head, which, with nothing under her feet, became a sort of slow bob, so that she sank a foot and came up again like a cork, and had to catch her balance with her free hand, and went pink to the ears.

And Tor, who had been watching all of this with enormous attention, looked from Tovey's nod to Trice's nod to Rolph's nod, and drew himself up in the air, and nodded too, very carefully, exactly as they had. And then, because he was Tor, and a nod on its own clearly wasn't enough, added, in a clear and courteous voice:

"Greetings."

The gray man's face didn't move at all. Rolph admired it very much.

"Good afternoon," said the gray man. "Curtis, captain of the river gate. I'm sorry to stop you. I'll need to know who you are, if you'd be so good."

Tovey moved forward a little in the air. It was his place to; he was the highest rank anyone was going to admit to. Rolph let him, and kept his own face very pleasant and very slightly bored, the face of a merchant's son on a long journey.

"Thompson," said Tovey. "Count. These are my party. The Ducharina Patricia Morgan, my cousin. Miss Sara Debri, of the Debri house. Scholar Torrance Baker, of Lairdgren." He turned his head, very slightly, toward Rolph. "And Alphonse."

Rolph felt it go through him, the name, the way his name always did in the open air: like hearing somebody behind you say your own thoughts aloud.

"But we all call him Rolph," said Tovey, quite smoothly, "at school, and when we're traveling." He paused, a well-judged pause. "For safety. You understand."

Captain Curtis looked at Rolph.

He looked at him for perhaps one heartbeat. It was not a long look. Nobody who didn't know what to look for would have seen anything in it at all. But Rolph had been looked at like that by men in red and white since before he could walk, and he saw the captain's back straighten by the width of a hair, and his left hand start to come up again, all by itself, toward his shoulder, and stop.

"Of course, my lord," said Captain Curtis. He let the hand fall. "I understand entirely." He looked round at all of them. "Welcome to the capital. All of you. Welcome—" There was the faintest catch in it, as if the word *home* had been at the back of his mouth and had been sent away again. "Welcome."

Daws, at the end of the line, was staring at Rolph with his mouth open. The man next to him trod on his foot. It was difficult to tread on a foot in the air. He managed it.

Beside Rolph, Tor was looking from the captain to Rolph and back again with a small, puzzled line between his brows.

"Alphonse?" he said, under his breath.

"Family name," said Rolph. "Awful, isn't it? Don't you dare."

"I think it's nice."

"You would."

"I'm afraid," said Captain Curtis, raising his voice a little, "that I'll have to ask you to land outside the wall. There's no flying permitted inside the city. Not yet." He lifted his bound right hand, a little ruefully. "These are very new, my lord. There aren't any rules for them, and until there are, there's an order. We've had accidents already. A man went through the roof of the chandlers' hall on the second day. Another one put a chimney through a lady's bedroom on the Hill, and the lady was in it." His face did not change. "She has made a complaint."

"Was anyone hurt?" said Tor, at once.

The captain looked at him properly for the first time.

"The chimney," said Captain Curtis, "was a total loss. If you'd follow me down to the river gate, my lord. Ladies. Sirs. You'll find a carriage."

* * *

There was more than one carriage. There were three, waiting in the shade of the gate arch, all of them clean, with fresh horses, as if they had been standing there since morning; one for the five of them, and two long open wagons for the trunks. Tor looked at the carriages, and then at the gate, and then up at the wall above it, where a man in red and white was watching them with a spyglass, and Rolph saw him decide, visibly, that this was simply how the capital was. Rolph didn't correct him.

It was hot.

It had been warm in the air, with the wind going round the shields, but on the ground, out of the wind, inside the white walls, the capital in summer came down on them like a lid. The stones gave it back. The air smelled of river and horse and hot tile and somebody's frying. In the carriage, even with every window down, it was like sitting in a bread oven with the door shut. Sara fanned herself with her hand. Trice sat perfectly upright and refused to acknowledge it. Tor sat by the window with his hair stuck to his forehead again and looked at everything, every single thing, all the way: the streets, the awnings, the fountains, a man selling ices from a barrow, a great stone horse in a square, a woman with a bird in a cage on her head, which turned out to be a hat.

Tovey bore it like a soldier. "Flying in," he said, at a crossroads, where they'd been stopped for five minutes behind a dray, "was far superior."

"To this?" said Trice. "A cart would be superior to this."

"To riding in. Every summer of my life I've come in by the road, and it's a week of dust and bad inns and losing a wheel at Barford. And today I've eaten lunch in three towns and arrived before the dinner bell." He looked out of the window, at the dray, with an expression of deep and personal grievance. "They'll all want to do it. Every house on the Hill. By the end of the summer, you mark me, there won't be a coach-maker in the city who isn't frightened."

"They'll have to change the order first," said Rolph.

"They'll change the order," said Tovey, with total confidence, and Rolph, who knew exactly who would have to be asked to change it, looked out of the other window and said nothing.

* * *

The Thompson house stood on the Hill, at the end of a short, steep street of other large houses, all of them pretending not to look at each other. It was white, like everything, and enormous, and had a portico, and a pair of stone dogs on the steps, and a great many windows with their shutters closed against the heat.

Tor got out of the carriage and stood on the hot pavement and looked up at it with his neck bent right back.

"It's not much," said Tovey. "It's only the town house. The third one. I'm hardly ever here. We open it in the autumn for the council of counts and shut it again when it's over." He was already going up the steps. "It'll be cool inside."

Trice, getting down after Tor, watched him take that in. She saw him look at the portico, and the dogs, and the windows, and count the windows, she was nearly sure, and arrive at a number, and then look at Tovey's back going up the steps with an expression of the purest bewilderment, as though Tovey had pointed at a cathedral and called it a shed.

Then the door opened, and it became clear that nobody in the house had expected them for a week.

It wasn't anything anyone said. The man who opened the door was tall and old and silver-haired, in black, and he bowed to Tovey quite perfectly and said "My lord," as if he had been standing behind the door waiting for this moment all his life. But behind him, in the cool dim of the hall, there was a sound like a wave going back down a beach: doors, and feet, and a voice saying "*Today?*" in a strangled whisper, and something falling over. Half the furniture in the hall was still under dust sheets. A maid went past the far end of it at a run, with her arms full of curtains. Somebody, somewhere deep in the house, began to bang on a gong, stopped, and started again in a more orderly way.

"Dallow," said Tovey, peeling off his gloves. "We've come early."

"So I perceive, my lord," said the silver-haired man. "How very pleasant."

He took their cloaks. His face showed nothing at all. Trice had been raised among faces like it, and she knew how much work went into one, and how much it cost. She could see the cost on this one, faintly, the way you can see a seam in good plaster if you know it's there.

Beside her, Tor had gone pink. He stepped closer to her and said, very low, appalled, "They didn't know."

"They didn't know *today*," said Trice. "They knew this week."

"But it's my fault. I said fly. If we'd come in the coach they'd have had a week. They'd have taken the sheets off." He looked at the dust sheets, and at the maid's disappearing back, and at Dallow, who was handing their cloaks to a footman who had arrived buttoning his coat. "I should apologize to him."

"You will *not*."

"I'd want somebody to apologize to me."

"You're not a butler," said Trice. "You're a guest. If you apologize to him it'll be the worst thing that's happened to him all year. Worse than the gong." She took his elbow. "Leave it. Dallow has been butler of this house since before Tovey could walk. He can survive five schoolchildren and a lot of trunks."

Tor went on looking at Dallow, and something happened to his face that Trice didn't understand. The pink went out of it. The anxious line went out of it too. He tilted his head a little to one side, the way he did at the pell when Kolb swung, watching hands rather than faces, and then he said, in quite a different voice, quietly, almost to himself:

"Oh. He's *pleased*."

Trice looked at Dallow. Dallow was directing the footman about the cloaks, with his face as smooth and closed as a shut door.

"He is not," she said. "He's furious. He's hiding it beautifully."

"No, he's pleased. He's ever so pleased." Tor was frowning now, but not unhappily: the frown of a boy who has found something in his pocket that he didn't put there. "He's glad Tovey's back early. Underneath the—underneath. He's—I don't know. He's been worried. About the road. And now Tovey's here, and he's not on the road, and he's so pleased he can hardly—" He stopped. He looked at Trice. "How do I know that?"

"You tell me."

"I *can* feel it. Like a—like warm. Coming off him." He glanced at Dallow again, quickly, as if afraid of being caught at it. "They talked about it in theory. In the second year. Some people can feel what someone's feeling, if it's strong. By the field. It's just the field. Everybody's got one, and if it's strong enough you can feel it, if you're the sort who can." He sounded half fascinated and half embarrassed, as if he'd been caught reading somebody's letters. "I never have. I always do the—the things. Things you can pick up. Copper. I never tried the other sort."

Sara had told her once that he'd known Douglas Sorvey was dangerous before anybody said a word to him. Trice had filed it at the time. She took it out now and put it next to this, and she looked at him, and she said nothing at all, because she had nothing yet to say.

"Well," she said. "Don't tell Dallow."

* * *

The servants brought water.

They brought it into a long shuttered room at the back of the house where the light came through the slats in bars and the floor was cool, polished stone, and a fountain ran somewhere outside in a courtyard. A footman carried it on a silver tray: a great glass jug, beaded on the outside with wet, and five glasses. And in the jug, knocking against the sides when he set it down, there were pieces of ice.

Tor stared at it.

He stared at it as if it were the city all over again. He picked up his glass when Dallow had filled it, and held it up to the barred light, and looked at the ice turning in it, and put his finger in, and took it out, and looked at the finger.

"Where do they get *ice*?" he said. "In the summer."

"Ice house," said Tovey. "Under the garden. They cut it off the lakes in the north in the winter and bring it down by barge and pack it in straw."

"All the way from the north?"

"All the way."

"And it lasts?"

"Most of it. Till about now." Tovey drank. "It gets expensive toward the end."

Tor looked at the ice.

Trice saw it start. She'd seen it start before, in his room, at a window, over a teacup. It began in the eyes: they stopped looking at the ice and started looking *through* it, at something behind it that nobody else could see. His glass tilted, slowly, in his hand. A trickle of water ran over his knuckles and he didn't notice.

"It's only the plate backwards," he said, to no one. "It's just the plate. The one on the wall. That takes the warm out and puts it in the ground. If you took it out of a box, a small box, and you didn't put it in the ground, you put it—it doesn't matter where you put it, you put it *out*—and you kept on, and there was a stop, so it didn't—then the box would go cold, and colder, and if you put water in it—" His free hand had come up, and was drawing a box in the air. "And the other way. If you took the warm out of the ground and put it all in a box, a small one, a closed one, and kept on—" His eyes went wider. "That's an *oven*. That's an oven with no fire. You'd never have to—Mother'd never have to get up at the third bell to light the—"

"Tor," said Sara.

"—and you'd want two, side by side, one takes and one gives, then you'd only have to feed it the *difference*—"

"Tor."

He didn't hear her. His eyes had gone still. Trice watched his shoulders begin to settle, the way they settled at the desk before a long night, and his hand drift down to the arm of the chair and stay there, and his breathing slow, and she thought, quite clearly: *not here. Not on the first day. Not in Tovey's back room with Dallow watching and four days of broth in front of me.*

She leaned across, took a piece of ice out of the jug with her fingers, pulled out the back of his collar, and dropped it down his neck.

Tor made a noise like a kettle.

He came out of the chair in one movement, all the way up, clawing at the back of his tunic, and danced, there was no other word for it, on the cool stone floor, while the ice went down his spine. The water went everywhere. His glass went on the floor, and didn't break, and spun. Rolph put his face in his hands. Tovey looked at the ceiling. Sara made a small, strangled noise and pretended it was a cough.

"*Trice!*"

"You were going," said Trice calmly. "I brought you back."

"You could have *said*!"

"Sara said. Twice." She sat back and picked up her own glass. "You didn't hear. You were making an oven."

Tor stood in the middle of the floor, damp to the waist at the back, with the ice somewhere in his belt, and looked at her. His face was quite pink. He opened his mouth, and shut it, and opened it again.

"Are all—" he said. He stopped and tried again, carefully, the way he did when he was afraid of giving offense and determined to know anyway. "I'm only asking. Are all noble ladies like this?"

"Like what?"

"*Violent*."

Rolph took his face out of his hands. Tovey stopped looking at the ceiling. Sara stopped coughing.

"Because my sisters aren't," Tor went on, earnestly. "Not really. Not most of them. Terlee shouts. But she doesn't *do* anything. And Tilda wouldn't hurt a fly, and Tessa's only little." He thought. "Teira hits. Teira hits quite a lot. But she's Teira. And none of them hits me as much as you do."

"I hit you in combat class," said Trice. "That's what combat class is *for*."

"You hit me in combat class," Tor agreed. "And at breakfast. With apples. And with a boot. And now ice."

"You *asked* me to throw things."

"I asked you to throw things. You did it with *enthusiasm*."

Trice regarded him over the rim of her glass. He was standing there dripping, quite seriously awaiting an answer, and she found, to her annoyance, that her ears had gone warm.

"Only the ones who like you," she said, and drank.

There was a short silence. Tor looked at her, puzzled, as if she'd answered a question in a language he could only nearly follow, and then laughed, uncertainly, the way he laughed at a joke he hadn't quite got, and sat down on the edge of his chair, and fished the ice out of his belt.

Trice put the glass down. "Right," she said briskly. "Tovey's here. The rest of us aren't stopping. Rolph's taking Tor home, and Sara and I are going to the Debri house, and Tovey can take his own sheets off." She stood up. "Tomorrow. Luncheon. At the Debri house, the third bell after noon, which is luncheon in the capital, Master Baker, so don't come at noon and stand on the step."

"You're all coming?" said Tor.

"Tovey has his own business," said Trice, without looking at her cousin, who inclined his head gravely, as if it had been his idea. "And I have mine. Sara wants you to herself, Master Baker. It's a working lunch." She glanced at Rolph. "You may bring him. Try not to talk."

"Thank you so much," said Rolph.

Sara said nothing at all. She was looking at Tor, and she had her contract face on, the one Trice had known for nine years, and under it, very faintly, something that wasn't contract at all.

Trice looked back at Tor. "And don't make an oven before then."

"I wasn't going to *make* it."

"Then don't think about it."

Tor opened his mouth to say that he couldn't promise that, and Trice could see him decide it would be a lie, and close it again, and that was answer enough.

She went to see about the trunks.
