Rolph talked the whole way across the city: the corn exchange, the river stairs where you could buy eels, the house with the blue door where a man had once kept a bear, which fountain was lucky and which one people only said was lucky. Then the carriage turned out of the noise into a long, quiet, tree-lined street with a white wall running down one side of it, higher than a house and without a single window, and Rolph stopped talking. He straightened his collar. A little later he straightened it again.

Tor watched him.

"Are you all right?"

"Fine. Hot."

"You've gone quiet."

"I'm saving it," said Rolph. "For later."

Tor thought about that. Then he said, gravely, "I'll talk to people. I promise. I won't stand at the back."

Rolph looked at him for a moment.

"I know you will," he said.

* * *

The carriage stopped at a gate set back in an arch in the long white wall: plain oak bound with black iron, a carter's gate. Two men stood in front of it and two more in the shade of the arch behind. All four wore purple and black, with short black rods at their belts.

Rolph got down. Tor got down after him and stood on the hot cobbles, looking at the guards. His eyes went to the purple tunics, the black sashes, the rods. He went still.

"Those are," he said quietly. "That's the royal—"

He stopped. He frowned, hard, as if at a sigil that wouldn't come right. He looked at the guards, and up at the wall, and along it. Then the frown cleared and he nodded to himself.

"Oh, of course. You'd be quite near it here. The palace. They'd have to watch the whole street."

Beside him, Rolph let out a breath.

The oldest of the four guards came out of the arch: broad, gray, with a face like a good saddle.

"Young sir," he said.

Rolph's back straightened. His chin came up a fraction. When he answered, his voice was slower and smoother than it had been all day, the vowels longer.

"George. Good afternoon. I trust we're not unexpected."

"Not at all, young sir. You're on the list." George's eyes moved past him. "And this will be the scholar."

"Scholar Baker. My guest for the summer. He'll want to come and go as he pleases."

"Very good, young sir."

Tor made a small swallowed sound. His mouth stayed perfectly straight.

"Ah," he said. Then he lifted his chin exactly as Rolph had, drew himself up to his full small height, and spoke in a voice that was Tovey's to the last long vowel, with Tovey's faint, unbothered boredom at the end of it.

"Do tell me, Rolph. Is that the house?" He nodded graciously at the roof that showed above the wall, a long gray-slated roof with a row of tall chimneys. Further off, above the trees, rose more roofs, and towers, and a dome. "The *small* one. I do so hope I'm to be put up in the small house. I'd feel quite at home."

Rolph stared at him. George's face did not change.

"Well, we call it the small house," said Rolph blankly, in his own voice. "Everybody does. It's smaller than the—" He waved at the dome. "How did you know?"

Tor's composure went. He put his hand over his mouth.

"It's the *privy*," he said through it. "At home. The small house is the *privy*, Rolph. Out the back, by the woodpile. With the moon cut in the door." And he began to laugh, helplessly, in front of the royal guard.

Rolph's face went blank for a moment longer. Then he laughed so hard he had to hold on to the carriage. George looked at the sky. One of the younger guards made a noise like a cough and was looked at.

"Oh, that's—" Rolph wiped his eyes. "Don't. Don't ever say that to my mother. Don't you *dare*." He straightened. "But the voice. Keep the voice."

"What?"

"The voice. That one. Tovey's. It's exactly right for in here. Nobody'll look at you twice." He considered Tor. "You'll want another one for outside. In the city. Something a bit rougher. Sara's, maybe. But in here, that's the one."

Tor stopped laughing and looked at him, slightly puzzled.

"All right," he said, in Tovey's voice. Then, more firmly: "All right. If you think so."

George had opened the gate. As they went through he said, evenly, to the air: "Everybody that comes in is on the list, young sir. Everybody. We had a man in the spring got as far as the fig walk, and they're still talking about him." His eyes rested on Tor. "Welcome, Scholar."

"Thank you, sir," said Tor, in Tovey's voice; and then, in his own, appalled, "Sorry. Thank you."

The gate shut behind them. On the other side of it George said "Sir," very quietly, to nobody, and his face worked for a moment before it settled.

* * *

On the second floor of the small house, in a long cool corridor with a carpet so thick that their boots made no sound, Rolph stopped at a door.

"That one's mine," he said, pointing at the next door along. "This one's yours. Dinner's at the eighth bell. Get cleaned up. I'll come and fetch you."

He went into his own room. The door shut.

Tor opened his door and stood in the doorway.

Beyond it was not a room but rooms: one with chairs in it and nothing else; beyond that a bedroom with a bed hung in pale green curtains, big enough to sleep a family crossways; windows to the floor. In the middle of the first room stood a great dark polished table with a cloth laid down its length, deep moss green. There was a rug, and pictures, and a bowl of white flowers on a stand. His trunk sat in the corner on its plate.

Tor looked down at himself: dust on his boots, in the seams of his tunic, in the creases of his hands. He took his boots off in the doorway and crossed the carpet in his socks, touching nothing, to a door at the far side.

Behind it was a washroom tiled in white and blue, with a bath on four brass feet and two brass taps. He turned one. Cold water roared into the bath. He turned it off fast and stood there. Then he turned the other. Warm.

"Oh," he said, to the tap. "Oh, *where* are you coming from?"

He filled the bath and got in and scrubbed until his skin was pink. He washed his hair twice. He picked up a cake of soap that smelled green and expensive, hesitated over it, and used it. When he climbed out the water was gray.

By the time he'd dried off and crossed back to the first room, with the towel round his shoulders, his forehead was shining again and his back was damp. He stopped in the middle of the carpet and looked at himself in despair.

He knelt at his trunk and opened it. On top, flat between the shirts in sacking, were copper plates.

Tor laughed out loud.

"Idiot," he said, fondly.

He took out one plate, book-sized, with the downward hook etched on it, and laid his palm on it for a moment. He looked at the walls, at the pictures, at the plaster, and didn't touch them. He carried the plate to the great table and set it in the middle of the green cloth, square, and stood back. Copper on green. He looked at it with his head on one side.

The curtains, which had hung slack, stirred very slightly. Tor stood over the plate with his eyes shut and his arms a little out from his sides. The shine went off his forehead. His shoulders came down.

Behind him the door had opened, without a knock. A girl stood in it: about eighteen, in a blue dress with something gathered at the shoulders, dark-haired, composed. She looked at his back for a moment. Then she looked past him, at the plate.

Tor opened his eyes and turned round.

He saw her. He went red from the hairline to the collarbone in one breath.

"Oh," said the girl. Her hand went to her mouth. "Oh, I'm *so* sorry. You're not Alphie." Her eyes went wide. "I thought this was— I'm sorry to catch you dressing."

Tor already had his shirt. He was into it before she'd finished, fighting his arms through the sleeves.

"No, I'm sorry," came out of the shirt. His head emerged. "I'm so sorry, miss. I didn't— the door was— I'm sorry." He buttoned the cuffs with great attention, not looking at her. Then he looked at her face, and at the wall between this room and the next, and back. "You'll be Rolph's sister."

"I'm Karina."

"Tor. Torrance Baker. I'm— we're at school together." A pause. "He's told us about you. A bit."

"Oh, Alphie's friend from school." She took a step into the room. "But I suppose you all call him Rolph there."

"Alphie," said Tor, trying it.

Karina took another step, and stopped. The loose curl at her temple, which had been stuck to her skin, lifted. She stood quite still with her lips slightly parted and her eyes half closed. Then her eyes opened and went to the plate on the green cloth, and stayed on it.

"You've got one," she said. Her voice came out a little flat; she smiled, and it lifted. "Already. On your *first day*." She looked at him again, for longer. "You must be terribly important."

"Me?" Tor looked startled. "No. No, it's mine. I mean— I made it."

"You made it."

"Well, yes. It's only a plate."

Karina sat down in one of the chairs, unhurried, and arranged her skirt, and folded her hands. She kept her eyes on Tor the whole time.

"I think," she said, "I'm going to come and sit in here a great deal. If you don't mind. It's the only cool room in the house."

Tor's face lit up.

"Oh, but you don't have to," he said. "That's the thing. I brought some."

He knelt at the trunk, and stopped, one hand on the lid. He turned his head and looked at her, with his head a little on one side, the way a man stands in a doorway listening to something in the next room.

"Are you all right?" he said.

Karina's hands, in her lap, had gone still. "Perfectly."

"You seemed—" He frowned slightly. "Sorry. You seemed surprised. Just then. As if I'd— as if I was up to something." He looked worried. "I'm not. I promise. They're just plates."

"I wasn't surprised," said Karina.

"Oh. Sorry. I must have been wrong." He turned back to the trunk.

Karina watched the back of his head. She didn't move.

He came back with four plates stacked flat in their sacking, carried in both hands like a baker's tray, and set them on the table beside the first.

"Two for you," he said. "And two for your sister. Varley? Rolph said Varley. One for where you sleep and one for where you sit. You put your hand on it, here, flat, and you tell it which way. In the summer it takes the warm out and puts it in the ground. In the winter, if you turn it round, it'll bring it back up." He looked at her anxiously. "They need nails, really. For the wall. Four. But you could just put them on a table if you'd rather. Like that one."

Karina looked at the four plates. She said nothing for a moment.

"That's a great deal too much," she said at last. "You can't just— these must be worth—"

"Oh, no, I brought them for you. For all of you." He was smiling, shy and pleased. "For Rolph's family. That's what they're *for*. I made them before we came." His face turned thoughtful. "Although I suppose I'll have to call him Alphie now. Or Alphonse. Now that we're in the city." Under his breath: "*Alphonse.*" A small face. "Alphie."

"Alphie," Karina agreed. She rose, gathered the four plates against her, and looked at him over the top of them, warmly.

"Nobody has two, you know," she said. "Not in the whole city. Except, of course, my parents." She tipped her head. "And obviously you."

Tor went pink again and looked at the plates, not at her. "I've only got the one," he said. "Out. The rest are spares."

She laughed. "Thank you, Torrance Baker."

"Tor. Please."

"Thank you, Tor."

She went out and shut the door.

A moment later it opened again, just far enough for her head. Tor was standing where she'd left him, looking at the door, his head on one side.

"Sorry," said Karina, with a small shy dip of the chin. "I don't suppose you know where Alphie is?"

"Next door," said Tor. "On the left."

"Thank you."

The door shut. In the corridor, her footsteps went away to the right.

* * *

Tor stood by the table and frowned at the door. His fingers rubbed together slowly, the way a baker's do over dough that doesn't feel right.

He looked round at the room: the still curtains, the white flowers upright in their bowl, the plate on the green cloth. He looked at the door to the corridor. He went and opened it a hand's width. Hot air came in. He shut it again.

"I'd take you with me if I could," he told the plate.

He stopped. His hands came up slowly, palms up, and he looked at nothing.

"Not the room," he said. "Not the *room*. Me."

He was at the table. He had a pencil from somewhere, and the back of a list from his satchel, and he was drawing: an egg; a small person inside it; an arrow from the bottom of the egg down through wavy lines; a hook.

"Not cold," he said aloud. "*Right*. Cool when it's hot. Warm when it's cold. Just *right*. Like the cellar. All the time." His pencil went down through the wavy lines again, harder. "And you can't put it in the ground, you're not in the ground, you're on a carpet. Unless it reaches. It reaches anyway. The wall plate reaches. So if you were clever—"

He reached for a second sheet.

* * *

Rolph knocked twice and went in. He stopped in the doorway, one hand still on the frame, and drew a long breath in through his nose.

The plate lay in the middle of the green cloth. At the far end of the table Tor sat in a clean shirt, wet-haired and barefoot, with four sheets of paper covered in eggs, his lips moving.

Rolph crossed the room, took him by the shoulder and shook him.

"Tor. *Tor.* Dinner."

Tor blinked up at him, then at the window, where the light had gone long and gold, then at the papers.

"Is it the eighth bell?"

"Nearly." Rolph glanced at the eggs. "What's that?"

"I don't know yet." Tor put the pencil down slowly. "Something."

"Well, leave it. Mother's here." A pause. "Father couldn't make it."

Tor got up, found his boots in the doorway and pulled them on sitting on the floor. Then he stood and looked down at himself: clean shirt, brown tunic, brown trousers.

"Rolph," he said. "Is this all right? To go down in?"

"It's fine."

"It's *brown*." He plucked at it. "Your sister was in a dress. A proper one. With—" a helpless gesture "—with the bit. At the shoulders. And I'm going to your mother's dinner in my school things, like I've come to deliver the coal."

Rolph turned round in the doorway.

"Karina was in here?"

"Earlier. She came in by mistake. Looking for you." Tor was still tugging at his tunic. "She was very nice. I gave her some plates."

"Some," said Rolph.

"Four. Two for her and two for Varley."

Rolph shut his eyes for a moment.

"Very generous of you," he said.

"Was it wrong?"

"No. No, it's—" He opened his eyes. "It's very like you. Come on." Then, as Tor's hand went back to his tunic: "Stop that. Tor, listen. It doesn't matter. It's only us tonight, and nobody's going to look at your clothes. And anyway, that's half of what we're here for. To get you comfortable. And get you some proper things for the summer. There's a man Mother uses."

"I can't afford a man Mother uses."

"Then we'll start with mine." Rolph looked him up and down. "I've got a whole press full of things I grew out of when I was fourteen. Some of them would go round you twice. We'll have a look tomorrow." He held the door. "Now come on. And, Tor?"

"Yes?"

"The voice."

Tor straightened his back, lifted his chin a hair, and said, in a voice like a long table with a cloth on it, "Of course. After you, Rolph."

Rolph laughed, and then stopped laughing, and led the way down.

* * *

The small dining room had a table for twelve, laid tonight for five at one end by the open windows onto the fig walk, under a painted ceiling of birds and vines. Eight servers stood along the walls.

A woman sat at the head of the table in a dark dress, with rings on most of her fingers. Beside her sat Karina, in blue, and a younger girl of about sixteen with her hands folded and her eyes on the door.

Rolph came in. Tor came in a step behind him, back straight, chin up a hair, not hurrying. He stopped at a proper distance from the head of the table and bowed from the shoulders.

"Good evening, ma'am. Thank you so much for having me."

His voice was well-schooled and pleasant, a little lazy in the vowels. The woman's eyebrows rose very slightly and came down again.

"Torrance," she said, and held out her hand. "At last. Come and sit by me. I've a great many questions, and Alphonse has been hoarding you."

"He's been very kind, ma'am."

"He's been very selfish. Sit."

He sat at her left, across from Karina. The younger girl, beside Karina, looked from Tor to her sister and back. Rolph took the seat at the end, by the window.

The servers moved. Bowls of cold green soup with a curl of cream on each were set down in turn: before the woman, before each of the girls, before Rolph. The head server, a gray, upright man, set Tor's bowl down himself and stepped back.

Tor picked up his spoon, smiled at the soup, and took the first mouthful.

The head server's hand stopped halfway to the next bowl. The younger girl's eyes went straight to her mother. One corner of Karina's mouth twitched. At the end of the table, Rolph laid his own spoon down on the cloth, very gently.

Tor swallowed and looked up, delighted.

"Oh, that's *lovely*," he said, in the same well-schooled voice. "Is that cucumber? We do one at home with sorrel. Mother puts the cream in before it's cold, which you're not meant to."

The woman picked up her spoon and took a mouthful, unhurried.

"It is cucumber," she said. "And I think your mother is quite right."

The head server's hand finished its journey. The younger girl breathed out. Spoons moved round the table.

* * *

From then on Tor watched Rolph's hands. When Rolph took up a fork, Tor took up the same fork a beat later. When Rolph laid his knife across his plate, Tor's knife went across his.

He answered every question put to him fully and politely, and then waited, hands in his lap, for the next. Two Rivers. His family. ("A lot of us. There's a T-name for every one. Mother says she ran out and had to start again.") The school. Kolb. ("He's very fair. He's going to kill me, I think, eventually. But fairly.") The younger girl laughed out loud, and then looked startled at herself.

Over the fish, Karina said lightly, "Mother, did Alphie tell you? Tor's brought cooling plates. He's given me two."

Her mother looked at her.

"Two," she said.

"And two for Varley. Haven't you, Tor?"

"I brought them for everybody," said Tor, anxiously. "I've a few more. In the trunk. I didn't know who'd want them. I'm sorry, I should have asked you first, ma'am, they're your walls—"

"Torrance," said Rolph's mother. "How does it work?"

Tor put down his fork. His hands came up off his lap and began to draw in the air. As he talked, his voice changed; the long vowels shortened, and something broader and rougher came into it, from further east.

"It's the cellar. Do you know, ma'am, when you go down into a cellar in summer, how it's cool? And in winter it's warm, warmer than the kitchen? That's because down there it's always the same. The ground doesn't care what the weather's doing. It's like a great big— like a bank, really, that you can put warm into and take warm out of. And the plate just reaches down. Through the house. Into the ground under it. And it carries the warm. It doesn't *make* any, that's the important part, making's dear, it just *carries* it, the way you'd carry water in a bucket, from where there's too much to where there's not enough. And in summer there's too much up here and not enough down there, so it carries it down. And in winter you turn it round—"

Along the walls, the servers had stopped moving. The head server stood with a sauceboat in his hand, listening.

"—and over a year it more or less pays itself back. The ground lends you the warm in winter, and you give it back in summer. So you hardly need to feed it anything at all. It'll go for years." He stopped. The long vowels came back all at once, and his hands went into his lap. "Sorry. I've gone on."

"You have not," said Varley, before her mother could speak. Then, very red, to her plate: "Sorry. Go on. I mean— is that why it's copper?"

Tor turned to her, beaming. "Yes! Well, partly—"

Their mother sat back and looked across at Karina. Karina was watching Tor with her lips moving very slightly, as if counting.

Then Rolph's mother looked down the table at her son. Rolph was watching Tor too, his chin on his hand, and did not notice her looking.

* * *

Later, in a sitting room with the windows open on the dark and moths coming in to the lamp, Rolph shut the door and stood with his back against it.

His mother sat by the window, turning her rings one after another.

"He took the first bite," she said.

"He doesn't know."

"No. I could see he didn't." She turned another ring. "He doesn't know a great many things, does he."

Rolph said nothing.

"Does he know anything at all?"

"He thinks we're merchants." Rolph's voice was flat. "Big ones. Near the palace. He thinks we supply the king with something. I don't know what. Cloth, possibly. He asked me if it was cloth." A pause. "He saw George's lot at the gate, and he knew them. He knew exactly what they were. And then he decided we must just live very close."

His mother was quiet for a while.

"Good," she said.

Rolph looked at her.

"Let him like us first," she said. "Let him like us *first*, Alphonse. Before anything else. He'll find out soon enough. Your father's home in the morning." She held out her hand. After a moment he crossed the room and took it. "I liked him. I didn't expect to. I expected to find him useful, and I do. But I liked him." She considered. "Karina is going to be impossible."

"Karina has two plates."

"Karina has two plates," his mother agreed, "and Lady Arden has one."

Rolph stood holding her hand. His face moved, and settled, and moved again.

"Go to bed," she said, and kissed him.

* * *

When Tor opened the door of his rooms, the curtains stirred toward him. He stood in the doorway in the dark for a moment, then lit two candles and set them at the end of the great table.

At the other end lay his four sheets of eggs. He sat down and pulled them toward him and looked at them for a long time, his chin in his hands. Once he yawned. Once he got up, went to the window, opened it, felt the heat come in off the city, and shut it again.

"Luncheon," he said, to the window. "In the middle of the afternoon. In *this*." He came back to the table. "And they'll all come across the whole city in their good things, and his mother sat there all through dinner and never said a word."

He looked at his trunk.

"And all I brought was things for *walls*."

He opened the trunk and took out a stack of small copper squares, a jar of acid, a pot of resist and a roll of little brushes, and set them out on the green cloth beside the plate. Then he counted out squares, touching each one.

"Rolph's mother. Karina. Varley. Rolph. Trice. Sara. Tovey." His finger paused. "Rolph's father. Me, to test." He laid down the tenth. "And one spare." He squared the stack. "Ten. You always do ten."

He sat back and looked at it.

"It's only the wall plate," he said. "Folded up small. On a cord, like the shield. Short night." He nodded to himself. "Done before the fifth bell."

He laid his hands flat on the cloth on either side of the stack and looked at the first square. His breathing slowed.

"Right," said Tor, quietly.

The candles burned down. The house went quiet around him. In the room, the curtains hung still.
