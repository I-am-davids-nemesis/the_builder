Tor came down to the yard after combat with a sack over his shoulder. He carried it with both hands under the bottom, the way a boy carries a basket of eggs up from the hen-house.

"Sir," he said. "If you've a moment, sir."

The yard was empty but for Kolb and the pell, and the long shadows of the racks lying across the sand, and a first-year raking the far end. The rake had slowed. Kolb looked at it. The rake sped up.

Tor set the sack on the bench by the water butt and took out a belt. Brown leather, plain, a little stiff, with a copper plate riveted flat at the back of it, a hand and a half wide. Then another belt. Then a third. Then a small cloth bag that clicked.

"They're the flying ones, sir," said Tor. "I've given the others theirs. Rolph and Trice and Miss Sara and the Count. But it was your idea, sir. So I thought the school ought to have some. For you to manage." He undid the bag and tipped three flattened copper eggs into his palm, each on its loop of cord. "The control goes on the wrist. Always on the wrist. If you drop it, the plate keeps on doing whatever you last told it, and it goes on doing it for about a year. So you mustn't drop it."

"A year," said Kolb.

"About a year, sir. Up, mostly." Tor looked at the eggs, and then at Kolb. "I'd put that in the rules, sir. If it was me."

Kolb picked up a belt and turned it over. He looked at the etching on the plate for a moment, and longer at the stitching. Then he looked up, past Tor, at the line of the hills beyond the valley, and stayed looking at them for the space of a breath.

"It'll go in the rules," he said.

"Thank you, sir."

"Who's flown it?"

"Me, sir. To Hollin." Tor went pink. "And back. Twice since. And the Count, a little. He's quite good. He says it's not the same as the other way." He hesitated. "I don't think he likes to talk about the other way, sir."

"No," said Kolb. "He wouldn't."

He put the belts back in the sack one at a time, each laid flat on the last. At the far end of the yard the rake had stopped altogether.

"Two hundred," said Kolb, without turning round, "if that rake stops again."

The rake started.

"Off you go, Baker. Nice and loose."

Tor went. Kolb stayed by the water butt with the sack at his feet while the bell tower counted the fifth bell of the afternoon, and for some while after it had finished.

* * *

For nearly a month after that, Tor was a student.

He was at the building master's door at the sixth bell and in the long room under the eaves at the seventh. He handed in a theory essay on time. When Kolb sent the class to Hollin he ran there on his feet, along the road, and slapped the stone of the well with the others, and on the way back did not once look up. On the wall above Rolph's bed, in pencil, small, there was a row of strokes, one for each day without a trance. By the middle of the spring there were nineteen.

Then the weather turned hot.

It came overnight. By the noon bell the court was white with glare, and the flagstones gave the heat back up through the soles of people's boots. In theory two students fell asleep and the master let them. The library was cool and full. At the sixth bell, in the men's corridor, the doors stood open to let the cool out of the rooms into the passage, and a boy lay on his bed in one of them with a wet cloth over his face and raised a hand as Rolph went by.

"Bless that boy," said the boy, under the cloth.

Rolph opened the door of their room. Heat came out past him into the corridor.

The window was shut. On the desk the candle stood bent over at the middle. Tor sat in front of it in his shirtsleeves, his hair flat to his forehead, his shirt dark down the back, drawing. A drop of sweat hung off the end of his nose. It fell onto the paper. He blotted it with his sleeve without looking and went on drawing.

Rolph looked at him. He looked at the book-sized copper plate on the wall above Tor's bed, held up by its four nails. He looked back at Tor.

He began to laugh. He leaned on the door frame and laughed until he had to hold on to it, and Tor turned round on his stool, blinking.

"What?"

"*Tor*. Tor. It's the hottest day of the year."

"Is it?" Tor looked at the window. "It is warm."

"It is *warm*. Feel your shirt. You could wring it." Rolph crossed the room and laid his palm on the plaster beside the plate. He took it away again and showed Tor the palm, as if there were something on it. "The whole school's turned theirs round. Every room in the east wing. The first-years' long room's like a cellar. Davie's got a queue of people outside his door who want to stand in it. The *infirmary* sister turned hers round. You showed her how. I watched you." He was laughing again. "Everybody in this school is lying in a lovely cool room tonight thanking you by name, and you're sitting in here under the thing you *made*, sweating like a cheese."

Tor looked at the plate for a long moment. Then he got up, went to it, put his hand flat on the copper and shut his eyes. His lips moved.

The candle flame, which had been standing straight up, leaned toward the wall. The bent candle began, slowly, to stop dripping. Rolph rubbed the back of his neck and looked at his hand. Down the back of Tor's shirt the dark patch shrank inward from the edges.

"Sorry," Tor said, to the plate.

"Don't apologize to it."

"I wasn't." Tor opened his eyes. "I was. A bit." He sat down on his bed under the plate and let out a long breath. His shoulders dropped an inch. "Oh, that's *better*."

"I know it's better. Everybody knows it's better. You're the last to know."

"I was busy."

"You're always busy." Rolph kicked off his boots and lay back on his own bed with his hands behind his head. "Listen. I've been meaning to ask you something. Tovey's going home for the summer."

"Is he?"

"He's got work, he says. *Work*." Rolph opened one eye. "He's going home to sit at the end of a long table and sign whatever his mother has already decided. But he calls it work, so it's work. And he's expecting you."

Tor sat very still.

"Expecting me," he said.

"To stay. Over the summer. At the Thompson house in the capital. He hasn't said so to you, because he's Tovey, and he assumes you know, because you're his declared friend, and that's what declared friends do. But he's told people. He's told Trice. He asked her what you eat."

"What I *eat*?"

"So that's Tovey." Rolph sat up. "But I asked first. I asked you in the autumn, if you remember, before you were anybody's declared anything. And it's my mother's house, and she wants to meet you, and she gets what she wants. And anyway—" he waved a hand at the room, the plate, the drawing, Tor "—you're family. Practically. Brothers beat friends. That's the rule."

"Whose rule?"

"Everybody's. Trust me." He grinned. "So you're staying with us. Tovey can visit. We're nearer everything anyway. His people are out on the Hill. It's very nice, if you like being half an hour from anywhere in a carriage. We're right in the middle. Everybody has to come past us to get anywhere." He paused on that, very briefly, and went on. "You'd like it. It's big and draughty and there's a lot of forks, but there's a garden, and there's a kitchen the size of this whole wing, and nobody in it would let you near the ovens, so you could have a rest."

Tor said nothing. He had both hands flat on the blanket on either side of him. His eyes were on the floor.

"I ought to go home," he said. "In the summer. I always go home."

"All right."

"For the harvest. They need—" He stopped. His right hand began to move against the blanket, a finger at a time. "There's Terlee. And Mother. And Father, and the twins, and Tam, and Tilda's back from her place now. And Tessa's nine." He looked down at the finger. "She can knead. She was just starting to, at midwinter. If she's kneading, then Tilda doesn't have to watch her, so that's—" The finger moved. "That's two. That's two people, really. That's more than me." He looked at his hand, and then up at Rolph. His eyebrows were up and the corners of his mouth were up, and then, for a moment, they weren't. "They don't need me."

"They'll miss you."

"Oh, they'll miss me," said Tor, quite cheerfully. "But they don't *need* me. That's different." He thought about it. "I'd write. I'd send the money. Mother'd say go. She'd say it in a voice, but she'd say go."

"So that's yes."

"If your mother really doesn't mind."

"My mother," said Rolph, "has written to me about you eleven times since the autumn."

Tor went pink. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be in the capital in the summer." Rolph lay back down, beaming at the ceiling. "We'll go down together the day after term. It's six days in the coach, but the inns are good once you're past the river. You won't need much. Mother'll want you in something decent the minute you arrive; she'll have a man in. But bring what you like. Books. Your copper. Trunks, a couple."

"Trunks," said Tor.

"A couple. I take four." Rolph shut his eyes. "Anyway. The house. There's the front, which is all stairs and nobody you'd want to talk to, and then there's the garden side, where we actually live. My sisters have the east end. Don't go in the east end. They'll eat you alive, and they'll be very charming about it. And the library's on the river side, and you can see the whole city from the top of it, all the roofs, Tovey's whole street. You can see right across to the Hill, actually, on a clear day. We used to throw things at it. Not *at* it. Toward it."

Behind him, a pen began to scratch.

Tor was back at the desk with a fresh sheet. On it, ruled neatly, was a square box with a strap round it; under the box, a flat plate; beside it, a stick figure wearing a belt. An arrow ran from the figure to the box. A second arrow curved from the box back to the figure. Under it was a word, written small.

"There's a great hall," said Rolph, watching the back of Tor's head, "where they do the winter dinners, and the ceiling's got all the old gods on it, and one of them's got the wrong number of fingers, and you're not allowed to say."

"Mm," said Tor. He drew a second box. Then a third, smaller, with a question mark on it.

"And my father has a dog called Admiral that's afraid of the stairs."

"Mm."

Rolph smiled at the ceiling and stopped talking. He lay still for a long while with his eyes open, in the cool, listening to the pen. Once he put his hand flat on his stomach and kept it there.

"And it'd have to *follow*," said Tor softly, to the paper. "Not just come. Follow." He drew another arrow.

* * *

Three days later the noon post brought a letter under the Debri seal. Sara read it standing in the cloister, twice, and then folded it and walked fast toward the men's wing, and met Rolph on the stair, and turned round and walked faster toward the east stair.

The workroom at the top had a slate on the door that said *BAKER*. The window was open. Tor sat at the bench with a sheet of copper the size of a tea tray in front of him, painting resist onto it in long lines, his tongue between his teeth. At Sara's knock the brush jumped and the line went crooked.

"Miss Sara. Sorry. Come in." He looked at the line, sighed, and put the brush down. "It's all right. It's only a trial."

"What is it?"

"It's for luggage." He looked at her face. "Is something wrong?"

Sara stayed standing, with the letter in both hands.

"There's a town called Ashford," she said, "on the river, about ten days south of here. The house trades through it. Grain, mostly, and wool going the other way. In the spring floods the sewers backed up and broke into the cistern, and the cistern feeds the town wells, and now the water's foul all through the lower town. There's flux. There've been deaths: children, and old people, mostly. They're boiling what they can, but there's not enough wood in a river town to boil water for eleven thousand people, and the purifiers they have are the little ones. They can't keep up." She held out the letter. "The house wants to know if there's anything that can be done. It's asked me to ask you."

Tor took the letter and read it through, down to the seal at the bottom, and frowned.

"But that's only a purifier," he said.

"Yes."

"Everyone's got a purifier. My mother's got one. It's on the water jug at home, on a bit of slate, it's older than I am." He turned the letter over and looked at the back. "That's the first thing anybody learns, Miss Sara. That's the *second-year* sigil. You find what isn't water and you tell it to go and sit at the bottom. There's nothing to it."

"For a jug," said Sara.

"Well, yes, for a jug, but—"

"Ashford hasn't got a jug. It's got a cistern the size of the chapel and a river, and eleven thousand people drinking out of it."

"It's the same thing," said Tor. "It's just more of it." His frown had deepened; he was frowning at the letter. "It's silly. It's a silly thing to be stuck on. The trick's the same. It's only that a jug field doesn't reach, and if you make it reach it gets thin, and if it gets thin it gets lazy and lets things through, and then you've got a lazy field in your cistern telling eleven thousand people the water's clean when it—"

He stopped. His eyes had gone to the copper on the bench.

"Unless you didn't make it reach," he said. "Unless you didn't make it bigger at all. Unless it stayed jug-sized, and you made the *water* go past it. All of it. A bit at a time. Like a sieve in a—" His hand rose and drew in the air over the bench: a box, a line going in, a line coming out. "The cistern's already got a pipe in and a pipe out. It must have. So you don't put it *in* the cistern. You put it on the *pipe*. And you put ten, one after the other, so if one gets lazy the next one—" He looked up. "How big's the pipe?"

"I don't know."

"Ask them. No—" He pulled a fresh sheet toward him. "No, it doesn't matter, I'll make it so it doesn't matter. You'd want to be able to bend it round. Copper bends." He was writing. "Ten. And a template. That's two days, I think. Maybe three, with the template. Would three be too long?"

"Tor," said Sara. "Three days is fast. Three days is—" She stopped, and when she went on her voice was slower and very even. "Three days would be very acceptable."

"All right." He kept writing. Then he put the pen down and looked at the letter again, at the seal. "Children," he said.

"Yes."

He nodded once and stood up.

Sara folded her arms. She opened her mouth.

Tor went past her and down the stair, and came back with a basket from the kitchens. He set it on the bench and packed it while she watched: a loaf, a second loaf, a round of hard cheese in a cloth, six apples, a crock with its lid tied down, a stone bottle of water with the stopper wired in, a bag of oatcakes, a knife. He went through it all again with one finger. Then he added a seventh apple.

Sara closed her mouth.

"Trice will ask," Tor said, without looking round. "Tell her I packed."

"I'll tell her."

"Tell her I packed *properly*."

"I'll tell her you packed properly," said Sara, and turned and looked out of the window.

He went down the east stair with the basket on his arm, the tea-tray copper under the other, and a roll of drawings in his teeth. At the bottom he turned toward the old wing and the worn stair that went down to the undercroft. He was muttering round the drawings. The bell began to ring the first bell after noon. Sara stood at the top of the stair until it had finished, and then went to her room and took out paper, and wrote, and crossed out *three days*, and wrote *as soon as may be*, and did not write *children*.

* * *

The slate by the chamber door read *BAKER, T. — In: second bell after noon, third day*, and below that, in another hand, in rows of five, forty-nine chalk marks.

On the fifth day, at the third bell of the afternoon, the door opened.

Tor came out with one hand on the frame. He was carrying a crate. In it, standing on their edges, were ten long copper sheets bent lengthwise into half pipes, and laid flat across the top was a square of pale unpainted wood with ink on it. The empty basket hung from his elbow. His face was gray. He blinked at the lamp in the passage.

"Oh," he said. "Hello, Mistress Haskins."

Bet Haskins, on the bench opposite, put down her mending. "Well, then."

Behind him, four flat copper plates came out of the chamber.

Each was square, the size of a tea tray. They came one after another at the height of a man's knee, in a line, a little apart, with nothing holding them up. When Tor stopped, they stopped. The first one bumped gently into the back of his leg.

"Sorry," Tor said to it, and it backed off a hand's breadth and hung there.

Bet Haskins looked at the plates. She did not pick up her mending.

"They're for luggage," said Tor. He set the crate on the end of her bench and took the chalk from its string. "You put a trunk on, and they follow. They follow the belt." He touched the plain brown belt at his waist. "I needed four. Rolph takes four. I think I might need five. I don't know what you take to the capital."

He wrote on the slate: *Out: third bell of the afternoon, fifth day.* Under it, *Thank you.*

Then he picked up the crate and walked off along the passage toward the worn stair. The four plates swung round in a line and went after him, at knee height, bobbing a little. At the foot of the stair he stopped, and they stopped. He went up a step. The plates hung where they were. Then the first one rose, a step's height, and came on, and the others came after it, one step at a time, up the stair and out of sight into the light.

Bet Haskins sat on her bench. After some time she reached over and took the slate off its nail and held it on her knee and looked at it. She hung it back up.

At breakfast the next morning she was the first to the staff table, and did not wait to be asked.
