Rolph talked the whole way across the city, and then, for the last street, he didn't.

He'd pointed out the corn exchange, and the river stairs where you could buy eels, and the house with the blue door where a man had once kept a bear. He'd told Tor which fountain was lucky and which one people only said was lucky. And then the carriage turned out of the noise into a long, quiet, tree-lined street with a white wall running down one whole side of it, higher than a house and without a single window, and Rolph found that he had straightened his collar. He found that he'd done it twice.

Tor was looking at him.

"Are you all right?"

"Fine. Hot."

"You've gone quiet."

"I'm saving it," said Rolph. "For later."

Tor considered this, and then said, gravely, the way he'd say a thing he'd been rehearsing, "I'll talk to people. I promise. I won't stand at the back."

Rolph looked at him, and felt something in his chest go warm and painful at once, and couldn't for the life of him have said which half was which.

"I know you will," he said.

* * *

The carriage stopped at a gate in the long white wall. It wasn't a grand gate. It was set back in an arch, quite plain, oak bound with black iron, the sort of gate a carter would use, and there were two men in front of it and two more in the shade of the arch behind, and all four of them were in purple and black.

Rolph got down. Tor got down after him, and stood on the hot cobbles, and looked at the men.

Rolph watched him do it. He watched Tor's eyes go to the purple tunics and the black sashes, and to the short black rods at their belts, which Tor had last seen in the practice yard in Mistress Wensa's hand. He watched Tor go still.

"Those are," said Tor, quietly. "That's the royal—"

He stopped.

Rolph waited. He found he was holding his breath.

Tor frowned. It was the frown he wore over a sigil that wouldn't come right: not puzzled, *annoyed*, as if there were a step he could see perfectly well and couldn't for some reason put his foot on. He looked at the guards. He looked up at the wall, and along it. And then the frown cleared, and he nodded to himself, and said, "Oh, of course. You'd be quite near it here. The palace. They'd have to watch the whole street."

Rolph let the breath go.

The oldest of the four came out from the arch. He was broad and gray and had a face like a good saddle, and he'd taught Rolph to whistle through his fingers, a long time ago, on the other side of this gate.

"Young sir," said George.

Something happened to Rolph's back. He didn't decide it. It straightened, and his chin came up a little, and when he spoke, it was in a voice that had very little Barrow in it and a good deal of long table.

"George. Good afternoon. I trust we're not unexpected."

"Not at all, young sir. You're on the list." George's eyes went past him. "And this will be the scholar."

"Scholar Baker. My guest for the summer. He'll want to come and go as he pleases."

"Very good, young sir."

Beside him, Rolph heard a sound. It was a small sound, swallowed, and he knew it. He'd heard it across a dormitory room two hundred times, generally when he'd fallen over something in the dark. He turned his head.

Tor's mouth was perfectly straight. His eyes were not.

"Ah," said Tor. And then he lifted his chin, exactly as Rolph had, to the hair, and drew himself up to his whole small height, and said, in a voice that was not his own at all, a voice that was Tovey's down to the last long vowel and the faint, unbothered boredom at the end of it:

"Do tell me, Rolph. Is that the house?" He nodded, very graciously, at the roof that showed above the wall: a long gray-slated roof with a row of tall chimneys, and beyond it, much further off, rising above the trees, another roof, and another, and towers, and a dome, going on and on in the afternoon light. "The *small* one. I do so hope I'm to be put up in the small house. I'd feel quite at home."

Rolph stared at him.

George's face did not change in any way that a stranger could have seen.

"Well, we call it the small house," said Rolph, blankly, in his own voice. "Everybody does. It's smaller than the—" He waved at the dome. "How did you know?"

Tor's composure broke. He put his hand over his mouth.

"It's the *privy*," he said, through it. "At home. The small house is the *privy*, Rolph. Out the back, by the woodpile. With the moon cut in the door." And he began to laugh, quite helplessly, on the cobbles, in front of the royal guard.

It took Rolph a moment. Then it went through him all at once, and he laughed so hard he had to hold on to the carriage. George looked at the sky. One of the younger guards behind him made a noise like a cough and was looked at.

"Oh, that's—" Rolph wiped his eyes. "Don't. Don't ever say that to my mother. Don't you *dare*." He straightened up. "But the voice. Keep the voice."

"What?"

"The voice. That one. Tovey's. It's exactly right for in here. Nobody'll look at you twice." He considered Tor critically, the way he'd once considered a horse. "You'll want another one for outside. In the city. Something a bit rougher. Sara's, maybe. But in here, that's the one."

Tor stopped laughing and looked at him, slightly puzzled, as if trying to tell whether this was a joke as well.

"All right," he said, in Tovey's voice, testing it. And then, a little more firmly: "All right. If you think so."

It was, Rolph thought, uncannily good. Tor had heard Tovey speak perhaps forty times in his life. He was going to have to watch that.

George had opened the gate. As they went through, he said, to the air, in the even tone of a man reading out a notice: "Everybody that comes in is on the list, young sir. Everybody. We had a man in the spring got as far as the fig walk, and they're still talking about him." His eyes rested on Tor for a moment, without any unkindness. "Welcome, Scholar."

"Thank you, sir," said Tor, in Tovey's voice, and then, horrified at himself, in his own, "Sorry. Thank you."

"Sir," said George to nobody, very quietly, as the gate shut behind them, and Rolph thought he had never seen the old man's face work quite so hard to stay where it was.

* * *

The small house was the biggest building Tor had ever been inside, and that included the chapel at Two Rivers, the long hall at school, and, he was fairly sure, the whole of Hollin laid end to end.

Rolph left him at a door on the second floor, in a long cool corridor with a carpet down the middle of it so thick that Tor's boots made no sound at all. "That one's mine," Rolph said, pointing at the next door along. "This one's yours. Dinner's at the eighth bell. Get cleaned up. I'll come and fetch you." And he went into his own room, and the door shut, and Tor was alone.

He opened his.

He stood in the doorway for some time.

It wasn't a room. It was rooms. There was a room with chairs in it, and nothing else, just chairs, as if the whole point of it was to sit. There was a room beyond that with a bed in it big enough for his whole family, if they lay crossways, and hung all round with pale green curtains, and the windows went right down to the floor. There was a great table in the middle of the first room, long and dark and polished, with a cloth laid along the middle of it, deep green, the color of moss after rain. There was a rug. There were pictures. There was a bowl of white flowers on a stand, for no reason at all that he could see, except that someone had thought he might like to look at them.

His trunk was there already, on its plate, in the corner, as though it had been waiting.

Tor looked down at himself. He was wearing ten hours of sky, and three towns, and the inside of a carriage. There was dust on his boots and in the seams of his tunic and in the creases of his hands, and he could feel his hair stiff with it.

He took his boots off in the doorway. Then he walked across the carpet in his socks, very carefully, the way you'd walk across a floor somebody had just washed, not touching anything, until he found a door that looked as if it might be the right one.

It was. It was a whole room for washing in, tiled in white and blue, with a bath in the middle of it on four brass feet like a beast's, and over the end of the bath, two brass taps.

Tor looked at the taps.

He turned one. Water came out of it. Clean, and cold, and a great deal of it, all at once, roaring into the bath. He turned it off, quickly, and stood there with his heart going. Then he turned the other. That one came out warm.

"Oh," said Tor, to the tap. "Oh, *where* are you coming from?"

The tap didn't say. He filled the bath anyway, half and half, and got in, and scrubbed. He scrubbed until his skin went pink and then went on. He washed his hair twice. He found a cake of soap that smelled of something green and expensive, and felt bad about using it, and used it. He washed behind his ears the way his mother had made them all do on feast days, lined up in the kitchen in order of size, Terlee first, Tessa last and wriggling, and he thought about that, and felt a long way from home, and then thought about the warm tap again and felt a bit better.

When he got out, the water in the bath was gray, and he was clean, and he was warm. Then he was hot.

It came on him the minute he'd dried off. The air in the rooms was thick, the way it had been in the carriage, the whole stored heat of the capital's afternoon pressing in through the tall windows, and by the time he'd crossed back to the first room with the towel round his shoulders he could feel it starting again on his back and his chest and his forehead, and he thought, in despair, that he was going to have to wash again before he'd even got dressed.

He knelt by his trunk for a clean shirt, and opened it, and there on top, in their sacking, packed flat between the shirts so they wouldn't knock, were the plates.

Tor looked at them, and at himself, and laughed out loud.

"Idiot," he said, fondly.

He took one out, a plain book-sized square of copper with the downward hook on it, and put his hand flat on it, and told it which way round to go. Then he looked about for somewhere to put it. There were no nails, and he wasn't going to knock nails into a wall like this without asking. So he carried it to the great dark table, and laid it in the middle of the green cloth, square, and stood back.

The copper lay on the green like a coin on moss. It looked, he thought, as if it had been made for the place. The colors went together. It was the first thing in the room he felt he'd had any part in, and it was quite nice.

The cool came off it, slowly, the way it came off a cellar step. He stood over it with his eyes shut and his arms out a little and let it come, and felt the sweat go cold on his back and then dry, and the thick air thin, and his own heart slow, and he breathed out for what felt like the first time since the river gate.

Then he opened his eyes and turned round, and there was a girl in the room.

* * *

Karina had chosen the moment with some care.

You couldn't go too soon; then you were the girl who'd been waiting behind a door. You couldn't go too late; then he'd be at dinner, under Mother's eye, and you'd never get a word that wasn't over the soup. Alphie had gone into his own room and shut the door, which meant he'd be lying on his bed in his boots for the next hour, which meant the corridor was clear. She gave it as long as a bath would take. Then she went along, and opened the door of the guest suite without knocking, because if you knocked you had to have a reason, and if you didn't you could always say you'd got the wrong one.

He was standing with his back to her, by the table, with his arms out.

He was small. That was the first thing. Alphie had written *small*, and Varley had said *how small*, and Alphie had only said *you'll see*. He was smaller than she was. He was wet-haired and barefoot and had a towel round his shoulders and nothing else above his trousers, and he was standing over something on the table with his eyes shut, like a boy warming his hands at a fire. Except that there was no fire. And the air that came across the room to her was—

He turned round.

He saw her. He went, in a single breath, from the color of a candle to the color of a radish, from his hairline to his collarbone, all the way down.

"Oh," said Karina. She put her hand to her mouth. "Oh, I'm *so* sorry. You're not Alphie." She let her eyes go wide. "I thought this was— I'm sorry to catch you dressing."

He had already found his shirt. He'd found it so fast that she hadn't seen him move: one moment it was in the trunk and the next it was over his head and he was fighting his way into it, arm by arm, with his face still scarlet.

"No, I'm sorry," came out of the shirt. His head emerged. "I'm so sorry, miss. I didn't— the door was— I'm sorry." He got the second arm in and pulled it straight, and stood there, buttoning the cuffs with great concentration, not looking at her. Then he did look at her, properly, and she saw him look at her face, and then at the door to the next room, and then back, and work it out. "You'll be Rolph's sister."

"I'm Karina."

"Tor," he said. "Torrance Baker. I'm— we're at school together." He hesitated. "He's told us about you. A bit."

"Oh, Alphie's friend from school." She came a step into the room. "But I suppose you all call him Rolph there."

"Alphie," said Tor, as if trying the weight of it.

She came another step in, and then she stopped, because the air had changed.

It had been like walking into an oven, all the way along the corridor. It had been like that all week, all month, all the long white summer; she'd had her maid sponge her neck with rosewater twice since lunch and it had done nothing at all. And here, three steps inside the guest suite, it was like walking into the cellar where the cook kept the butter. It was cool. It was *cool*. It came across her bare arms and the back of her neck and down inside her collar like the first breath of an autumn evening, and she stood quite still in it and very nearly closed her eyes.

Then she saw the plate on the table, square on the green cloth, and understood.

She'd heard. Everyone had heard. Somebody's cousin had come back from somewhere with a story about a plate on a wall that turned a room cold in a heat wave, and there'd been talk all through the spring of the Debri house, and a waiting list, and a price that made Father raise his eyebrows at dinner. Lady Arden had one. *One.* In her morning room. She'd had it a fortnight and nobody had been able to talk about anything else.

And here was this one, on a table, in a guest suite, like a dish of fruit.

"You've got one," said Karina. She heard her own voice come out a little flat, and fixed it. "Already. On your *first day*." She looked at him again, differently. He was still buttoning his cuffs. "You must be terribly important."

"Me?" He looked genuinely startled. "No. No, it's mine. I mean— I made it."

"You made it."

"Well, yes. It's only a plate."

Karina sat down in one of the chairs. She did it gracefully and without hurrying, and arranged her skirt, and put her hands in her lap, and while she was doing all of that she was thinking very fast. *He made it.* Alphie's friend from school. The scholarship boy. The one Alphie had written all those letters about, that Mother had read twice and then locked in her desk. *He made it.* And she was sitting in his room, in the cool, the first of anybody, and Lady Arden had *one*, in her *morning room*—

"I think," she said, "I'm going to come and sit in here a great deal. If you don't mind. It's the only cool room in the house."

He brightened all at once, like a lamp turned up.

"Oh, but you don't have to," he said. "That's the thing. I brought some."

He knelt at his trunk.

And then, quite suddenly, he went still, kneeling there, with one hand on the lid, and turned his head, and looked at her. It was an odd look. It wasn't the look of a boy at a girl. It was a look she'd once seen on Father's old falconer, standing in the mews with his head on one side, listening to something on the other side of the wall. He looked at her as though she'd said something out loud, and he'd heard it, and wasn't sure what it was.

"Are you all right?" he said.

Karina found that she had stopped breathing. "Perfectly."

"You seemed—" He frowned a little. "Sorry. You seemed surprised. Just then. As if I'd— as if I was up to something." He looked worried. "I'm not. I promise. They're just plates."

"I wasn't surprised," said Karina, who had been, very.

"Oh. Sorry. I must have been wrong." He went back to the trunk. And she sat there in the cool with her hands in her lap and her heart going rather fast, and thought: *what was that?* and couldn't answer.

He came back with four of them. He carried them across the carpet stacked flat in his two hands, in their sacking, like a baker carrying a tray, and set them down on the table beside the first.

"Two for you," he said. "And two for your sister. Varley? Rolph said Varley. One for where you sleep and one for where you sit. You put your hand on it, here, flat, and you tell it which way. In the summer it takes the warm out and puts it in the ground. In the winter, if you turn it round, it'll bring it back up." He looked at her anxiously. "They need nails, really. For the wall. Four. But you could just put them on a table if you'd rather. Like that one."

Karina looked at the four plates.

Two. Each. Lady Arden had *one*.

She found, for a moment, that she couldn't quite speak. It was not like her. She didn't care for it.

"That's a great deal too much," she said at last, and meant it, a little, which surprised her. "You can't just— these must be worth—"

"Oh, no, I brought them for you. For all of you." He was smiling now, shy and pleased. "For Rolph's family. That's what they're *for*. I made them before we came." He paused, and his face went thoughtful. "Although I suppose I'll have to call him Alphie now. Or Alphonse. Now that we're in the city." He tried it, under his breath. "*Alphonse.*" He made a small face. "Alphie."

"Alphie," Karina agreed. She stood. She gathered up the four plates against her, carefully, sacking and all, and looked at him over the top of them, and let a little of the warmth into her eyes that she'd been keeping back, and put it where she wanted it.

"Nobody has two, you know," she said. "Not in the whole city. Except, of course, my parents." She tipped her head. "And obviously you."

Tor went pink again, but differently, and looked at the plates, not at her. "I've only got the one," he said. "Out. The rest are spares."

She laughed, and didn't quite know why. "Thank you, Torrance Baker."

"Tor. Please."

"Thank you, Tor."

She went out. She shut the door behind her, and stood in the hot corridor with the four plates in her arms, and looked at them, and smiled.

Then she opened the door again, just enough to put her head round it.

He was standing where she'd left him, by the table, looking at the door with that same listening look on his face.

"Sorry," said Karina, with a shy little dip of the chin she'd practiced in a glass since she was twelve. "I don't suppose you know where Alphie is?"

"Next door," said Tor. "On the left."

"Thank you."

She shut the door. She didn't go next door.

* * *

Tor stood by the table and looked at the shut door, and frowned.

It had been there again, at the end. When she'd smiled at him and said *obviously you*. Not the shock this time. Something else. It had come off her the way warmth came off a loaf: not anything she said, and not her face, which had been very kind. Underneath. He'd felt Dallow's gladness like that in Tovey's hall that afternoon, warm and plain. This was different. It was quick and bright and pleased with itself, like a cat that has found the cream left out. Not cruel. There wasn't anything cruel in it. It was just very much about *her*. As if the plates were going somewhere, and he wasn't.

He didn't know what to do with that. He wasn't sure it was his business to know. She was Rolph's sister, and she'd been nice, and she'd been surprised, and he'd been rude and told her so. He put it away.

He looked round the room. It was cool now, all through, beautifully cool, the curtains hanging still and the white flowers quite upright in their bowl. He could have stood there all evening.

But he couldn't. He'd have to go to dinner. He'd have to walk all the way along that hot corridor, and down those stairs, and through whatever was at the bottom of them, and sit in some room that wasn't this one, with Rolph's mother, and sweat into his good shirt, and drip on the tablecloth, and—

He looked at the plate on the table.

"I'd take you with me if I could," he told it.

And stopped.

He stood very still. His hands, which had been hanging at his sides, came up, slowly, and turned palm upward, and looked at nothing.

"Not the room," he said. "Not the *room*. Me."

The plate cooled a room because the room was a box, and it took the warm out of the box and sent it down. But a person was a box. Near enough. A person was a warm box, walking about. And you didn't need to cool the whole room around a person, any more than you needed to heat the whole sky to keep a loaf warm; you only needed the bit right next to them. A hand's width. Less. The air that touched you. And the shield already held a skin of air round a person, an egg of it, all day long, and let the rest go by. So if the shield held the skin, and something like the plate kept the skin at the right warmth—

"Not cold," he said, aloud. "*Right*. Cool when it's hot. Warm when it's cold. Just *right*. Like the cellar. All the time."

And where did you put the warm? You couldn't put it in the ground; you weren't standing in the ground; you were walking about on a carpet on the second floor. Unless—unless you could reach it anyway. The wall plate reached. Straight down, through the house, through the foundations, into the cellar-place underneath where it was always the same. If you were clever, if you were very clever, you could make it reach from wherever you were standing. Steal a little from the ground. Give a little back. Feed it the difference.

He was at the table. He didn't remember crossing to it. He had a pencil, from somewhere, and the back of the drying-field list from his satchel, and he was drawing: an egg, a person in it, an arrow going down out of the bottom of the egg, a hook—

* * *

Rolph knocked twice, and then went in, because there was never any point knocking twice on Tor.

The room was cool. That was the first thing; it stopped him in the doorway like a hand. The second thing was the plate, lying in the middle of the green tablecloth like a dish nobody had served. The third thing was Tor, sitting at the far end of the table in a clean shirt with wet hair and bare feet, with four sheets of paper in front of him covered in eggs, and his lips moving.

Rolph knew that look. He crossed the room and took Tor by the shoulder and shook him, not hard, the way you'd shake a man out of a doze in church.

"Tor. *Tor.* Dinner."

Tor blinked. He looked up at Rolph, and then at the window, where the light had gone long and gold, and then at the papers, as if somebody else had left them there.

"Is it the eighth bell?"

"Nearly." Rolph glanced at the eggs. One of them had a small person in it, with an arrow coming out of the bottom of the egg and going down through a lot of wavy lines. "What's that?"

"I don't know yet." Tor put the pencil down, reluctantly. "Something."

"Well, leave it. Mother's here." He paused. "Father couldn't make it."

Tor stood up. He looked at his feet, and went and found his boots in the doorway, and put them on, sitting on the floor. Then he stood up again, and looked down at himself, at his clean shirt and his brown tunic and his brown trousers, school brown, the best he had, and Rolph saw his face change.

"Rolph," he said. "Is this all right? To go down in?"

"It's fine."

"It's *brown*." He plucked at it. "Your sister was in a dress. A proper one. With—" he gestured, helplessly "—with the bit. At the shoulders. And I'm going to your mother's dinner in my school things, like I've come to deliver the coal."

Rolph stopped in the doorway. He looked round.

"Karina was in here?"

"Earlier. She came in by mistake. Looking for you." Tor was still worrying at his tunic. "She was very nice. I gave her some plates."

"Some," said Rolph.

"Four. Two for her and two for Varley."

Rolph closed his eyes, briefly. He saw Lady Arden's morning room. He saw Lady Arden's face. He saw the next six weeks of breakfasts, and Karina coming down to every one of them looking like a cat in a dairy, and the way she'd say *oh, Tor gave them to me* to every girl in the city, and the letters that would come, and the way Mother would look over the top of them.

"Very generous of you," he said.

"Was it wrong?"

"No. No, it's—" He opened his eyes. "It's very like you. Come on." Then, seeing that Tor's hand was still on his tunic: "Stop that. Tor, listen. It doesn't matter. It's only us tonight, and nobody's going to look at your clothes. And anyway, that's half of what we're here for. To get you comfortable. And get you some proper things for the summer. There's a man Mother uses."

"I can't afford a man Mother uses."

"Then we'll start with mine." Rolph looked him up and down, consideringly. "I've got a whole press full of things I grew out of when I was fourteen. Some of them would go round you twice. We'll have a look tomorrow." He held the door. "Now come on. And, Tor?"

"Yes?"

"The voice."

Tor looked at him for a moment. Then he straightened his back, and lifted his chin a hair, and said, in a voice like a long table with a cloth on it, "Of course. After you, Rolph."

Rolph laughed, and then didn't, and led the way down.

* * *

The small dining room was the room the family used when there was nobody to impress, and Connie loved it for that. It had a table that sat twelve, and so sat five tonight in a little knot at the end of it, by the open windows onto the fig walk; and it had a painted ceiling of birds and vines that nobody had looked at in thirty years; and it had, at present, eight people serving five, which was perhaps two more than the evening strictly needed, but the kitchens had heard there was a guest.

She watched him come in behind Alphonse.

She'd had eleven letters about him. She'd read them all twice and some of them more, and she'd built a boy out of them, piece by piece, the way you build a man out of a dispatch: *small. Poor. Clever beyond anything. Works until he falls down. Bakes. Apologizes to the furniture.* She'd expected, she realized, a sort of shy brown mouse, who'd hang back by the door and have to be coaxed to a chair.

He was small. He was in brown. And he came across the room toward her at Alphonse's shoulder with his back straight and his chin up, not too high, quite unhurried, and stopped at the right distance, and bowed, not deeply, from the shoulders, the way young men had bowed at her table for twenty years, and said:

"Good evening, ma'am. Thank you so much for having me."

It was the voice that did it. It was a perfectly ordinary voice. It was a voice she heard every day of her life, round every table in the city that mattered: well-schooled, a little lazy in the vowels, pleasant. Connie had been prepared for Two Rivers. She heard the Hill.

She revised the mouse.

"Torrance," she said, and gave him her hand. "At last. Come and sit by me. I've a great many questions, and Alphonse has been hoarding you."

"He's been very kind, ma'am."

"He's been very selfish. Sit."

He sat. He sat on her left, with Karina across from him, very bright-eyed and very pleased with herself in her blue, and Varley beside her, sixteen and watchful, who had been told at some length by her sister what had happened upstairs and plainly hadn't decided yet what she thought about it. Alphonse sat at the foot of the little knot, by the window, where he could see everybody.

The servers came. The soup came: cold, green, with a curl of cream on it, because nobody in the city ate hot soup in this heat if they could help it. It was set before Connie, and before each of them in turn. The server at Torrance's elbow, a young man named Peck who had been in the house a year, set the bowl down and stepped back.

And Torrance, who had had nothing since the posting house at noon, and who had just been told to sit, and who was nothing if not obedient, picked up his spoon, and smiled at the soup, and took the first mouthful.

There was a stillness.

It was very small. A stranger wouldn't have seen it. Peck's hand, which had been reaching for the next bowl, stopped halfway, in the air. Varley's eyes went straight to her mother's face. Karina's mouth twitched at one corner. At the foot of the table, Alphonse put his own spoon down very gently on the cloth, the way you'd put down something that might go off.

In this house, at this table, at every table it had ever laid, the first bite was the host's. It always had been. You sat; the food came; the host ate, and you watched her eat, and then you ate, and that was how you knew. There were people in the city who'd have stood up and left the room.

Torrance swallowed. He looked up from the bowl, with an expression of simple delight.

"Oh, that's *lovely*," he said, in the Hill's voice. "Is that cucumber? We do one at home with sorrel. Mother puts the cream in before it's cold, which you're not meant to."

Connie picked up her spoon and took her own mouthful, quite unhurried, as if she'd been about to all along.

"It is cucumber," she said. "And I think your mother is quite right."

Peck's hand finished its journey. Varley let her breath out. The meal went on.

* * *

He watched Alphonse's hands after that. Connie saw him do it. He wasn't clumsy about it; he did it the way a good rider watches the horse in front over a fence. When Alphonse took up a fork, Torrance took up the same fork, a beat later. When Alphonse put his knife across the plate, Torrance's knife went across his. There were, she supposed, rather a lot of forks. She had stopped seeing them about the age of six.

And he talked. He'd been told to; she could see that, too, he'd been told to talk to people, and he did it like a duty: he answered every question she asked him, fully and honestly and politely, and then waited, with his hands in his lap, for the next one. Two Rivers. His family. ("A lot of us. There's a T-name for every one. Mother says she ran out and had to start again.") The school. The building master. Kolb. ("He's very fair. He's going to kill me, I think, eventually. But fairly.") Varley laughed at that, out loud, and then looked startled at herself.

It was Karina, in the end, who did it.

"Mother," she said, over the fish, in the light, careless voice she used when she had been waiting all evening to say a thing, "did Alphie tell you? Tor's brought cooling plates. He's given me two."

Connie looked at her daughter.

"Two," she said.

"And two for Varley. Haven't you, Tor?"

"I brought them for everybody," said Torrance, a little anxiously, as if he'd been caught out in something. "I've a few more. In the trunk. I didn't know who'd want them. I'm sorry, I should have asked you first, ma'am, they're your walls—"

"Torrance," said Connie. "How does it work?"

And he forgot to be shy.

She watched it happen. She'd watched a great many men talk about the thing they knew best in the world, at this table and others, and most of them got louder. Torrance didn't. He got *quicker*. He put his fork down, and his hands came up off his lap and began to draw in the air, and the Hill went out of his voice without his noticing, a little at a time, and something else came into it: something from a long way east, with flour on it.

"It's the cellar," he said. "Do you know, ma'am, when you go down into a cellar in summer, how it's cool? And in winter it's warm, warmer than the kitchen? That's because down there it's always the same. The ground doesn't care what the weather's doing. It's like a great big— like a bank, really, that you can put warm into and take warm out of. And the plate just reaches down. Through the house. Into the ground under it. And it carries the warm. It doesn't *make* any, that's the important part, making's dear, it just *carries* it, the way you'd carry water in a bucket, from where there's too much to where there's not enough. And in summer there's too much up here and not enough down there, so it carries it down. And in winter you turn it round—"

The servers had stopped moving. Connie noticed that. Peck was standing against the wall with a sauceboat in his hand, quite still, listening. So was the man beside him.

"—and over a year," said Torrance, "it more or less pays itself back. The ground lends you the warm in winter, and you give it back in summer. So you hardly need to feed it anything at all. It'll go for years." He stopped. He seemed to hear himself; the Hill came back into his voice in a rush, and his hands went down into his lap. "Sorry. I've gone on."

"You have not," said Varley, before her mother could. And then, very red, to her plate: "Sorry. Go on. I mean— is that why it's copper?"

He turned to her with real pleasure, as if she'd given him a present.

"Yes! Well, partly—"

Connie sat back, and let them have it, and looked at Karina. Karina was looking at Torrance, with an expression her mother knew very well, the expression of a girl doing sums.

Then Connie looked down the table at her son. Alphonse was looking at Torrance too, and his face was doing something she hadn't seen it do since he was small: proud, and fond, and a little bit afraid, all at once, as if he were watching somebody walk along the top of a high wall.

* * *

Alphonse came to her afterwards, as she'd known he would, in her sitting room, with the windows open on the dark and the moths coming in to the lamp.

He shut the door. He stood with his back to it.

"He took the first bite," said Connie.

"He doesn't know."

"No. I could see he didn't." She turned her rings, one after another, the way she did. "He doesn't know a great many things, does he."

Alphonse said nothing.

"Does he know anything at all?"

"He thinks we're merchants." Her son's voice was quite flat. "Big ones. Near the palace. He thinks we supply the king with something. I don't know what. Cloth, possibly. He asked me if it was cloth." He paused. "He saw George's lot at the gate, and he knew them. He knew exactly what they were. And then he decided we must just live very close."

Connie was quiet for a while.

"Good," she said at last.

He looked at her.

"Let him like us first," said Connie. "Let him like us *first*, Alphonse. Before anything else. He'll find out soon enough. Your father's home in the morning." She held out her hand to him, and after a moment he came and took it. "I liked him. I didn't expect to. I expected to find him useful, and I do. But I liked him." She considered. "Karina is going to be impossible."

"Karina has two plates."

"Karina has two plates," his mother agreed, "and Lady Arden has one."

She watched her son's face. She watched him hear what she'd said, both halves of it, the *liked* and the *useful*, and lay them side by side, and not know which one to pick up. She thought he probably never would. She thought, too, that it was very possibly the best thing about him.

"Go to bed," she said, and kissed him.

* * *

The cool was waiting for Tor when he opened the door.

He stood in it for a moment, in the dark, and let it come round him. Then he lit a candle, and then another, and set them at the end of the great table, and pulled out a chair.

Down at the other end, where he'd left them, were his four sheets of eggs.

He sat and looked at them. He was tired, he supposed; he'd been up before the fifth bell and flown the length of the kingdom and eaten more forks than he'd known existed. But it wasn't the right kind of tired. It was the kind that buzzed.

He thought about dinner. Varley asking about the copper, and going red, and asking anyway. Karina doing sums; he'd felt her doing sums, all through the fish, like an abacus going in the next room. Rolph's mother, who'd let him talk, and had looked at him, the whole time, as if she wanted to know every single thing. Who had been kind. Who'd sat there in her dark dress, at the end of her table, with a little shine of sweat on her lip all through dinner, the whole of dinner, in the heat, and never once said so.

And tomorrow there was luncheon, at the Debri house, in the middle of the afternoon, in the hottest part of the day, and Trice and Sara would come, and Tovey, all of them, in their good things, across the whole hot city.

He'd brought presents. He'd brought a whole trunk of presents, and they were all for walls.

Tor pulled the eggs toward him.

It wasn't hard. That was the thing. He could see it all the way through already, the way you could see through a loaf when you'd made a thousand of them: it was only the wall plate, made small. Made to hang on a cord, like the shield, against your chest. The shield to hold the skin of air; the plate to keep it right; a hook down into the ground to steal a little warm and give it back. It wasn't a new thing at all. It was an old thing, folded up small enough to carry.

One night. A short one. He'd be done before the fifth bell, and asleep before anyone was up, and at luncheon they'd all be cool.

He fetched the copper from his trunk, a stack of small squares, and the acid and the resist and the little brushes, and laid them out on the green cloth beside the plate. He counted out ten squares, because you always did ten. Connie. Karina. Varley. Rolph. Trice and Sara and Tovey. Rolph's father, who couldn't make it. One for himself, which he'd test. And one spare, in case.

He squared the stack. He put his hands flat on the cloth on either side of it, and looked at the first square, and let his breathing slow.

"Right," said Tor, quietly, to nobody.

And the candles burned down, and the house went to sleep around him, and the room stayed cool.
