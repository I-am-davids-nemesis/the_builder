Baker came down to the yard after combat with a sack over his shoulder, and Kolb knew what was in it before he'd put it down.

It was the way he carried it. Not like copper. Copper, Baker carried like bread, any old how, swinging. This he carried like eggs.

"Sir," said Baker. "If you've a moment, sir."

Kolb had a moment. The yard was empty but for the two of them and the pell, and the long shadows of the racks lying across the sand, and a first-year raking the far end who was making very slow progress now that something interesting was happening. Kolb looked at him. The first-year raked faster.

Baker set the sack on the bench by the water butt and took out a belt. Brown leather, plain, a little stiff, with a copper plate riveted flat at the back of it, a hand and a half wide. Then another belt. Then a third. Then a small cloth bag that clicked.

"They're the flying ones, sir," said Baker. "I've given the others theirs. Rolph and Trice and Miss Sara and the Count. But it was your idea, sir. So I thought the school ought to have some. For you to manage." He undid the cloth bag and tipped three flattened copper eggs into his palm, each on its loop of cord. "The control goes on the wrist. Always on the wrist. If you drop it, the plate keeps on doing whatever you last told it, and it goes on doing it for about a year. So you mustn't drop it."

"A year," said Kolb.

"About a year, sir. Up, mostly." Baker looked at the eggs in his hand, and then at Kolb, with his polite, braced face. "I'd put that in the rules, sir. If it was me."

Kolb picked up a belt. It was lighter than it looked. He turned it over and looked at the etching on the plate, which meant nothing to him and never would, and then at the stitching, which was good, and then at Baker.

He'd stood in a valley once, a long way from here, and looked up.

"It'll go in the rules," he said.

"Thank you, sir."

"Who's flown it?"

"Me, sir. To Hollin." Baker went a little pink. "And back. Twice since. And the Count, a little. He's quite good. He says it's not the same as the other way." He hesitated. "I don't think he likes to talk about the other way, sir."

"No," said Kolb. "He wouldn't."

He put the belts back in the sack, one at a time, the way you'd put away a set of good knives. The first-year at the far end of the yard had stopped raking altogether. Kolb didn't look round.

"Two hundred," he said, "if that rake stops again."

The rake started.

"Off you go, Baker," said Kolb. "Nice and loose."

Baker went. Kolb stood by the water butt with the sack at his feet and listened to the bell tower count the fifth bell of the afternoon, and didn't move for a while.

* * *

After that, for nearly a month, Tor was a student.

He went to the building master at the sixth bell and to Doris at the seventh. He sat through theory with his notes in order. He ran to Hollin when Kolb said, on his feet, along the road, and slapped the stone of the well with everyone else, and didn't look up. He handed in an essay on time. Rolph, who had been keeping count without saying so, made it nineteen days without a trance, which was a record, and he didn't mention it, in case it heard him.

Then, halfway through the spring, the weather turned hot.

It came on overnight, the way the cold had, as if somebody had opened an oven door. By the noon bell the court was white with glare and the stones gave the heat back up through your boots, and in theory two people fell asleep, and the master let them. Rolph spent the afternoon in the cool of the library with a book open on his chest. When he came up the stair at the sixth bell, the corridor was quiet and pleasant and smelled of stone, and the boys who had leaned on the wall in the cold snap had their doors open to let the cool through, and one of them waved at him from his bed with a damp cloth over his face and said, "Bless that boy."

Rolph opened the door of their room and the heat hit him like a sack of meal.

It was an oven. The window was shut. The air was thick and still and smelled of hot copper and hot ink and boy. And at the desk, in his shirtsleeves, with his hair stuck flat to his forehead and his shirt stuck dark to his back and a bead of sweat hanging on the end of his nose, sat Tor, drawing.

The bead fell off. It landed on the drawing. Tor blotted it with his sleeve, without looking, and went on.

Rolph looked at him. He looked at the copper plate on the wall above Tor's bed, the book-sized one with the four nails, that had warmed the whole corridor in the cold and that every other plate in the school had been copied from. He looked back at Tor.

He began to laugh.

He didn't mean to. He leaned on the door frame and laughed until he had to hold on to it, and Tor looked up at last, blinking, as if he'd come up from under water.

"What?"

"*Tor*." Rolph wiped his eyes. "Tor. It's the hottest day of the year."

"Is it?" Tor looked at the window, as if it might tell him. "It is warm."

"It is *warm*. Feel your shirt. You could wring it." Rolph pushed himself off the door frame and went over and put his hand flat on the wall beside the plate. The plaster was cool. Not cold; the plate wasn't doing anything at all. It was sitting there being a plate. "The whole school's turned theirs round. Every room in the east wing. The first-years' long room's like a cellar. Davie's got a queue of people outside his door who want to stand in it. The *infirmary* sister turned hers round. You showed her how. I watched you." He was laughing again. "Everybody in this school is lying in a lovely cool room tonight thanking you by name, and you're sitting in here under the thing you *made*, sweating like a cheese."

Tor looked at the plate.

He looked at it for quite a long time. Then he got up, and went and put his hand on it, and shut his eyes, and his lips moved.

The air changed. Rolph felt it start at the wall and come across the room, not a draught, nothing so rough, just a cool settling, the way the air changes when you go down cellar steps. The sweat on his own neck went cold. The ink smell thinned. Tor stood with his hand on the copper and his eyes shut and the back of his shirt slowly going from dark to less dark, and said, quietly, to the plate, "Sorry."

"Don't apologize to it."

"I wasn't." Tor opened his eyes. "I was. A bit." He sat down on his bed under it and let out a long breath, and his shoulders came down from somewhere round his ears. "Oh, that's *better*."

"I know it's better. Everybody knows it's better. You're the last to know."

"I was busy."

"You're always busy." Rolph sat down on his own bed opposite and kicked his boots off and lay back, and the cool came down on him like a sheet, and he shut his eyes and thought that there were people in this kingdom who would give a great deal of money for this exact feeling, and that he knew some of their names. "Listen. I've been meaning to ask you something. Tovey's going home for the summer."

"Is he?"

"He's got work, he says. *Work*." Rolph opened one eye. "He's going home to sit at the end of a long table and sign whatever his mother has already decided. But he calls it work, so it's work. And he's expecting you."

There was a silence from the other bed.

"Expecting me," said Tor.

"To stay. Over the summer. At the Thompson house in the capital. He hasn't said so to you, because he's Tovey, and he assumes you know, because you're his declared friend, and that's what declared friends do. But he's told people. He's told Trice. He asked her what you eat."

"What I *eat*?"

"So that's Tovey." Rolph sat up. "But I asked first. I asked you in the autumn, if you remember, before you were anybody's declared anything. And it's my mother's house, and she wants to meet you, and she gets what she wants. And anyway—" he waved a hand, generously, at the room, the plate, the drawing, Tor "—you're family. Practically. Brothers beat friends. That's the rule."

"Whose rule?"

"Everybody's. Trust me." Rolph grinned. "So you're staying with us. Tovey can visit. We're nearer everything anyway. His people are out on the Hill. It's very nice, if you like being half an hour from anywhere in a carriage. We're right in the middle. Everybody has to come past us to get anywhere." He stopped, and considered that, and moved on. "You'd like it. It's big and draughty and there's a lot of forks, but there's a garden, and there's a kitchen the size of this whole wing, and nobody in it would let you near the ovens, so you could have a rest."

Tor was quiet.

Rolph knew that quiet. It wasn't the building quiet. It was the other one, the one where Tor went politely somewhere inside himself to see whether he was allowed something.

"I ought to go home," said Tor. "In the summer. I always go home."

"All right."

"For the harvest. They need—" He stopped. He was counting. Rolph watched him do it, on his fingers, against the blanket. "There's Terlee. And Mother. And Father, and the twins, and Tam, and Tilda's back from her place now. And Tessa's nine." He looked at the finger. "She can knead. She was just starting to, at midwinter. If she's kneading, then Tilda doesn't have to watch her, so that's—" The finger moved. "That's two. That's two people, really. That's more than me." He looked at his hand, and then at Rolph, with an expression Rolph couldn't quite read: surprised, and pleased, and a little bit something else. "They don't need me."

"They'll miss you."

"Oh, they'll miss me," said Tor, quite cheerfully. "But they don't *need* me. That's different." He thought about it a moment longer. "I'd write. I'd send the money. Mother'd say go. She'd say it in a voice, but she'd say go."

"So that's yes."

"If your mother really doesn't mind."

"My mother," said Rolph, "has written to me about you eleven times since the autumn."

Tor went pink. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be in the capital in the summer." Rolph lay back down, enormously pleased with himself, and put his arms behind his head. "We'll go down together the day after term. It's six days in the coach, but the inns are good once you're past the river. You won't need much. Mother'll want you in something decent the minute you arrive; she'll have a man in. But bring what you like. Books. Your copper. Trunks, a couple."

"Trunks," said Tor.

"A couple. I take four." Rolph shut his eyes. "Anyway. The house. You'll want to know the house. There's the front, which is all stairs and nobody you'd want to talk to, and then there's the garden side, where we actually live. My sisters have the east end. Don't go in the east end. They'll eat you alive, and they'll be very charming about it. And the library's on the river side, and you can see the whole city from the top of it, all the roofs, Tovey's whole street. You can see right across to the Hill, actually, on a clear day. We used to throw things at it. Not *at* it. Toward it."

There was the small scratching sound of a pen.

Rolph opened one eye. Tor was at the desk again, in the cool now, with his back to the room and his head down, drawing. Not the thing he'd been drawing when Rolph came in. A new sheet. Rolph could see it from the bed, over Tor's shoulder: a square box, very neatly ruled, with a strap round it. A trunk. And under the trunk, a flat plate. And beside the trunk, a small stick figure with a belt on, and an arrow from the figure to the trunk, and a second arrow, curving, from the trunk back to the figure, and a word under it that Rolph couldn't make out.

"There's a great hall," Rolph said, watching him, "where they do the winter dinners, and the ceiling's got all the old gods on it, and one of them's got the wrong number of fingers, and you're not allowed to say."

"Mm," said Tor. He drew a second trunk. Then a third, smaller, with a question mark on it.

"And my father has a dog called Admiral that's afraid of the stairs."

"Mm."

Rolph smiled at the ceiling. He'd lost him; he knew the signs. Some present for his mother, probably. Tor had been asking him for a month what she might like, and whether a lady in the capital would think a heater was rude.

He let him draw. He lay on his bed in the cool, in the room that had been an oven, and listened to the pen going, and thought about his mother's eleven letters, and about what he'd say in the twelfth, and about the ceiling in the great hall, and about how Tor was going to look the first time he walked under it, and found that his stomach had done something small and anxious that he didn't care to look at. He told it to stop. It mostly did.

Behind him Tor said, very softly, to the drawing, "And it'd have to *follow*. Not just come. Follow." And drew another arrow.

* * *

The letter came on the third day after that, with the noon post, and Sara read it standing in the cloister, and then read it again, and then went to find Tor.

He wasn't in the room. Rolph, coming out of it, said he'd gone to the workroom on the east stair and had taken the copper with him and had the look, and that if Sara wanted him in this world she should hurry. She hurried.

The workroom was the little one at the top, the one with the slate on the door that said *BAKER* in a hand that had gotten more confident since the spring. The window was open. Tor was at the bench with a sheet of copper in front of him the size of a tea tray, painting resist onto it in long careful lines, with his tongue between his teeth, and when she knocked he jumped and the line went wobbly.

"Miss Sara. Sorry. Come in." He looked at the wobbly line, sighed, and set the brush down. "It's all right. It's only a trial."

"What is it?"

"It's for luggage." He said it as if that explained it. "Is something wrong?"

Sara didn't sit down. She'd thought on the stairs about how to put this, and had decided on the way she'd been taught: the problem, then the size of the problem, then the ask, and no adjectives.

"There's a town called Ashford," she said, "on the river, about ten days south of here. The house trades through it. Grain, mostly, and wool going the other way. In the spring floods the sewers backed up and broke into the cistern, and the cistern feeds the town wells, and now the water's foul all through the lower town. There's flux. There've been deaths: children, and old people, mostly. They're boiling what they can, but there's not enough wood in a river town to boil water for eleven thousand people, and the purifiers they have are the little ones. They can't keep up." She held out the letter. "The house wants to know if there's anything that can be done. It's asked me to ask you."

Tor took the letter. He read it. He read it the way he read everything, all the way through, including the seal.

Then he frowned.

"But that's only a purifier," he said.

"Yes."

"Everyone's got a purifier. My mother's got one. It's on the water jug at home, on a bit of slate, it's older than I am." He turned the letter over, as if there might be something more difficult on the back. "That's the first thing anybody learns, Miss Sara. That's the *second-year* sigil. You find what isn't water and you tell it to go and sit at the bottom. There's nothing to it."

"For a jug," said Sara.

"Well, yes, for a jug, but—"

"Ashford hasn't got a jug. It's got a cistern the size of the chapel and a river, and eleven thousand people drinking out of it."

"It's the same thing," said Tor. "It's just more of it." He sounded, Sara thought, almost offended: not at her, at the problem, for being so ordinary. "It's silly. It's a silly thing to be stuck on. The trick's the same. It's only that a jug field doesn't reach, and if you make it reach it gets thin, and if it gets thin it gets lazy and lets things through, and then you've got a lazy field in your cistern telling eleven thousand people the water's clean when it—"

He stopped.

He was looking at the copper on the bench. Not at the luggage line. At the copper.

"Unless you didn't make it reach," he said. "Unless you didn't make it bigger at all. Unless it stayed jug-sized, and you made the *water* go past it. All of it. A bit at a time. Like a sieve in a—" His hand had started to move, drawing in the air over the bench: a box, a line going in, a line coming out. "The cistern's already got a pipe in and a pipe out. It must have. So you don't put it *in* the cistern. You put it on the *pipe*. And you put ten, one after the other, so if one gets lazy the next one—" He looked up. "How big's the pipe?"

"I don't know."

"Ask them. No—" He was already reaching for a fresh sheet. "No, it doesn't matter, I'll make it so it doesn't matter. You'd want to be able to bend it round. Copper bends." He was writing. "Ten. And a template. That's two days, I think. Maybe three, with the template. Would three be too long?"

"Tor," said Sara. "Three days is fast. Three days is—" She stopped, and found her contract voice, and used it, because otherwise she'd have said something foolish. "Three days would be very acceptable."

"All right." He was still writing. Then he put the pen down, and looked at the letter again, and at the seal on it, and his face did something she didn't expect. "Children," he said.

"Yes."

He nodded, once, and got up.

Sara watched him pack. She'd watched him go into a trance before. She'd watched him go in with no food and come out with none, gray and stiff and blinking, and had once, after the template, sat beside Trice on a bench in the undercroft while Rolph went for bread. So she watched now with her arms folded and her mouth already open to say it, and didn't need to.

He went down to the kitchens and came back with a basket. In the basket was a loaf, and a second loaf, and a round of hard cheese in a cloth, and six apples, and a crock of something with a lid tied on, and a big stone bottle of water with its stopper wired in, and a bag of oatcakes, and a knife. He checked it over the way his mother must once have checked his school box. Then he put in a seventh apple.

"Trice will ask," he said, not looking at her. "Tell her I packed."

"I'll tell her."

"Tell her I packed *properly*."

"I'll tell her you packed properly," said Sara, and found she had to look out of the window for a moment.

He went down the east stair with the basket on one arm and the tea-tray copper under the other and a roll of drawings in his teeth, and at the bottom he turned toward the old wing, and the worn stair, and the undercroft. He didn't look back. He was muttering round the drawings. Sara stood at the top of the stair and listened to him go, and to the bell, which began just then to ring the first bell after noon, and counted it without meaning to, and then went to write a letter in her contract voice that would not say *three days* in case it turned out to be four, and would not say *children* at all.

* * *

Bet Haskins had looked in on him every bell for two days. She had a slate by the door with the marks on it to prove it, forty-nine, in her own chalk, under his: *BAKER, T. — In: second bell after noon, third day.* For most of those bells he'd been sitting on the floor of the chamber with his back to the wall and a big copper plate across his knees, and his lips moving. Twice he'd been eating an apple, without looking at it. Once, in the dark of the second night, he'd been asleep, curled on his side round the plate like a dog round a bone, and she'd stood at the shutter longer than she needed to and then gone back to her bench.

On the fifth day, at the third bell of the afternoon, the chamber door opened, and he came out.

He came out slowly, the way they all did, with one hand on the door frame. He was carrying a crate, with ten long copper sheets standing up in it on their edges, bent into a curve like a half pipe, and a square of pale unpainted wood laid flat on top with ink on it. The basket was over his arm, quite empty. He looked tired and gray and very young, and he blinked at the lamp in the passage, and he said, "Oh. Hello, Mistress Haskins," as if he'd met her in the street.

"Well, then," said Bet.

Then she saw what was behind him, and didn't say anything else.

There were four of them. Flat copper plates, square, each about the size of a tea tray, and they came out of the chamber after him one behind the other at the height of a man's knee, floating, in a line. When he stopped, they stopped. The first one bumped very gently into the back of his leg, and he said "Sorry" to it, and it backed off a hand's breadth and hung there, waiting.

Bet looked at the plates. The plates, in so far as plates can, looked at nothing.

"They're for luggage," said Tor. He set the crate down on her bench and took up the chalk. "You put a trunk on, and they follow. They follow the belt." He touched the plain brown belt round his own waist, as if she'd know what that meant. "I needed four. Rolph takes four. I think I might need five. I don't know what you take to the capital."

He signed out. *Out: third bell of the afternoon, fifth day.* And under it, as he always did, *Thank you.*

Then he picked up the crate again and went along the passage toward the worn stair, and the four plates swung round in a line and went after him, at knee height, in perfect order, bobbing a little, the way a row of ducklings goes after the duck. At the foot of the stair he stopped, and they stopped. He went up a step. They considered this. Then the first one rose, very carefully, a step's height, and came on, and the others came after it, one step at a time, all the way up, into the light.

Bet Haskins sat down on her bench.

She sat there for some time. After a while she reached over and picked up the slate, and looked at the forty-nine marks, and the *Thank you*, and put it back on its nail.

She would have something to say at breakfast.
