The capital came up out of the haze the way it always did, as a line first, white and too straight to be anything that had grown there, and then all at once as roofs and towers and the dome, with the river laid along the bottom of it like a gray ribbon somebody had forgotten to pick up. Rolph had been seeing it come up like that all his life, from coaches and barges and once, aged nine, from the top of a hay cart he'd had no business being on. He'd never seen it from up here without feeling it in his chest. He felt it now, and with it the third bell after noon, and his father at the head of a table, and the faint, standing dread of being late for one's own parents.

Then he saw the crowd.

It was below the wall, outside it, on the near bank downstream, where the tanners had their yards: a flat, sour, trodden stretch of sheds and drying frames and square pits, with hides stretched on racks in rows like washing nobody would want to wear. Rolph could smell it from three hundred feet. He always could; the whole city could, when the wind was wrong. In the middle of it, between the pits, there was a knot of people packed tight and dark, and in the middle of the knot a ring of red caps, standing very still, and around them the crowd heaving and pressing. And from the river gate, along the path, men were running. They had planks on their shoulders, long pale ones that bounced as they ran, and they were running the way men only run when somebody important has shouted at them.

"Riot," said Trice, beside him, flatly.

Rolph didn't answer. He was thinking of other things it might be. A hanging, done badly. A fight that had gone to knives. A man from the Hill with his horses in the tanners' way and his guards drawn, and a crowd that had had a hot summer and a long drought and had decided, all at once, that it had had enough of the Hill. His father would want to know. His father would already know. Rolph tipped his wrist down and went, faster than Kolb would have liked, and heard the others come down behind him.

They landed at the back of the crowd, on the hard bald earth by a drying frame. Rolph was out of his belt before his feet had properly stopped. Beside him Tor came down neatly, the way he always did now, and didn't unbuckle. He was looking at the ground.

Not at the crowd. Not at the guards. At the ground, by his own boots, with his head a little on one side, the way he looked at a square when he was reading it.

"Someone's down a hole," he said, quietly.

Rolph didn't hear him. He heard it afterward, the way you hear a thing in memory that you walked straight past at the time; but just then he was already moving, shouldering into the backs of tanners and their wives and a boy with a tray of buns and two old men who swore at him, and the crowd gave in front of him because crowds generally did, and he came out at the front by the ring of red caps and opened his mouth, and what came out of it wasn't his voice at all.

"*Situation report!*"

It was Kolb's. Rolph knew it the instant he heard it. It was the voice that came across the practice yard on a wet morning and found you behind the pell where you thought you were hiding, and he had never in his life used it, or known he had it; and it went out across the tanners' yard like a thrown brick. The crowd shut up. The red caps turned. A man in the middle of them, a captain by his sash, with his white tunic gone gray at the armpits and a face the color of raw liver, looked at Rolph, and Rolph saw him see a very large young man who had just dropped out of the sky and was barking at him; and the captain's mouth answered before the rest of him had decided to.

"Child," said the captain. "Boy. Seven. Tanners' boy." He jerked his head behind him. "Went down an old well. Midden, really. Been a midden since my grandfather."

And that was all the attention he had to spare for Rolph, who he was, or where he'd come from. He'd already turned away, toward the path, where the first of the running men had arrived with their planks and were laying them down in the dirt.

"No," said the captain. "No. *No.* What are those? Those are *palings*. I said timbers. Those won't hold a cat." His voice went up on the last word and cracked, and stayed up. "We have to put a man down! Proper timbers, *now*, the big ones off the bridge stores, go *on*—"

Rolph looked past him and saw why.

The hole wasn't much. That was the first thing. It was a ragged mouth in the trodden earth between two of the tanning pits, a couple of feet across, no more, with a rim of rotten brick round part of it where there'd been a wall once, a long time ago, before anyone now living. The earth round it was dark and greasy-wet. And it was moving. Not much. Rolph had to look twice. A little dribble of dirt ran down off the rim and into the dark, and stopped, and a moment later another one ran down somewhere else, and stopped, the way the sand goes in the top of a glass; and as he watched, a piece of the rim the size of a loaf leaned, quite slowly, and went.

Sticking up out of the hole were a pair of legs in red-and-white stockings, and three enormous men in leather aprons had hold of the ankles.

"Pull him," said the captain.

They pulled. The legs came up, and the rest of a guardsman came after them, upside down and brown to the waist with muck, and stinking, and they set him on his feet and he stood there swaying and wiping his face on his sleeve, which only moved it about. He was thin. He was the thinnest man Rolph had ever seen in a uniform; he looked like he'd been made out of a rake. He shook his head before he spoke.

"No good, Captain. There's a bend about my height down. Goes off sideways. I can't get my shoulders round it, and if I dig at it, it all comes down on him; I heard him." He swallowed. "And you can't get a rope round a corner. I tried. It only goes straight down and stops." He looked the captain dead in the eye. "Somebody's got to go down to him. Somebody's got to go *round*."

Nobody said anything. Rolph saw the guards look at one another, and at the thin man, and down at themselves; and he saw them each work it out. If the rake couldn't, nobody could.

A woman cried out behind him.

"*Simon!*"

It wasn't loud. It was worse than loud. Rolph turned and she was there, at the edge of the ring, held back by a guard's arm across her: a young woman, younger than his mother by a long way, with a smudged face and her hands brown to the wrist, the tannery brown that never came off. "Simon," she said again, at the hole, as if it might answer. "Simon, baby, Mama's here—" A broad dark man in a stained shirt and a leather apron stiff as a board had his arms round her from behind, and was saying something into her hair, over and over, and it was doing no good at all, and he was crying too, without seeming to know it.

Rolph looked away from them. It was the only thing he could think to do.

And found Tor at his elbow.

He hadn't heard him come. Tor had got through the crowd somehow, the small way, between elbows, and was standing just behind the ring of guards, perfectly still. He wasn't looking at the mother. He was looking at the thin guardsman, and at the thin guardsman's shoulders, steadily, up and down, the way he'd look at a beam to see if it'd bear; and then at the hole; and then at the ground at the hole's edge, with his head on one side again, as if he were listening to it.

"It's going," Tor said. "Underneath. It's going all the time. Can you feel it?"

Rolph couldn't feel anything but the sun on the back of his neck. He opened his mouth to say so.

"Excuse me," said Tor.

He said it to the captain. He said it the way he said it to the headmaster when he needed a pen, politely, in his best schoolroom voice, the Two Rivers smoothed nearly out of it; and the captain, who was shouting at a man with a plank, didn't hear. Tor touched his sleeve.

"Excuse me, sir. I'm sorry. I'm quite small. I think I'll go round." He was already turning. "Could I have a rope, please? A long one, and a short one. Thank you. Rolph, could you do the plates? All of them. On your trunk, please, it's got the iron on the corners, it's the strongest. All ten." He had his belt off. He had his tunic off, over his head, in one pull, and handed it to Rolph without looking, and started on his shirt. "And your button. And your shield, please. I'll give it back. Quickly, please. I'm sorry. It's going."

The captain had turned round. He looked at Tor. He looked at Tor's shirt coming off, and at Tor's chest, which was white and narrow and had very nearly nothing on it, and at Rolph, who was holding Tor's tunic.

"Who in the gods' name—"

"Do what he says," said Rolph.

He didn't know he was going to say it until he had. It came out in Kolb's voice again, and the captain shut his mouth.

Rolph turned and went back through the crowd at a run for the luggage.

They'd left the trunks at knee height by the drying frame, waiting, the way the plates did. He went down the line yanking straps, Sara's case and Trice's two and Tor's battered box with the books in it, and the plates slid out from under each in turn and hung there in the air looking patient. He got them stacked under his own trunk, all ten, one on another like a pile of plates in a kitchen, and the trunk rose an inch on them as if it had taken a deep breath. He felt in his pocket for the button. When he came back through the crowd with the trunk bobbing after him like a big dog, somebody had found a coil of rope, and Tor was standing by the hole in his drawers.

That was all he had on: a pair of old Two Rivers linen drawers gone gray with washing, and his own shield on its cord round his neck, and a second one wound round his wrist. Rolph's. Rolph put a hand to his own collar and found it bare, and couldn't remember handing it over. Nothing else. He was standing in the middle of the tanners' yard of the capital, in front of two hundred people, and the whole city guard, and Sara, and Trice.

Rolph had seen Tor go scarlet because Trice took her boots off on a sofa. He'd seen him go scarlet at a bare ankle at the palace, and at a statue in the queen's garden that wasn't even a particularly undressed statue. He looked at Tor's face now. It was white. Not a mark of color in it anywhere. He was holding his arms out, a little away from his sides, so that one of the tanners could pass the long rope round his left ankle, and looking at the hole.

That was how Rolph knew how frightened he was.

The man doing the rope was the oldest of the three who'd held the guardsman, with gray in his beard and hands like a pair of old boots. He took the rope twice round Tor's ankle, above the bone, and made a knot in it that Rolph didn't know, and pulled it down hard, and Tor's mouth went thin and that was all.

"That's tight, lad," the tanner said. He didn't let go of it. "It's got to be, or you'll slip out of it, going up. But I'll tell you now. You get that off you in ten minutes. Fifteen at the outside. Any longer and it'll do you damage you'll not walk off."

"Thank you," said Tor. "That's very kind. I will."

He turned round to the ring of guards.

"Excuse me," he said again. "Is there— which of you has the steadiest hands, please? Only, it has to be someone who won't mind doing what I tell him. Even if it seems wrong." He looked at them, all along the ring, small and white and courteous in his drawers. "I'm sorry. It's important."

The guards looked at one another. Then they looked, all of them, at the same man, and the man sighed and stepped out.

He was older than the rest, older than the captain, a short square man with gray stubble and a nose that had been broken at least twice and a long white scar running down into his collar. He had the plain sleeve of a man who'd been offered stripes and turned them down. He came and stood in front of Tor and looked down at him without any expression at all.

Tor took the button out of Rolph's hand and put it into his.

"This is the trunk," he said. "You hold it, and the trunk goes where it is. Up's up. Down's down. Slowly, please, it's a lot of plates; it'll go quicker than you think." The soldier lifted the button an inch, and over by Rolph's knee the trunk lifted an inch, and the soldier's eyebrows went up a hair, and that was the whole of his surprise. "The rope goes on the trunk. I'll go down and get a rope on him. When I shout, take us up. Straight up. Slowly. And don't stop." Tor looked up at him. "Even if I'm screaming. *Especially* if I'm screaming. Don't stop for anything until we're out."

The soldier looked at him a moment longer.

"Got it," he said.

They tied the long rope off on the trunk's iron handle. Tor slung the short one over his shoulder, coiled. Then the soldier lifted his hand, and the trunk went up, and the rope went taut, and Tor's left foot went up with it, and the rest of Tor followed, until he was hanging head-down over the hole by one ankle, turning very slightly, his hands loose above the dark, the cords of the two shields hanging down off him toward it.

Over by the edge of the ring, Rolph saw Trice. She'd got herself between the crowd and the pits with her arms out wide, and was walking the front row backward step by step in a voice like a governess's, and they were going. Beyond her he saw Sara, who'd gone to the mother. She hadn't said anything to her; she'd only taken the woman's brown hand in both of hers and was holding it, hard, and the woman had let her.

"Down," said Tor.

The soldier's hand went down, slowly. Tor went into the ground hands first, and then head, and then shoulders, and the rope paid out after him, and the last of him to go was his left foot, white, with the rope round it; and then there was only the rope.

* * *

It was dark at once, and it stank.

Tor had smelled the tanners' yard from the air and thought it was bad. It hadn't been. This was underneath that, a long way: the smell of things that had been thrown down here for forty years and left, gone sweet and black and sharp all at the same time, so that it was in his eyes as much as his nose. He breathed through his mouth. That was worse. He shut his mouth and breathed through his nose and let his eyes water and went on down.

The walls were close. They scraped at his elbows, and his shoulders, and his ears. Everything he touched was soft and cold. Little runs of dirt came loose where he touched it and pattered past his face, down, ahead of him, into the dark, and he could hear them land, not far off, on something.

"Slowly," he whispered. "Slowly. You're all right."

He could feel it. He'd felt it from the air, very faintly, like a sound in another room, and he'd felt it standing on the edge, and down here it was all round him: the field of the earth, the weight of it, holding itself up. It was holding itself up the way a man holds a heavy box at arm's length. It was all right now. It wouldn't be all right for very long. He could feel it getting tired.

The bend came. The rake-thin guardsman had been right about it: the shaft went down straight, and then it simply went off sideways, at a slant, as if whoever had dug it had changed his mind. Tor put his hands on the lip of it and felt round the corner with his fingers, and then pushed his head in, and then his shoulders, one and then the other, turning, the way you put on a coat that's too small; and they went. Just. He came round the bend with his chin in the dirt.

"Down," he called, and up above someone said *down*, and he slid.

His hand found a foot.

It was a bare foot, very small, with a hard little heel, and it was warm. It jerked when he touched it.

"Hello," said Tor. His voice came out strange, flattened, as if he were talking into a pillow. "Hello. Simon? It's all right. My name's Tor. Your mum's up top. She's waiting for you."

There was a noise below him. It wasn't words. It was the noise a child makes when he's cried for so long there's nothing left to cry with, a sort of hiccup with a squeak in it. Tor went on down, hand over hand along the boy's leg, and found his middle, and his arm, and his face, wet and hot. He was wedged at the bottom of the bend on his side with one arm up over his head, and he was ten feet down at most, and the dirt had come in round him to his waist.

"There you are," said Tor. "There you are. Good lad. Here. Put your head up for me."

He got Rolph's shield off his wrist and the cord over the boy's head, and put his thumb on it, and pressed. He felt it come on: the small soft push of it, opening, and the dirt sliding away off the boy's face and chest in a ring, hissing. The boy gasped, a long one, all the way down, the first real breath he'd had in a while.

"That's it. That's yours now. Keep it on." Tor got the short rope off his shoulder. His hands were shaking and he made them stop. He passed it round under the boy's arms and behind his back and tied it, the knot his father used on the flour sacks, the one that didn't slip, and tugged it, and tied the other end round his own wrist, twice. "Hold on to the rope. Both hands. Hold on to me if you want. You can't hurt me. All right? All right." He twisted his head back toward the light, what light there was, a gray coin a long way up past his own feet.

"*Up!*" he shouted. "Up! Take us up!"

The rope at his ankle took hold.

And past the boy, as he went, farther in, where the shaft bellied out into a sort of pocket under the bend, he saw another face.

It was smaller. It was a good deal smaller than the boy's. It was a little girl, three, perhaps, or four, with her hair stuck flat to her head and her eyes wide open in the dark, looking at him; and she wasn't crying. She'd stopped crying. She was just looking.

"*Wait!*" Tor screamed. "Wait! *Stop!* There's another—"

They didn't stop. Of course they didn't. He'd told them not to. The rope pulled at his ankle, steady, slow, the way he'd asked, and he went up past the corner and the boy came after him, bumping, and the girl's face went past below him, a foot, two feet, and he reached for her and his fingers went through dirt.

He got his own shield off. He tore it off over his head and it took a strip of his ear with it, and he threw it. Underarm, downward, behind him, at the face. He felt it hit her. He felt it, somewhere down there, a little copper weight against her, and he reached after it, not with his hand, with all the rest of him, the way you reach for a jar at the back of a high shelf you can only just touch with your fingertips: *on*. *On. Please come on.*

He didn't know if it did.

The walls came in.

They didn't fall. It wasn't like falling. It was like the earth had been holding its breath all this time and had let it go: it simply leaned, from all sides at once, softly, and came in, and the gray coin of light far above his feet went out, and there was no light at all, anywhere, and the dirt was in his eyes and his nose and down his back and pressing on every part of him, and the girl was under it.

He thought of Rolph.

He thought of Rolph at Ward's table, with his face gone strange, and the force going out of him along a line, straight, carrying everything in its way until it hit something. Tor had stood in that line. He'd stood in it with his shield up and felt it go round him, and he knew the shape of it from the inside, the way you know the shape of a wind by leaning on it. Nobody made those. You didn't build them. You were born with one or you weren't, and Tor wasn't; Tor was a baker's son from Two Rivers.

He made one anyway.

He made it down through the dark from himself to her, through all the moving weight of the earth, a line, a holding, *this to this*, as straight as he could think it. It wasn't any good. It was a thread. It was the thinnest thing he'd ever made. He held on to it.

Filth came into his mouth, a great choking cold handful of it, and he couldn't spit. The rope at his ankle hauled. The earth held him and the rope pulled him, both at once, hard, and his ankle went long, longer than it went, and something in it tore, and went on tearing, and then, with a sound he heard inside his own head, snapped.

The dark went white.

He nearly went with it. He felt himself going, the way you feel the edge of sleep, warm, easy, so easy, just to let go of everything, the thread too—

*No.*

He held the thread.

* * *

The trunk came up, slowly, the way it had been told, and the rope under it came up taut and trembling, and for a long moment that was all there was: the rope coming out of the ground, and the ground round it slumping, and the soldier with the button in his fist and his face like a shut door, not stopping.

Then the earth bulged, and broke, and Tor came out of it feet first.

He came out upside down and brown all over, so brown and so filthy that for a moment Rolph didn't know what he was looking at; and then the arms, hanging, and the face, and the rope from his wrist going down taut into the ground; and then out of the ground after him, on the end of it, came a bundle of muck the size of a small sack that had a faint shimmer all round it, like the air over a road in summer, with the dirt sliding off it in sheets.

The mother screamed. Not a word. Just a scream.

Rolph was moving before he knew it. Tor was saying something. His mouth was going, black inside, and his eyes were open, and he was saying something over and over, but what came out of him was hardly air, and the crowd was roaring now, the whole yard of it, and Rolph couldn't hear a word. The soldier lowered them. He did it beautifully; they came down out of the air and onto the dirt like a pair of eggs into a basket, Tor first, the boy after. Then there were hands, a dozen of them, tanners and guards and a woman with a basket, grabbing Tor under the arms and the boy by the rope and dragging them both back from the hole; and behind them, with a long soft sound like a man sighing in his sleep, the hole fell in.

It didn't fall far. The ground where it had been went down into a bowl, a wide shallow dish of loose earth twenty feet across, and a drying frame at the edge of it leaned over and lay down slowly on its side. Dust came up off it in a low brown cloud and hung.

The father had the boy. He'd got the rope off him somehow and the muck and was holding him, and the boy was crying, really crying now, a high furious bawl, and the shield round his neck had gone dull and quiet. Sara was there with the mother. Everyone was there. The whole crowd had come in round them, shouting and laughing and slapping the guardsmen on the back, and somebody had started to cheer.

Rolph got to Tor.

He'd been set down on his back in the dirt. He wasn't lying on it. He was sitting up, or trying to, on one elbow, with his left leg out in front of him; and the foot at the end of it was at an angle that Rolph looked at once and didn't look at again. He wasn't looking at the foot. He wasn't looking at anything near. He was looking at the bowl, at the middle of it, where the hole had been, and his right arm was out over it, straight, with the hand open and the fingers spread, shaking.

Rolph got down on his knees beside him in the dirt.

"Tor. Tor. It's all right. You got him. Look, he's there, he's—"

"*Move*," said Tor.

It wasn't loud. It was a whisper, and the crowd was so loud that Rolph could only hear it because he was a foot away; and it wasn't Tor's voice. Rolph had heard Tor angry, and frightened, and stubborn, and all of them were in his voice, they were *made* of it. This had nothing in it at all. It was flat, like a table. It was the most frightening thing Rolph had ever heard come out of anybody, and it was a whisper.

Tor's face was turned to the bowl, and his teeth were showing.

"*Move*," he said again. He was trying to shout it. It came out of him in pieces, gasping. "Move. *I command you.* Move—"

The hairs went up on Rolph's arms.

He felt it. He felt it come up through his knees, through the ground, the way you feel a cart go over a bridge when you're standing on it, a long low gathering, *getting ready*; and he knew it. He knew it from the inside. He'd felt that same thing get ready in his own chest at Ward's table, a breath before he'd put a table through a wall.

Nobody else had moved. They were all round the bowl, all two hundred of them, cheering, slapping, crowding to see the boy, standing on its very edge.

"*Run!*" Rolph screamed.

It went up at the end. It went up a long way. It came out of him high and cracked and thin, a sound he hadn't made since he was eight years old and a goose had got him by the backside in the palace kitchen yard, and it had nothing of Kolb in it at all. It didn't matter. The crowd ran. They went away from the bowl in every direction at once like a dropped bag of peas, tanners and guards and the boy with the buns and the captain with them, and Trice at the far side dragged two old men off the edge by their collars, one in each hand.

The ground went up.

It went up all at once, the whole middle of the bowl, as if something underneath had stood. It went up in a column, straight, a great tan pillar of earth, thirty feet, forty, higher than the drying frames, higher than the sheds, and at the top it opened and spread like the head of a fountain in a square and came down again all round in a long hissing rain. Dust came off it in a cloud that went over the sun. Things came up with it. Things that had been down there forty years and ought to have stayed: broken crocks, and black rags, and a boot, and somebody's dinner bones, and a green glass bottle with old letters on it that turned over and over in the light; a rusted iron hoop; a dead cat, regrettably, from some year long ago. They went up and they came down, and the crowd, at a distance now, said *ohhh* all together like the crowd at a fair.

And last of all, out of the top of it, came the girl.

She came up out of the earth like a cork out of a bottle. She was tiny, and brown all over like Tor, and she came turning end over end through the air, slowly, the way the bottle had, with her arms and legs out and her mouth wide open, howling; and in one small fat fist, clutched so tight it shone, she had a square of copper on a cord.

She came down ten feet from Tor. She hit the ground flat on her back, hard, and didn't bounce. She stuck. She lay where she'd landed as if somebody had nailed her there by every edge, arms out, legs out, pressed down flat into the dirt; and round her, very faint, the air shimmered, the way it had round the boy; and she screamed. She screamed and screamed, a high, outraged, ear-splitting squeal, the loudest noise in the whole yard, which was saying something.

Rolph stared at her.

The shield. It had to be the shield; there it was in her fist, and there was the shimmer. Only Rolph had worn one of Tor's shields since the spring, and been knocked flat in it by Kolb more times than he could count, and he had never once seen one hold a body down to the ground like a hand. He looked at the girl, and at the shimmer, and at the copper, and didn't know.

Then the shimmer went out. The girl came up off the ground in a scramble, still screaming, and a woman nobody had noticed came running out of the crowd with her apron up over her mouth and snatched her and ran off with her, both of them screaming, and the crowd closed round them and they were gone.

The rain of earth stopped. The last of it pattered down. The dust began, slowly, to drift away over the river.

Tor had let his arm fall.

He'd got the boy. Rolph didn't know how. The father must have brought him, or the boy had come on his own; at any rate he was there, in Tor's lap, wrapped round him, a small filthy screaming weight with his face in Tor's neck, and Tor had both arms round him and was sitting in the dirt with his broken leg out in front of him, and holding on. He was looking at the place where the girl had been. He wasn't looking at anything.

"She's crying," Tor said.

"She is," said Rolph. "Gods. She is. They both are. Listen to them."

"Crying's good," said Tor. "It means they're—"

His eyes filled. Rolph saw it happen: they went bright, and full, and two clean lines ran down through the mud on his face and dripped off his chin onto the boy's hair. He opened his mouth as if he were going to say something else.

Then, quite simply, with the boy still in his arms, he tipped over sideways, and Rolph caught his head before it hit the ground.

Somewhere up the hill, beyond the white wall, a thin bell began to ring, and a moment after it the great one in the palace tower, so deep that Rolph felt it in the ground under his knees. He counted without meaning to. Three.

Trice came across the bowl toward them through the settling dust. She was brown to the elbows, and her scarf was gone, and there was something in her hair that Rolph decided not to look at.

"We're going to be late," she said.

Rolph looked down at the small, filthy, sleeping face in his hands.

"Yes," he said.
