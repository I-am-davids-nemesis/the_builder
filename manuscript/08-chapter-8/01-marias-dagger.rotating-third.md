"Who?" said Sara.

It came out sharper than she'd meant. She heard it go across the rug, and saw Tor flinch from it, a small, quick tuck of the chin, as if she'd thrown something; and she couldn't have said herself whether she'd said it frightened or furious. Both, perhaps. She'd been raised in a nursery like everyone else's, and Doretta was not a name. Nobody had ever been christened Doretta, not in a hundred years. Doretta was a thing you *were*.

Beside her, Trice uncurled.

She did it slowly. She'd been sitting with her boots tucked under her and the cushion she'd thrown at Rolph in her lap, and she put the cushion down, and sat up straight, and turned her head to look at Rolph; and when she spoke it was so low and so level that the fire nearly had it.

"Was it Maria?"

Sara didn't know the name. She knew the voice. She'd heard Trice use it twice in nine years, once to a boy who'd hit a dog in front of her, and once in the courtyard, in the dark, to Tovey.

Tor looked from one of them to the other, and his face did a thing Sara had seen it do in the yard, when Kolb set Trice on him from the front and Sara from behind: the polite, slightly anxious look of a person who has just noticed that both doors are guarded.

Rolph got up.

He didn't answer straight away. He got up off the rug, all of him, which took a while, and stepped over the honey jar, and went round the end of the hearth to the other side of the fire, where he could stand with his back to the open shutters and the noise of the sea and see Sara and Trice both at once. The firelight came up under his face. He didn't look at Tor. Sara noticed that, because Rolph always looked at Tor; it was the first thing he did in any room.

"Yes," said Rolph.

"Rolph," said Tor. "Don't. Honestly. It's a very old—"

"It's a story, Little Tor. I told you. You're in it." Rolph put his hands behind his back, like his father, and then seemed to notice he'd done it and took them out again. "And a story's got to be told properly, from the beginning, or it goes off. Like milk." He nodded at the crock Walt had brought, on the hearthstone by Tor's knee. "Pass that, would you?"

Tor passed it. He did it the way he'd have handed a cup to a man on a scaffold, carefully, with both hands, and then sat back against the sofa and put his hands between his knees and looked at the fire, with his ears going red; and Sara understood that he was not going to stop this, because he'd never in his life known how to stop Rolph doing anything, and that he would very much rather have been back under the bucket.

Rolph drank from the crock, and wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, and began.

* * *

"Picture it," said Rolph. "First year. I'm twenty. I come down the drive at Lairdgren in a closed carriage with no crest on it, with two trunks and a lockbox and a story about a merchant family that I've practiced in the mirror for a month. And what do I expect? I expect a room. My own room. With a fire in it, and a man to see to the fire. I'm the heir to the whole of Noram; I think a room's the least they can do." He spread his hands. "And the dean takes me up the stairs himself, and opens a door, and there's a second bed in it."

"A second bed," said Trice, gravely.

"A *second bed*. A roommate. Me." Rolph put a hand on his chest. "And on the second bed there's a boy about the size of my leg, in brown, sitting with his knees up, reading a book on sigil theory that he's *already* read, I found out later, twice, because he was nervous and it was the only one he'd brought. And the dean says, 'Mr. Merchant, this is Mr. Baker, from Two Rivers,' and goes away and leaves me there. And I'm standing in the door thinking, Two Rivers. Where in the name of all the gods is Two Rivers?"

"Lots of people know where Two Rivers is," said Tor, to the fire.

"Name one."

Tor thought about it. "Mother," he said.

Trice made a small noise into her hand.

"I worked it out eventually," said Rolph. "Not Two Rivers; that took me a year and a map. Why *him*. Who's the one boy in the whole school that nobody, nobody in a thousand years, is going to look at and think, *there's a prince next to that*? A baker's son from a village no one's heard of, a quarter my size, with an accent you could stand a spoon in." He took another drink. "And I think, it's just possible, the dean thought I could do with learning a little humility. Living with someone who'd had a fraction of my schooling. And was about eight times as clever. I was very put out for a whole afternoon." He grinned, briefly, down at the top of Tor's head. "Then he fixed the window catch. With a bootlace and a sigil. And I liked him, and that was that."

"What happened to the accent?" said Sara. She'd wondered. Tor's voice was Two Rivers when he was tired and nowhere at all when he wasn't.

"*Gone*. That's what happened to it. In days. Hours, possibly. He listened to people talk at supper the way he listens to a field, and he took it to pieces, and then he just—" Rolph made a gesture like a man folding up a map. "Talked like us. It was the most frightening thing I'd ever seen. At the time."

Tor said nothing. He was turning one of the leftover biscuits over in his fingers, round and round, looking at the layers.

"Anyway," said Rolph.

* * *

"There was a girl," said Rolph.

He said it lightly, like the start of a song. Sara watched Tor's hands go still on the biscuit.

"Halfway through the year. Just before the spring dance. A girl in his theory class who sat by the window and laughed with her friends at the long table, and he'd noticed her, and he'd said so, to me, once, in the dark, when he thought I was asleep. And *I*—" Rolph pointed at himself with the crock. "I, who knew everything about everything, said, ask her. Go on. Ask her to the dance. What's the worst that could happen?"

"Oh, Rolph," said Sara.

"I know. I *know*. But listen. I'd looked at her. I'd looked properly, the way I'm taught, and I'd have sworn on my mother's pearls she wasn't peerage. No more than he was. And even if she had been, so what? What's the worst a girl does, when a nice boy asks her to a school dance? Her card's full. She's promised to somebody. She's *so* sorry. Or she's got a friend who'd love to go, and isn't that lucky, and he gets a dance anyway with the friend and everybody's face is saved. That's the worst. That's the *rules*." He looked at Trice. "You'd have done it in your sleep."

"I have done it in my sleep," said Trice.

"And he wasn't asking for anything else. That's the thing. Not a betrothal. Not a walk in the dark. A *dance*. In the hall, with the lamps on and a hundred people and the dancing master tapping his stick. He just thought she was nice, and he'd like to dance with her." Rolph shook his head slowly. "How sweet is that? Gods. I could have put him in a glass case."

"Rolph."

"Sorry, Tor. So. I coached him. Two evenings. I gave him the words, the proper words, the ones that leave every door open, and he practiced them on me till he had them, and I don't mind telling you I was a very convincing girl. And the night before, he went out into the wood behind the stables with a knife and a basket, and he came back at midnight, and he sat up till the second bell doing something at the desk, and in the morning there were flowers."

He stopped. He looked across the fire at Sara, and Sara saw that he wasn't joking any more, not even a little.

"You know how he is with a build," said Rolph. "How it all has to be *right*. Every line. Well, it isn't only builds. He's like that with everything. Bread. Boots. That window catch. And he was like that with those flowers. I've grown up in a house where people send flowers the way other people breathe, and I'm telling you, my mother would have kept that. My *mother*. Who once sent a bouquet back to an ambassador with a note."

"What was in it?" said Sara, before she could stop herself.

"Celandines," said Tor.

They all looked at him. He was still looking at the fire, and his face was quite red, but he'd said it the way he said things about squares, as if the words had come up on their own out of the part of him that couldn't help it.

"Celandines, mostly. And blackthorn, because it was out. And I'd found some primroses down by the ditch at the end of the long meadow, early ones, so I put those in the middle. And ivy, to hold it. You have to cut the stems on a slant and stand them in water all night or they—" He stopped. "It wasn't very much," he said.

"It was beautiful," said Rolph. "It was the most beautiful thing in that school, and everybody who saw it knew it. He could set up a stall in the flower market at home and be turning ladies away by the end of the week. If he'd given it to *me* I'd have gone to the dance with him."

Trice laughed, but not much.

Rolph didn't laugh at all. He lifted the crock and drank, and went on drinking, a long time, much longer than he needed to, and then lowered it and stood holding it against his chest and looked out past the end of the hearth at the shutters, at the dark, where the sea came in and stood up and fell over. He didn't say anything. The fire talked to itself. A log settled. Sara counted, without meaning to, the way she counted everything: twenty breaths, thirty, forty. She'd never known Rolph be quiet for forty breaths in his life except asleep.

"So he went up to her," said Rolph, to the dark. "At lunch. In the cafeteria. With the flowers."

His voice had gone careful.

"And he said it perfectly. Every word. I was three benches off and I heard it. Would she consider, if she wasn't already promised, and if it wouldn't be any trouble, coming to the dance with him? That's all. Nothing presumed. Nothing she could take wrong. A hundred ways out, and every one of them polite. She could have been promised. She could have been unwell that night. She could have had a friend. She could have taken the flowers and said how lovely and *perhaps*, and let it go cold, and in a week nobody'd have remembered. Or—"

He took a breath. Sara saw his free hand close.

"Or she could just have said no." It came out hard, two years hard; she'd have sworn it had been waiting in him the whole time, under the grin, with its boots on. "She could have looked him in the eye and said, no, thank you, and handed him back his flowers. That would have been *rude*. That would have been rude and I'd have been angry with her for a month. That would have been *fine*."

Sara winced. She felt it happen, in her face, and couldn't stop it.

Beside her, Trice put out a hand and laid it on Tor's arm.

Tor jumped. Not much; but he jumped, the way a horse goes sideways from a paper in the road, and his arm came away from her hand, and then at once he was sorry: "Sorry. Sorry, Trice, you just— I didn't see you— sorry," and he put the arm back, awkwardly, where it had been, as if to give it back to her. Trice didn't touch it again. She folded her hands in her lap.

Sara kept her eyes on Rolph.

She did it on purpose. It was the only thing she could think of to give him. Tor was sitting a foot from her, red to the ears, turning a biscuit over and over, and the kindest thing in the world, she was quite sure, was for nobody to look at him at all; so she didn't. She looked at Rolph, hard, and felt the place where she wasn't looking at Tor like a draught on the side of her face.

"What did she do?" said Sara.

* * *

"She screamed at him," said Rolph.

He said it flatly. He seemed to have decided to get it over with.

"Right there. In front of every bench in the hall. She stood up and she screamed at him. That he was poor. That he was ugly. That he was a dirty little nobody from a mud village and he'd probably got something catching, and how *dare* he, how dare he come near her, who did he think he was, did he not know his place? On and on. Five minutes. Six. I've never heard anything like it before or since, and I've sat through my father's council. She went on until her voice cracked, and then she went on through the crack. Two of the fourth-year girls had to take her by the arms in the end and walk her out of the hall, and she was still going at the door." He looked down at the crock. "And he just stood there. With the flowers. Until it stopped."

There was a silence.

"That's—" Trice began.

"That was bad," said Rolph. "That's not the part I mind most."

Tor looked up.

It was the first time he'd looked at anyone since Rolph had stood up. He looked at Rolph across the fire with a puzzled, startled face, the face of a man who has been told that the stair he's standing on has another step below it, and Sara saw that he truly hadn't known; that whatever was coming, it had happened somewhere he'd never been.

"There was more?" said Tor.

"There was more." Rolph's jaw moved. "She didn't stop. After. She went round that school for weeks with a story about you. A lot of stories. You'd done this. You'd said that. You'd been seen somewhere. Anything that went wrong in that school from the spring dance to the summer exams, somehow you were at the bottom of it. And you didn't know, because you didn't leave the room."

"I left the room."

"You didn't leave the room for nineteen days, Tor. I counted. I brought bread up and left it on the desk and took it away again in the evening and brought more." Rolph's voice had dropped; he was speaking straight to Tor now, as if the girls weren't there. "The dean came up twice. They were going to send you home. Did you know that? They had the letter written to your mother. Unfit for the term. And I— I wrote to mine."

Tor stared at him.

"Express," said Rolph. "That night. And she wrote to the headmaster by return of post, and you weren't sent home, and nobody ever said another word about it." He shrugged, too big a shrug, a merchant's son's shrug. "She'd never met you. She did it because I said so. That's all. That's all it took." He looked away. "They were frightened, the masters. Of what you'd do if you heard what was being said. They had a man on the stair at night for a while, did you know that? No. Of course you didn't. I'm not going to tell you what she said. Not any of it. Not even now."

Tor didn't say anything. He was holding the biscuit very still in both hands, and his face had gone, Sara thought, the way it went when a build he'd been sure of did something he didn't understand: not hurt, not yet. Only taken apart, and trying to see how it went together.

"I remember people looking," said Tor slowly. "After. In the halls. I thought it was just— because of the cafeteria. Because they'd seen."

"Some of it was."

"I didn't know there was *more*."

"I know you didn't. That was the point of me." Rolph turned the crock in his hands. Then, more lightly, because he was Rolph and couldn't keep it up any longer than that: "And you can stop thinking Kolb's got you by mistake."

"I don't think—" Tor stopped. "Kolb?"

"Every first-year gets two afternoons a week in the yard with an under-instructor and a wooden sword, and nobody minds. *You* got Kolb. Every afternoon. From the week you came out of that room." Rolph pointed the crock at him. "You thought it was a muddle in the roll. You told me so. You thought one day somebody'd look at a list and say, good gods, what's the baker's boy doing in with the head instructor, and send you back."

"It *was* a muddle. It must have been. I'm useless at—"

"Kolb came to the room door himself, Tor. While you were still in it. And asked me if you could run. Nobody sent him. I asked, after, everybody I could think of. Nobody'd said a word to him." Rolph shook his head. "He picked you. Himself. He looked at what happened in that hall and he decided nobody was ever going to stand over you like that again without you being able to *get away*. Why do you think it's always running with him? Running and dodging and going left? That's why you'll never be let off. Not now, not ever. You're his. I think he takes it personally." He considered Tor's size, frankly. "And you aren't exactly a big job."

Tor opened his mouth, and shut it again, and looked at the fire with an expression Sara couldn't price at all.

"What happened to her?" said Trice.

"Gone, by the summer. Her family sent for her, all in a hurry, a week before exams. I never knew why." Rolph sat down on the edge of the hearth, heavily, as if his legs had finished before he had. "I thought somebody'd told them. I thought they'd heard what their daughter had been up to and packed her off somewhere quiet, in disgrace. To another school. Or married off to Howard Turnbull."

Tor's head came up.

"Oh!" he said. "I know who he is now."

It was so pleased, and so entirely sincere, that Sara laughed out loud before she could stop it, and so did Trice, helplessly, into her hands; and Rolph looked at Tor for a moment with something in his face Sara had no word for, and then laughed too, and the room let out a breath it had been holding.

* * *

"Well," said Trice, when she'd stopped. She'd sat up very straight again, and her voice had gone back to its usual edges. "That explains the dinner, at any rate."

"The dinner?" said Sara.

"He went the color of candle wax. He didn't eat. He couldn't go under; I watched him try." Trice looked at Tor. "I thought it was Ward. Or the brooch. Or my uncle bowing to you. I'd no idea." She shook her head. "And she just *walked in*. On a count's arm. And smiled at you. I can't believe she dared show her face. Can you believe it, Sara?"

"I can't believe anything yet," said Sara. "I don't know who she is. Maria who? Should I know her? Is she somebody at the palace? A lady's maid, or—"

"She's a countess," said Trice.

Sara looked at her.

"She married Count Ward." Trice said it precisely, each word set down by itself on the rug. "She's Maria Ward. She sat at the end of my uncle's table in green, and she wouldn't look at Ursula, and she did it beautifully. I admired it. I sat there and *admired* her." Her mouth went thin. "Vile, vile woman. Ursula's worth ten of her. Ursula drank second, at that table, with her chin up, with everybody watching. Maria Ward hasn't got the spine for that in her whole body. She's got *nerve*. That's different." She turned to Rolph. "She'd never have done it, you know. In the cafeteria. If she'd had the faintest idea who he was going to be."

"Nobody did," said Rolph.

"That's what the rule's *for*. That's the whole reason. You never know who anyone's going to be." Trice turned back to Tor and looked at him, and Sara saw her doing a sum, fast, and not liking the total. "Tor. What if you took it into your head to make war on her family? Over it? Or on Ward's?"

Tor blinked at her.

Then he laughed.

It wasn't the kind laugh from before, the one that had made Sara want to shake him. It was a real one, surprised out of him; and before he'd got to the end of it Trice had leaned across, quite suddenly, and put both arms round him from the side, stiffly, the way you'd hug a stranger's child who'd fallen over at a fair, with her chin bumping his shoulder. Tor stopped laughing and sat very still inside it, looking puzzled and pink and, Sara noticed, not at all as if he wanted to be let go.

"Trice," he said, muffled. "I'm not going to make war on anybody."

"You might."

"*With what?*" He freed an arm, carefully, so as not to dislodge her, and counted on his fingers. "I've got a hundred and twelve copper squares. Some boards. Davie. Davie'd come. Davie'd come, but he'd want to know what time we'd be back for supper." He put the hand down. "Count Ward's got an *army*. A real one, with horses. I could probably trip him up, if he wasn't looking. If I got behind him. Then two of his men would come and sit on me, and the rest would stand about and have a picnic while they waited for them to finish. They could have races. An egg and spoon." He shook his head. "It's not a war if it's one person and a picnic."

Sara laughed again; she couldn't help it.

Rolph didn't.

"My mother and father aren't laughing," he said.

Tor looked at him over Trice's arm.

"They talked about it," said Rolph. "The night of the dinner. After. I heard them; they didn't trouble to close the door. Whether you'd go after Ward. On your own. Over her." He held up a hand before Tor could speak. "I know. I *know*. But you didn't see what they saw."

"He'd sit on me," said Tor. "Count Ward. He'd just sit on me."

"He tried," said Rolph. "For about a quarter of a bell."

* * *

He told them about the dinner.

He told it differently from the rest. Sara noticed that at once, the way she'd have noticed a man change currencies halfway through a sale. The first story had been his; he'd told it like a man who had been there, three benches off, with every word cut into him. This one he told carefully, a piece at a time, and twice he stopped, and looked at Trice, and Trice nodded, and he went on.

"I don't remember most of it," he said, when Sara looked at him. "Not properly. It's like that, the rage. You're not driving. Somebody else has got the reins and you're in the back with a sack over your head." He rubbed his forehead with two fingers, as if the axe were still in it. "I've had it off Trice. And Mother. And one of the guard, who was very frank, the next morning, and I gave him a gold piece to stop." He took a breath. "Sara. He stood between us."

Sara looked at Tor. Tor was looking at the honey jar.

"Ward was hitting him from behind. Ward. Who once put a man through a stable wall at a horse fair, for a bet. Hitting him as hard as he could, with that muddle of his coming off him so thick the guards couldn't think. And I was coming at Ward from the front, and every time I tried to go round, *he* stepped into my way. Left. Right. Kolb's footwork. Taking it. Everything Ward meant for me, he took. We were both pouring it out, both of us, the whole time, field and fists and all of it, and it went into him like rain into a field." Rolph spread his big hands, helplessly. "Trice says it was like trying to get at someone round a mountain. It was the shield, of course. The fourth one. But nobody else in the world *has* one. Nobody else in the world could have stood there. And you can't take it off him, because he'd only make another."

"The floor cracked," said Trice quietly. "All the way across. Like a leaf."

"And in the *middle* of it," said Rolph, "with Ward pounding on him like a door, he starts asking the room, very politely, whether anybody knows an unmarried nobleman. For Ursula. Three steps. *Somebody suitable.*" He laughed, a short helpless sound. "In Father's falcon. In black. Standing in the middle of the king's dining room with the king's own bird on his chest, calm as anything, telling two counts and a prince to stop it, because there was a girl in the room who needed seeing to and *we were in the way*. Trice says Thorgood looked at him as if Father had walked out from behind the guard and told us all to heel. Ward too. Did you see Ward, after? Like a dog that's been shouted at by somebody it didn't know was the master."

"I saw," said Trice.

"And then he walked off. Not out of breath. Not a word to me about it, not then, not since. Not a word to *Ward*. He'd every right in the world to have us both called to account, and he went and asked who Howard Turnbull was." Rolph looked at Tor at last, straight across the fire. "That's what they saw, Tor. All down that table. That's what Mother and Father saw."

Tor snorted.

It was a small, ordinary, disbelieving snort, the sort he gave Rolph across their room at school when Rolph said something especially silly, and it was so much the wrong size for the room that Sara felt it like a missed stair.

"Count Ward didn't even know I was there," said Tor. "He was hitting the shield. That's all. It could have been a pell." He turned the biscuit over again. "And Maria didn't know who I was. She looked straight at me and smiled like she'd never seen me in her life. Or she knew, and didn't care. Which—" He shrugged. "I can see. I mean, it's not as if I'm *anybody*."

"Tor," said Sara.

He smiled at her. The kind smile again. Sara looked at Trice, and Trice looked back, and neither of them said anything, because they had said it already, an hour ago, with the biscuits, and it had gone off him like Ward's fists off the egg.

Rolph got up off the hearth. He came back round the fire, stepping over the honey jar again, and sat down on the rug where he'd been before; only it wasn't quite where he'd been before. It was a little to one side. It was beside Sara. He sat down heavily, and brushed at the rug as if there were sand on it, though there wasn't, and leaned back on his hands; and his shoulder, when he'd settled, was against hers.

Sara didn't move.

She knew exactly what it was worth. She'd always known. She'd done the sum two summers ago, in her head, on the steps of her mother's house, and got the answer she'd known she would get, and put it away somewhere and not taken it out since: a merchant's second daughter, and a prince, and three steps between, and the steps all going the wrong way. She knew what it was worth. She didn't move anyway. The fire was warm on her face and his shoulder was warm on hers, and for the length of this one evening, with the shutters open and nobody's mother within a hundred miles, she found she didn't want to price it at all.

Across the rug, Tor was watching them.

He was smiling. It was a gentle, pleased smile, the smile of somebody looking at a lit window from the street on a cold night, and glad there were people inside, and quite sure the door wasn't for him. Sara saw it and wanted to put her hand over his face.

Then he looked at Rolph. He looked for a long time.

"You wrote to your mother," he said.

"Mm."

"The queen. You wrote to the *queen*. About me. In our first year." He frowned at it, working it out, the way he worked out a field. "When I thought you were a merchant's son."

"I was a merchant's son," said Rolph. "Allegedly."

Tor nodded slowly. "That's— I thought it was nice of you. Then. Bringing bread. I thought you were very nice." He looked at the fire. "I didn't know it was the *queen*."

"She was only my mother, then," said Rolph, "as far as you knew."

"I know," said Tor. "That's what I mean."

Rolph didn't say anything to that. Sara felt him not say it, through her shoulder.

* * *

"Can I ask," said Tor, after a while, in a careful, changing-the-subject voice. "Who's Doretta?"

They all looked at him.

"Only I keep hearing it," he said apologetically. "Rolph said it at the dinner. And then tonight. And Trice said Count Wylde, and wouldn't tell me, and— everyone keeps going quiet. I don't know anybody called Doretta. I've never met anyone called that in my life."

"No," said Trice. "You wouldn't have." She looked at him, and something went out of her face, some guard, and she said, more gently than Sara had heard her say anything in a long time: "It's not a story they'd tell you in Two Rivers, I suppose. It's for us. Nobody names a daughter Doretta now. Nobody *would*." She hesitated. "I said not tonight."

"You did," said Tor.

"Well." She drew her boots up under her again, and folded her hands, and Sara, who had sat beside her in a hundred lectures, saw her put the facts in order, from the beginning, with the sums at the bottom. "It's tonight. I'll do the short one. The rest of us have all had it a thousand times."

She looked at the fire. When she began, it was in a voice Sara knew from the nursery at Debri house: not Trice's at all, but every nurse's, the rise and fall that came down through children's rooms from one generation to the next like a good table.

"Once," said Trice, "there was a shopkeeper. A young man, in a big city by a river, with a shop on a good street. He sold—" She stopped. "It depends who's telling it. Mine sold ribbons. He worked very hard, and he was clever, and fair, and his shop did well. And there was a young woman who came into the shop most weeks, for ribbons, and they used to talk. Friendly. Over the counter. About the weather, and the river, and the price of things."

"Was she Doretta?" said Tor.

"Hush. Yes. And one spring morning he made up his mind, and when she came out of the shop with her parcel he followed her into the street and asked her to marry him. In front of the shop. In front of all his neighbors."

Tor winced, very slightly, and Sara saw Trice see it, and go on.

"Now Doretta was noble. Not very. A knight's daughter, or a squire's; it depends who's telling it. And she said no. She said it quite kindly, in my nurse's version. She was even sorry. But she said it out loud, in the street, where the neighbors could hear, and she told him he must have known it was impossible; that a man who sold ribbons couldn't look so high, and ought to have known better than to try." Trice paused. "That was all. It's never more than that, in any version. One no, in the street."

The fire cracked. Out in the dark the sea came in.

"So the shopkeeper sold his shop," said Trice. "He couldn't stay on the street with everyone knowing. And he went for a soldier, because he meant to be killed, and wipe the shame out that way, the old way. Only he wasn't killed. He was good at it. He was clever, and he worked very hard, the way he'd worked in the shop, and he went up and up. And one day in a battle he saved the life of the prince himself, and the prince made him his friend—"

She stopped. Her eyes went, quite deliberately, from Tor to Rolph, and back to Tor, and back to Rolph; and Sara felt Rolph's shoulder shake once against hers, and Trice let out a small snort through her nose.

"—which always seemed to me," said Trice, "the least likely part of the whole thing. Until recently." She folded her hands the other way. "And the prince became king, and the shopkeeper became a general, and then a count. Count Wylde. And he never married. And he waited."

"How long?" said Tor.

"Twenty years. More, in some versions. Until he found out where she'd gone. She'd married a baron by then, a long way off, and had children, and grandchildren coming. And Count Wylde took his army there, all of it, and met her husband in the field, and killed him. And then everyone. Everyone in the barony. Her children, and her brothers, and her brothers' children, and the people in the villages, and the mill, and the dogs. And when it was all gone, and she was the only one left, he had her rowed out to a little island off the coast. A rock, with one tree on it. And left her there. With nothing. So that she'd have the time to sit and think, while she starved, about how one word, said in the street, on one spring morning, to a man she thought didn't matter—" Trice looked at Tor. "—had cost all of that."

Nobody said anything.

Tor was sitting with his arms round his knees. He'd forgotten the biscuit; it had gone into crumbs in his fist, and he hadn't noticed. He looked at Trice, and then out of the open shutters at the dark, toward the sea, as if there might be a rock out there with a tree on it, and his face was very troubled.

Rolph reached for the crock and drank, and found it empty, and looked into it with mild surprise, and passed it to Sara anyway. She held it. It seemed rude not to.

"It never happened," said Rolph. His voice had gone low. "It's not a history. Nobody's ever found the barony. It's a nursery story. It's for children, so they'll learn, before they're old enough to cause real trouble, that you don't know who anyone is. Not really. Not who they'll be. So you're polite. You're kind. To everyone, every time, because you can't tell." He leaned his head back against the sofa. "*Don't be a Doretta.* That's what they say. My nurse said it every time I snatched a toy off a page boy. I must have heard it six times a month till I was twelve. Every one of us has."

"Merchant children too," said Sara quietly. "Ours had a different ending. Doretta's father went bankrupt."

"Worse, in some ways," said Rolph.

"Much worse," said Sara.

"And some of us," said Rolph, to the ceiling, "hear it six times a month for twelve years, and still go and stand up in a cafeteria." He closed his eyes. "They know better. That's the thing. They *know*. They've just decided it couldn't possibly be this one."

Tor was quiet for a long moment.

"Rolph said, at the dinner," he said at last, slowly, "that Doretta was kind. In the story."

"She was," said Trice. "By comparison."

"And they think I'm—" Tor stopped. He looked from one to the other of them, and Sara watched him get there, all the way, the whole length of the king's table and every face at it; and watched him, quite visibly, refuse to believe it. "But I'd never," he said. "I'd never put anybody on an island. I wouldn't put a *goat* on an island."

"We know," said Trice. "That's rather the trouble." She reached out, and this time he didn't jump, and she took the ruined biscuit gently out of his fist, and dusted the crumbs off his fingers into the fire. "Nobody else does."
