Nobody said anything for a while after the goat.

The fire had burned down to a bed of red, with one log lying across it gone to silver scale, and every so often a piece of it broke and fell in and sent up a small, soft fountain of sparks. Rolph lay on his back on the rug and watched them go up into the dark under the beams and go out. The crock was empty. The biscuits were gone, apart from the crumbs Trice had brushed into the fire. Out past the open shutters the sea went on doing what it did, which was come in and stand up and fall over, all night, without needing anyone.

It was Trice who moved. She got up off the rug in one piece, the way she did everything, and dusted her hands, and stood over the three of them with her fists on her hips.

"Well," she said. "This evening wants changing."

Rolph knew the voice. He'd known it since she was eleven and used it to announce that nursery tea was over and they'd all be going down to the stables now, whatever Nurse said. He looked at her upside down. She didn't look at him. She looked at Sara.

Sara got up too, more slowly. On the way up her eyes met Trice's, and something went between them, quick as a coin across a counter; and then, and Rolph never afterward knew which of them began it, they were both giggling. Not loudly. The smothered kind, behind a hand, that gets worse the more you try to stop it. Trice caught Sara by the wrist and towed her off down the passage toward the room they shared, and the giggling went with them, rising, and broke out properly just as they reached the door.

At the door Sara looked back.

Rolph saw it because he was watching her. He generally was. She looked back over her shoulder, still laughing, at the room and the fire and the rug, and her eyes went to Tor; and the laugh went out of her face as if somebody had turned down a lamp. For a moment she stood there with her mouth still open on it. Then Trice pulled, and the door shut, and there was a last little burst of giggling behind it, Trice's, alone, and then nothing.

Rolph turned his head on the rug to see what she'd seen.

Tor was sitting where he'd been all evening, with his back against the sofa and his arms round his knees. He was looking at the passage door. His ears had gone red, and then his neck, and the color was still climbing, steadily, like water in a jug. He had the expression of a man who has walked into a room at the wrong moment and is trying, very politely, to back out of it without turning round.

Rolph sighed at the beams. It was a long sigh, and he let all of it out, since it was likely the last entirely honest thing he'd be doing for a while.

"Rolph," said Tor, very quietly.

"Mm."

"Should I go somewhere?"

"What?"

"Only, I could. I don't mind." Tor was speaking to his knees. "I could go to my room. Or for a walk. On the beach. It's a lovely night for it. Or I could go down and see Walt; he said he sleeps light, I expect he's still up—" He stopped. He lowered his voice still further, though there was nobody to hear but the fire. "Is it a— a noble thing? That you all do? On holidays?"

Rolph sat up so fast he coughed.

"No," he said. "*No.* Gods, Tor. Nothing like that."

"Like what?" said Tor, earnestly.

Rolph opened his mouth, and found that he had walked into a room himself, and that Tor was standing in the doorway of it looking anxious and helpful, and that there was no way out except back. He shut his mouth again.

"Like whatever you're thinking," he said. "It isn't that. It's *lists*. It's school. They've got their summer work to go over, and they'll sit up half the night reading notes to each other and laughing at the dull bits. You know what they're like."

That wasn't, strictly, a lie. There would almost certainly be lists.

"All right," said Tor.

He said it in the voice he used for the headmaster: courteous, attentive, and believing, out of pure good manners, rather less than half. Then he looked at the fire, still red, and was quiet.

Rolph lay down again. He gave it a count of twenty, because there was no sense rushing a thing like this. Then he said, to the ceiling, idly, in the voice of a man with nothing much on his mind:

"Here. That thing you said on the beach."

"What thing?"

"It only listens. You said it only listens." He folded his hands on his stomach. "Listens to *what*, though? Your hand? Only yours? Because I'll tell you now, Little Tor, the first week it's up, there'll be a man in Ford who puts his hand on it just to see what happens. There's always one. I'd do it."

"Not a hand," said Tor. "Hands are all the same to it. That's the whole trouble. It's like a door with no lock on; it doesn't know whose hand it's—" He stopped. "A lock."

Rolph said nothing at all.

"No," said Tor. "Not a lock. A lock has a key, and a key's just a shape, and a shape can be copied. Anything can be copied. That's what copying *is*." His head had gone over on one side. "It has to listen to something that can't be— that isn't just a shape. That—"

His eyes went past Rolph, into the fire, and through it, and out the other side, wherever it was they went; and his lips went on moving after his voice had stopped.

Rolph had done it a hundred times. More. To get him to eat, at school, or out of the bath before the water went cold, or to bed before the third bell after midnight: you put a question in front of him like a saucer in front of a cat, and you sat back. It had never once failed. Every time it worked Rolph liked himself a little less, and every time he did it anyway; and he lay now and looked at Tor's small, absent, intent face in the firelight, gone wholly elsewhere, and liked himself rather less than usual.

He got up.

"Come on," he said. "Bed."

Tor came. He came the way he always came in this state, which was obligingly, in whatever direction he was steered, like a boat on a rope. Rolph went into his satchel by the door for a copper square, and a scribing nail, and put them in his hands, and Tor's fingers closed round them without his eyes coming back. Rolph took him by both shoulders and walked him down the passage.

"—listens to a *pattern*," Tor was saying, to the wall. "But then a pattern can be— no. Unless it's a pattern that changes. Unless it's never the same twice, and only the one end knows what it's going to be—"

They passed the girls' door. There was no giggling behind it now. There was no sound at all. Rolph walked by it without looking at it, steering, and opened the door at the end.

Tor's room was the smallest in the house. It was whitewashed, with a narrow bed under a faded blue cover, and a chest, and one small square window that looked straight into the dunes, so that the grass on the top of the nearest one tossed against the stars. Rolph had slept in it himself, the summer he was eight, because it had been the only room in the house where he couldn't hear the sea, and he'd been afraid of the sea that summer. He'd never told anyone that. He sat Tor down on the bed, and put the copper square on his knees, and lit the lamp on the chest from his own, and turned the wick up.

Tor's hand went flat over the copper. His eyes half closed.

Rolph went to the door. He stood in it.

"Tor," he said.

Nothing.

"Tor. The house is on fire."

"Mm," said Tor. "In a minute."

Rolph grinned, in spite of everything. He knew that *in a minute*. It was the one that meant a bell, or two, or the whole night, or on one occasion in second year a day and a half. He stood a moment longer and watched the lamplight on the side of Tor's face, and on the copper, and on the scribing nail beginning, very slowly, to move.

Then he shut the door, softly, and went back down the passage.

The girls were already in the front room. They'd changed. They stood by the cold end of the hearth, out of the last of the firelight, in plain dark clothes with nothing on them that would catch it, no buckle, no brooch, no comb; Trice had her hair bound tight under a dark scarf and her boots in her hand. They'd done it, Rolph reckoned, in about the time it had taken him to walk Tor thirty feet.

"Well?" said Trice.

"In a minute," said Rolph. "He said in a minute."

Trice nodded once. It was the answer she'd wanted, and she put it where she put such things.

Sara didn't nod. She was looking past him down the dark passage.

"Is he all right?" she said.

"He's building."

It wasn't an answer, and she knew it wasn't, and she looked at him a moment longer as if she might say so. Then she looked away, and bent, and pulled her own boots on.

They went out by the terrace, not the front door. Down off the end of it onto the dune path, single file, the sand cold now and squeaking a little underfoot, the grass hissing at their knees. At the top of the last dune Trice stopped, and looked back the way the house faced, toward the gate; and Rolph, looking where she looked, saw the small orange point by Walt's cottage, steady in the dark, go brighter for a breath and then dim again. The pipe. He was still up.

Trice watched it all the way down to the beach. She didn't take her eyes off it once.

Down at the water's edge the tide had gone out a long way, and the wet sand shone under the stars like a slate. The Teeth stood up out of it in their black row. The three of them stood together on the hard sand, and buckled on, and checked the controls at one another's wrists without needing to say so, the way Kolb had taught them; and then they went up, quietly, one, two, three, into the dark, and the beach was empty.

* * *

Tor woke up with his face stuck to a copper square.

He peeled it off. It came away with a small, reluctant sound, and left, he could feel, the edge of itself printed into his cheek. He was lying on top of the blue cover with his boots still on, and the lamp on the chest had burned itself dry and gone out, and the room smelled of hot wick and of salt; and outside the little window the grass on top of the dune was gray, and the sky behind it was gray going pink.

He didn't know what bell it was. He'd lost count of them somewhere. The light said early.

He sat up and looked at the square.

There was a pattern on it, scratched with the nail, and then another pattern on top of that one, and a third, and all three were crossed out with his thumbnail, hard, so that the copper had a raw, bright, worried look, like a slate after a long sum. That was the listening. He hadn't got it. He looked at it for a while and didn't get it now, either.

But down in the corner, small, there was something else. He didn't remember doing it. It was a little curl, a hook with a tail, and he turned the square toward the window and looked at it in the gray light, and then sat quite still on the edge of the bed.

"It doesn't have to drop," he said.

His voice sounded loud in the little room. He lowered it.

"It's already going. It's *going*, it's got somewhere to go. So you don't stop it. You just stop giving it any more." He traced the curl with one finger. "And it goes. It goes all the way to the end. Like the last of the water out of a pump, after you've stopped pumping." He laughed, once, quietly. "Oh. *Oh.* Well, that's one."

He got up, and found he was stiff all down one side, and very hungry, and went out into the passage.

The girls' door was shut. He went past it on his toes, out of habit and good manners, and into the front room; and stopped.

They were all three asleep on the sofas.

They hadn't gone to bed at all. Rolph lay full length on the long sofa nearest the hearth, flat on his back, with his mouth open and one arm trailing on the floor and his boots, which were on, propped up on the far arm. Trice was on the second sofa, curled into a knot in the corner of it with her knees drawn up and one arm over her face, and her feet were bare and gray with sand. Sara was on the third, sitting bolt upright at the end of it with her hands folded in her lap and her head tipped back against the wall, fast asleep, exactly like a lady waiting for a carriage that had been a very long time coming. All three of them were dressed in dark clothes he'd never seen before. There was sand on the cushions. There was sand on the rug, in little drifts and scatters, all the way from the terrace door, and the terrace door was standing open to the morning, and in the gray light coming through it everything had the rumpled, abandoned look of a room where something had gone on very late.

Tor looked at them for a long moment.

He went pink. He could feel it happen. It went up from his collar to his hair and stayed there.

"None of my business," he said, in a whisper, very firmly.

He stood a moment longer. Then he went over to the second sofa, still on his toes, and took the dust sheet that had been folded over the back of it and shaken out and never put back, and laid it over Trice's sandy feet, carefully, so as not to wake her. A board creaked. Rolph snored on. Tor tiptoed to the terrace door and out, and pulled it to behind him, and went down to the sea.

* * *

The beach had changed in the night.

The trenches from yesterday were nearly gone, smoothed over into long shallow dips by the tide. The tide itself had gone out, and out, so far that there was a whole new country of wet sand he hadn't known was there, ribbed and shining and pocked with tiny holes, with weed lying on it in dark ropes. The Teeth, which yesterday had stood in the surf with white water breaking round them, now stood on dry land with their feet in puddles, and he could walk right up to them. He did. They were taller than he'd thought, a good deal taller than him, square and pitted and gray, crusted at the base with small shut shells. One of them had lost its top at some point long ago, and the top lay at its foot in the sand: a block of the same gray stone, about the size of a bread oven, square-cornered, with something rusty and red running through its middle like a bone through a joint.

Tor put his hand on it. It was cold and wet, and very old, and it didn't feel like stone at all, not really; it felt *made*.

"Hm," he said.

He looked back at the house, and the dunes, and the line of weed and wreck high up the beach where the water had come to yesterday; and then down at his feet, and the puddles, and the far, flat, glittering edge where the sea was now.

"It moves," he said. "It goes all that way. And then it comes all that way back." He frowned at it. "And it'll do more than that, some days. There'll be storms. There'll be days it comes right up into the grass, I expect, and days it goes out even further than this, and I won't be here to see." He did the sum. He did it slowly, out loud, the way he'd been taught to with anything that mattered. "A mile out. Then it's always under, whatever the tide does. Always clear of the sand, too. A mile's plenty." He looked at the sea. It looked back at him, enormous and calm. "Two miles," said Tor. "To be safe."

He set the square into a hollow in the top of the block, where the rusty bone came through, and worked it in. It took a while. He had to find the place where the old made stone would take the copper, and then persuade it, and then hold it there while the field set; and at the end the square was part of the block, and the block was part of the square, and you couldn't have said where one stopped. Then he put the trick from the luggage plates on it, and the block went light under his hands, and came up out of the sand with a long sucking sound, and hung there at knee height, waiting, the way the plates did.

He buckled on, and went up, and towed it out to sea.

It was a strange thing to do alone. He hadn't done it before. The beach went away behind him, and then the house, until it was a small gray box with a pinch of chimney smoke at the gate end, which was Walt's; and below him the water went from pale green to dark green to a green that was almost black, and the bottom went away out of sight, and there was nothing under him at all but the long slow heave of it, rising and falling, like something breathing in its sleep. The wind came off it cold. The light came up gold over the land behind him and laid itself on the water in a long road. He looked back once more, and the house was gone into the line of the dunes, and he thought that was probably two miles, and then he went a little further, to be safe, and let the block go.

It dropped. It went into the water with a sound like a door shutting, and a ring went out from it, and was gone, and there was nothing to show where it had been. He hung there over the place for a moment, feeling it go down and down, a long way, until it settled, and he felt it settle. Then he went back.

He put the other square on the beach, wedged in the sand just above the Teeth, and sat down beside it, and closed his eyes.

The water came up out of the sea two miles away. He couldn't see it at first; he felt it. Then he could see it, a line, a bright thread lifting off the swell far out and coming in, low over the water, a foot thick and as clear as air, with the morning sun going through it. It came all the way. It came in over the breakers and up the beach and past him, close enough to touch, cold coming off it like the breath of a cellar; and on along the foot of the dunes in a long, lazy curve, the way he'd told it, and down again past the Teeth, and back into the surf a couple of hundred yards along, where it went in with a soft continual pouring.

Tor stood up and looked at it. Then he put his hand on the square in the sand.

He did it the way Sara had done it. He thought *stop*, and braced, with his shoulders up round his ears, as if something were about to fall on him.

Nothing fell on him.

Far out on the sea, the bright thread stopped lifting. The end of it came up off the water, clean, like the tail of a rope pulled up onto a deck; and the whole line went on. It went on coming in, the last of it, over the breakers and up the beach and past him, and on round its long curve under the dunes, and down past the Teeth; and its tail went by him, a foot from his face, glittering, and on round the curve; and he turned on his heel and watched it all the way, the end of it running after the rest like the last child in a game, until the very last of it poured itself back into the surf two hundred yards down and was gone.

The beach was dry. Not one drop had come down on it.

Tor laughed out loud, alone on the sand, and didn't care who heard.

"*That's* one," he said.

Then he looked down at his own hand, still flat on the square, and the laugh went out of him slowly.

"And anyone could still do that," he said. "Anyone at all."

He stood a long while with his hand there, frowning at it. Then he worked the beach square out of the sand and put it in his pocket, and left the other where it was, two miles out, dark and quiet on the bottom of the sea, because it would keep. It would be there when the king said yes. And if he didn't, well. It wasn't doing any harm.

He walked back up the beach toward the house, muttering. "Listens to something that's never the same twice," he said. "That only the two ends know. Like— like two people who—" He shook his head. "No. Think, Tor. Breakfast first."

* * *

Sara woke up because something smelled wonderful.

She was sitting up. That was the first thing. She was sitting bolt upright on the end of a sofa with her hands in her lap, and her neck had set like glue, and when she moved her head it made a sound. The second thing was the sand. It was in her boots and her stockings and her collar and her ears and, she discovered, in her mouth. The third thing was the smell: something frying, and something sweet and hot, and woodsmoke, coming down the passage from the kitchen.

The fourth thing was the door.

It came back to her all at once, the way such things do, before she'd even properly sat up: the passage door last night, and herself in it, laughing, and Tor's face. That red, careful, polite face, looking at her across the rug as if he'd walked in on something, and wanted to go. She'd seen it, and she'd gone on through the door anyway, because Trice was pulling and because there was a job to do, and she had not once, the whole long night, quite managed to stop seeing it.

Sara sat very still for a moment. Then she got up, and stepped over Rolph's arm, and went to wash.

The bathroom at the summer house had a pump, and a tin bath, and a jug and basin, and a square of old mirror gone brown at the edges, and the water from the pump was so cold it made her gasp. She used it anyway. She washed her face and her neck and her arms and got the sand out of most of the places it had got into; she took the dark clothes off and folded them small and put on her own green; she combed her hair till it lay down and pinned it up properly, with every pin. She looked at herself in the brown mirror. She looked like a merchant's daughter who had slept well in a good bed, and she went down to the kitchen.

Tor was at the range.

He had a pan going on the fire and another on the iron top, and the kitchen was full of warmth and the smell of frying and of corn. There were cakes, flat and golden, stacked on a plate by the oven door to keep warm, and one of the jars of jam from the pantry standing open beside them; there was something pink and salty hissing in the pan that she worked out after a moment had come out of one of the dented tins; and there were eggs, a whole bowl of them, brown, with the straw still on. The fire had been laid properly this time, small sticks and then large, and it was burning clear and hot with no smoke at all.

"Good morning," said Tor, to the pan.

"Good morning." She stood in the doorway. "That smells lovely."

"There's no milk," said Tor. "Rolph drank it. All of it. In the story." He turned the meat over with a fork. "So it's corn cakes, not proper ones. They don't rise so well with water. I'm sorry."

"They look perfect."

"They're all right," said Tor. He still hadn't looked at her. He looked at the pan, and at the eggs, and at the oven door, and at the window over the sink, which had the sea in it, and anywhere at all that wasn't the place she was standing; and his ears, she saw, were faintly pink.

Sara came into the kitchen. She took the plates down off the rack without being asked, and set them out on the table, four of them, and the forks, and found the honey jar from last night and put that out too, because it went with corn; she took the cakes off the oven door when he said they were ready and carried them to the table and said they were beautiful, which they were; and she asked him, while the eggs went in, how he'd slept, and where he'd been, because his boots were wet and there was sand on his knees.

"The beach," said Tor. "I was trying something." He broke another egg, one-handed, neatly. "It worked."

"What did?"

He told her. He told it to the eggs at first, and then, as it went on, and he got to the part about the block going light and the water going black and the house going small, to the window; and by the time he got to the tail of the line running past him on the beach, a foot from his face, he'd forgotten to be pink, and turned round with the egg slice in his hand to show her the curve with it in the air. Sara sat at the table with her chin on her hand and listened, and watched his face come back, and didn't price any of it. She could have. It was all there to be priced, the sea and the stone and two miles and the whole of Ford. She left it.

"And then I put my hand on it," said Tor. "Like you did. And nothing fell."

"Nothing at all?"

"Not a drop. It all just went home." He turned back to the pan. "But I could still stop it. Anyone could. So that's only half."

"It's a very good half," said Sara.

Trice came in.

She came in the way the tide had gone out: a long way, slowly, without hurry. Her hair, unbound, stood out round her head like a hedge that had been through a storm; there was a red crease down one cheek from the sofa arm and sand on the other; and she had the dust sheet from the sofa round her shoulders like a shawl, and looked at it, when she sat down, as if she'd no idea where it had come from. She didn't say good morning. She sat down at the table and put her elbows on it and looked at the corn cakes with the fixed, grave attention of somebody reading a contract, and then took two, and ate them, and then a third, and then said, "Is there jam," in a voice like a gate on a rusty hinge.

Sara passed her the jam.

Rolph came last.

They heard him before they saw him: a bump, and a word, and a long scrape, which was his boots coming off the sofa arm. Then he came down the passage and into the kitchen in a rush, and he was enormously cheerful. He was more cheerful than any person had any need to be at that hour, in that state. His hair was flat on one side and his shirt was out on the other and there was sand, Sara saw, actually in his ear, a little ridge of it along the edge.

"*Morning!*" said Rolph. "Gorgeous morning. Isn't it? Slept like a log. Like a whole forest of logs. What's this? What *are* these? Little Tor, you are a marvel. I've always said so. Haven't I always said so?"

"There's no milk," said Tor.

"Isn't there?"

"You drank it."

"Ah," said Rolph, and sat down, and looked at the plate Tor put in front of him, and then, quickly, at Tor, and then at Sara, and then at the plate again.

He did that all through breakfast. Sara watched him do it. Every few mouthfuls his eyes would go to Tor, sideways, the way a man glances at the sky when he's left his washing out, and then away again; and every time, Tor was eating his own breakfast with his eyes on his own plate, or getting up to bring more eggs, or passing the honey to someone a moment before they'd have asked for it. He was perfectly courteous. He was more courteous than he'd been since the day he arrived at the palace. He passed things and fetched things and said *please* and *thank you* and *would anyone like* and he didn't ask a thing. He didn't ask where they'd been. He didn't ask why they were sandy, or why the sofas, or what the dark clothes were, folded small on the end of Sara's bench where she'd put them without thinking. He didn't look at any of them for longer than it took to pass a plate.

Sara had seen that before. She'd seen it in the Debri counting room, in the face of a good clerk, when a customer comes in and pays with the wrong sort of money and says nothing about it: the polite, deliberate not-seeing of a person who has decided that what he doesn't see can't be his business. Tor had decided. She could see him holding the decision in both hands, like a full cup, carefully, so as not to spill.

And she'd done that. Not the night, not the homework; that had been coming whatever she did, and she'd do it again. But the door. The giggling at the door, and not stopping.

"Tor," she said.

"Mm?" He was buttering a cake. There was no butter, really; he was spreading honey with great care right to the edges.

"About last night."

"Oh, it's quite all right." It came out fast, and kind, and he went red to the hair. "Truly. It's none of my business. I expect it's a— a sort of noble thing. That people do. I don't mind a bit."

Trice made a sound into her jam that was not quite a cough.

"It's *not*—" said Rolph.

Trice kicked him under the table. Sara heard it.

Sara folded her hands on the table. She couldn't tell him. That was the plain fact of it. She couldn't tell him what it had been, and if she tried to tell him what it hadn't been, it would only be worse; she'd seen Rolph try it, last night, from three doors down the passage, and it had gone about as well as Rolph usually went. So she didn't try. She told him the other thing instead, the one she could.

"I was rude," she said. "Going off like that. Laughing. After you'd— after the story. After all of it." She made herself look at him while she said it, and not at the honey. "I saw your face at the door, and I went anyway. I'm sorry."

Tor looked up.

It was the first time all morning he'd looked straight at any of them, and he looked at her with real surprise, as if she'd handed him something he hadn't ordered.

"Oh," he said. "No. You weren't." And then, considering it, gently: "You were only laughing. It's all right. I like it when you laugh."

He smiled at her, and went back to his honey; and Sara sat with her hands folded and felt something in her chest that she didn't have a column for, and didn't look for one.

* * *

They flew home along the edge of Ford.

It was worse from the air in the morning than it had been coming the other way. The light was lower and showed everything. The fields lay brown and pale in their squares, and the hedges between them had gone the dull gray-green of things that have stopped trying, and the roads were white, and every so often there was a pond that was a cracked brown plate with a ring of white round it where the water had been. Tor flew in front, as he had on the way out. He didn't mutter this time. He looked down.

Then the country began to change under them, slowly, the way a face changes when the light moves. A river came in from the north, wide and slow, and the land along it went green: real green, a long ribbon of it on either bank, fields and orchards and the dark round heads of trees. Sara had been flying long enough now to know when somebody was hungry, and Rolph had been hungry since the second hour, and said so, at intervals, to the wind.

They came down in a copse of alders by the river, a mile above a village, where nobody could see them land; Kolb had been very plain about that, and so had Tovey, who'd been taken for an Austran in four towns on the way to the capital and had not enjoyed it. They unbuckled among the alder roots and the cow parsley, and stowed the belts in Rolph's satchel, and walked down along the towpath into the village like four people who had walked a long way, which in a sense they had.

It was a small place. A street, a green, a stone bridge, a church with a squat tower and a bell that rang a quarter while they were crossing the bridge, and the inn on the corner by the water, long and low, with a plough painted on its sign and geraniums dying in the window boxes. Inside it was dark and cool and smelled of beer and floor polish. The woman behind the counter had forearms like a baker and a face like a closed shop.

"Something to take with us, please," said Tor. "If it's not any trouble. Bread, and cheese, maybe, for four. And whatever's quick."

She looked at the four of them. She looked longest at Rolph, who filled her doorway, and then she went into the back and came out with a loaf, and a wheel-end of yellow cheese, and four cold pasties, and four apples, and began wrapping them in a cloth.

"Six silver," she said.

Tor's hand stopped on its way to his pocket.

Sara saw it. She'd done the sum herself before the woman had finished saying it; she couldn't help it, she'd been doing that sum since she was six. Bread and cheese and four pasties and four apples, in a river village in summer, came to perhaps a silver and a half. Two, in a bad year, from a hard woman. Six was not a price. Six was a fact about something else.

"I'm sorry," said Tor, in his politest voice. "Is that— I'm sorry, I don't think I quite— is that for the week?"

The woman finished tying the cloth. Then she put both hands flat on the counter and looked at him, and Sara saw her decide he wasn't mocking her.

"You've come from the west," she said. "Haven't you. I can tell by your boots."

"From the coast."

"Then you've seen it. Over there." She jerked her head west, toward Ford. "Brown, isn't it. All of it. Well, it'll be brown here too, come the end of the month. Look out there." She nodded at the window, at the fields across the river, green to the hills. "You'd think that was green. Wouldn't you."

They looked. It was green.

"It's not," said the woman. "Not the right one. Look at the wheat. That's a pale sort of green, that is, that's thin. That's wheat that's stopped. It's standing there green because it hasn't the strength to go yellow yet, and it never will now. I've seen it twice before. I know what it looks like." She tapped the counter. "Two months from now there won't be a full sack of flour in this village. And when there isn't, the merchant houses'll bring it down the river from the east, in their barges, the Debris and the Fennimores and the rest of them, and they'll bring all you like. At their price. And I'll have to pay it, or shut. So I'm taking what I can, now. While there's anyone coming through to take it from." She looked at Tor, not unkindly. "Six silver. I'm sorry for it. I'd not have charged it you last year."

Sara didn't say anything.

She looked at the cloth bundle on the counter. Her mother would have priced this summer in the spring. Sara knew it the way she knew her own handwriting: somewhere in the Debri counting house there was a sheet of figures, neat, in her mother's clerk's hand, with Ford at the top of it, and a column for the barges, and a column for the price, and the sum at the bottom already done. It would be a fair price, by the house's lights. It would be a good deal more than six silver.

Rolph paid.

He got there before Tor, which took doing; he reached past Tor's shoulder and laid the coins on the counter, six silver, counted out one at a time without any haggling at all. Then he didn't pick up the bundle. He stood there with his big hand flat beside the coins and his face very still, and listened, as if there might be more; and Sara, who had seen that face at his father's table, recognized it with a small shock. It was the king's.

"Tell them in the capital," said the woman, sweeping the coins into her apron. "Not that it'll do much good. The king's never been west of his own river in his life, I'd put money on it. If I had any."

"No," said Rolph. "I'll tell them."

He picked up the bundle. He thanked her, in his ordinary voice, and held the door for the rest of them, and walked them back up the towpath to the alders without saying another word.

* * *

They ate in the air.

It was the only way, if they were going to be at the palace for the third bell after noon, and Rolph said so; and it turned out you could eat a cold pasty at a good speed behind the teardrop shield, if you were careful, and kept your elbows in, and didn't mind the crumbs going backward into Trice. They followed the river. It was the King's river, Rolph said, the one that went past the palace steps and on through half of Noram and out to the sea along Ford's edge; and it wound away ahead of them toward the capital, slow and brown and lower than it should have been, with long pale bars of sand showing in it like the backs of sleeping animals.

Tor had stopped eating.

Sara flew beside him. She'd been watching him since the inn. He'd paid nothing, and said nothing, and climbed back into the air with his bread in his hand and not touched it; and he'd been flying ever since with his eyes on the ground and his face as worried as she had ever seen it, more worried than at the shield test, more than on the beach yesterday when he'd sat down in the sand with his knees gone. He wasn't muttering. Somehow that was worse.

Then she saw his face change.

He was looking down at the river. Then along it, far ahead, to where it went round a bend into the haze. Then down again, closer, at the fields on either side of it; and she looked where he was looking, and saw nothing much. Fields. Hedges. And between the fields, running off the river at a slant, one after another all the way along both banks, little straight lines of water, glinting, like the veins on the back of a leaf: the ditches. Every farm had one. Some had three. They went off the river and in among the fields and branched and branched again, finer and finer, until you couldn't see them for the green.

"Oh," said Tor.

"What?"

"They've *done* it." He turned in the air to look at her, and his bread went, and he didn't notice. "Sara. Look. They've already done it. Every farm. Every single one, all the way down. Somebody's already built it. Hundreds of years of somebody." He pointed down at the ditches, and then along the river, and then away west, toward Ford and the brown. "I don't need the farms. I was going to go to every farm. Every one, with a branch, all over the county, and it'd have taken years, and— I don't need any of it." He laughed, suddenly, the way he had on the beach. "I only need the *rivers*. Just fill the rivers. And the ditches do the rest. They've been waiting to do the rest for— for *ever*."

"Little Tor," said Rolph, from behind. "You dropped your lunch."

"Did I?" said Tor, and looked down, and didn't care, and turned back to the river with his whole face lit.

Sara looked down too. She watched the bread go, turning over and over, a long way, until it went into the King's river with a tiny white splash and the river took it. And then, because she couldn't help it, because she'd been doing it since she was six, she found she'd done another sum. She hadn't meant to. It had done itself, in the time it took the bread to fall: what a sack of the Debri house's flour would be worth in Ford this winter, at her mother's price, if the rivers of Ford were full.

Ahead of them the haze thinned, and the white wall of the capital came up out of it, and the dome behind it catching the sun.

Sara put the number away somewhere, and didn't look at it again.
