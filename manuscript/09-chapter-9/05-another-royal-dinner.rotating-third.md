The ice box had got to his room before he did.

It stood against the wall between the two long windows, where the chest had been that morning, and the chest had gone somewhere else, quietly, the way things went in the palace. It was a good deal bigger than the one on the queen's table. It came up past Tor's hip. It was dark wood, polished until the windows showed in it, with brass at the corners and a lid that lifted on two hinges, and inside, when he hopped over and lifted it, there was a padded lining, quilted in squares like a bedspread, and a great deal of cold, and in the middle of all of it, on a bed of chipped ice, a pitcher of water with a cloth over its mouth.

"Oh, *Connie*," said Tor, to nobody.

He'd asked at lunch. It was barely past the first bell. Somebody had carried this up two flights of stairs in the time it took him to hop back along one corridor with Hale.

He let the lid down gently and stood holding on to it, looking at it. Then he looked round the room for a chair, and found the one with the roses on it, and dragged it across the floor one-handed with a long, terrible scrape that made him wince for whoever was maintaining the floor, and sat down facing the box with his leg out on the footstool and his elbows on his knees.

"Right," he said.

There was copper on the desk. There always seemed to be copper on the desk lately; he'd stopped wondering about it. He fetched an armful in two trips, and a stylus, and the little pot of cheap ink, and a board to lean on, and sat down again, and lifted the lid, and put his hand in among the ice to feel what it was like in there, and took it out again and sat with it tucked into his armpit until it stopped hurting.

The box didn't need much. That was the beautiful part. It already wanted to be cold. All it needed was something to take the warm out of it as fast as the warm crept in, through the wood and the lid and the cracks, and put it somewhere else.

That was where he stopped, the first time, with the stylus in the air.

Because the somewhere else ought to be an oven. That was the whole idea. That was what he'd laughed about at lunch with his hand over his mouth. You put a cold box and a hot box back to back, and the one fed the other, and the kitchens could stop carrying buckets of ice up from the cellars in the morning and carrying ash down to the yard at night. He looked round the room, as if there might be an oven in it somewhere. There was a painted sky on the ceiling with birds in it, and a bed the size of a hay cart, and the chair with the roses, and a vase. There was nothing that wanted to be hot.

"Well," said Tor, a little sadly. "Next time."

He sent it down instead. Down through the floor and the floor under that, and the cellars, and the old stone the palace was sitting on, into the ground, which was very large and had been taking heat from things for a long time and wouldn't mind a little more. It was a long line to draw. He drew it carefully, twice, once on the board and once in his head, and then he drew the rest.

It took him most of two bells. At the end of them there was a template on the board, still wet, and ten squares of copper in a row along the floor beside his chair, each one marked the same, each one a little warm from his hands. He looked at them. He counted them twice, from habit. Then he picked up the first one and leaned over and put it in the box, flat, on the bottom, under the ice, and touched it, and woke it.

Nothing happened.

He put his hand into the box. He put it in right up to the wrist, among the ice, beside the pitcher, and held it there.

Nothing. Not cold. Not anything. It felt like putting his hand into a cupboard.

Tor took his hand out, and looked at it, and then at the plate, and then at the template, and felt the bottom go out of his stomach in the old way, the way it had in the copying room in his first year, when the lamp squares wouldn't light and the building master's eyebrows went up.

"It happens," he told himself firmly. "It happens to everybody." It did. He'd heard them, the second-years, in the corridor outside the workrooms, kicking the wall: a whole day's work and the thing just sat there, and nobody could ever say why. It had never happened to him, it was true. But that only meant it had been waiting. "Think, Tor. Look at the line. You've drawn the line backwards, or you've put the—"

He'd been reaching for the template, and his sleeve pulled, and something swung forward out of the neck of the silk tunic and knocked against his chest.

Two cords. The shield on one, from the spares in his trunk, because his own was gone off somewhere round a little girl's neck. And on the other, the amulet. The temperature one. He'd taken it off for the bath, because it made bath water feel like nothing; and afterwards, quite without thinking, he'd put it back on.

He looked down at it for a while.

"Oh," said Tor. "Oh, you *idiot*."

He pulled it off over his head and dropped it in his lap and put his hand straight back into the box, and yelped, and took it out, and shook it, and laughed out loud in the big empty room. It was cold. It was gloriously, painfully cold in there, colder than the ice had been on its own, and getting colder; he could feel it coming up out of the open lid against his face like the breath from a cellar door.

He reached in again, carefully this time, and took hold of the pitcher by the handle, and lifted it out.

The water in it was clear. It was perfectly clear and perfectly still, and he thought, *that's odd, it ought to be frozen*, and then the pitcher tipped a little in his hand and the water moved; and the moment it moved, it went white. It went white from the top down, all at once, in feathers and spikes, the way frost comes across a window, only fast, so fast he could hear it, a tiny crackle like somebody walking on paper; and then there was no water in the pitcher at all, only a solid lump of ice with a cloth frozen to the top of it.

Tor sat with it in his lap and looked at it.

"Well," he said, eventually. "That's that working, then."

He put the pitcher back in the box. He'd have liked to have done it all again, from the start, to see. He sat for a while, instead, with his hands round his knee, looking at the nine plates still on the floor, thinking.

A room was no test. Nobody opened this box but him. What he wanted was somewhere that opened a box fifty times a day, and slammed it, and leaned on it, and put hot things in it that oughtn't to go in, and complained. What he wanted was a kitchen. And he couldn't get to a kitchen. He couldn't get to the end of the corridor without Hale.

"I wish Burks was here," said Tor, aloud.

There was a click.

A piece of the wall beside the fireplace, a long panel with a painted garland on it that he'd been looking at for three days without seeing it, swung neatly outward on hinges he'd have sworn weren't there. Behind it was a dim little passage, plain plaster, with a smell of candle smoke and floor polish; and in the opening, as neat and small and quiet as ever, in his dark coat, with his hands folded, stood Burks.

"Sir?" said Burks.

Tor stared at him. Then at the panel. Then at him.

"Is that a *passage*?" he said. "In the wall? For the servants? Oh, that's clever. That's very clever. So you don't have to go round by the corridors with the—with the trays and everything. Does it go all the way along?"

"A good deal of the way, sir," said Burks.

"That's wonderful." Tor remembered himself. "I'm sorry. I didn't mean to call you. I mean, I did, but I didn't know you'd—were you just passing?"

"I was nearby, sir. Did you need something?"

Tor held up a plate.

He explained. He explained rather a lot, with the plate in one hand and the frozen pitcher in the other, about ovens and ice and chimneys and the ground, and about failing, and the amulet, and about how it was all very well in a room where nothing ever happened, but a kitchen was where you'd find out, and he couldn't get to a kitchen, and would Burks—only if it wasn't any trouble—take one of these down to whoever looked after the ice, and ask them to put it in a box and try it, and say what they thought?

Burks listened to all of it. Then he came across the floor and took the plate, and turned it over once in his fingers, and looked at the line drawn down the back of it for a moment longer than Tor would have expected; and then wrapped it in his handkerchief, as if it were something that might take cold.

"Most certainly, sir," he said.

And he went back the way he had come, into the wall, and the panel closed behind him with a soft click, and the garland was only a garland again.

* * *

Hale hadn't seen anyone go in.

He thought about that, on and off, for most of the afternoon. He'd brought the boy back from lunch himself, and got him into the chair, and the boy had asked for the door to be left open again, so it was left open. Pask had gone off at the second bell and young Cobb had come on, and Cobb was a good lad but he talked, so Hale had sent him to the far end of the corridor to talk to the window. Nobody had come down the corridor all afternoon but a maid with lamp oil and a footman with a tray, and Hale had looked at both of them, and both of them had stayed outside.

But some time after the third bell, he'd heard the boy talking. That was nothing. The boy talked to himself; Hale had heard him do it for three days, at all hours, plans and sums and *think, Tor*. Only this time somebody had answered him. A quiet voice, a man's voice, not loud enough to make out. Hale had put his head round the door, and there was the boy in the chair with the roses on it, in his silk, holding a pitcher of ice and looking pleased with himself, and there was nobody else in the room at all.

Hale had looked at the garland on the wall beside the fireplace for a while. Then he'd gone back to his post.

Old palace. Everybody knew it had its ways. Nobody who'd been on the doors as long as Hale had went poking at them.

After that the boy didn't talk to anyone but the floor.

He got down onto it. Hale didn't see how he managed, with the leg; he heard a thump and a hiss and went in fast, and found him sitting on the carpet with his back against the bed and the leg straight out in front of him, quite happy, with paper all round him in a ring. He'd been at the desk for more of it. There was paper on the bed. There was paper on the chair with the roses. He was drawing on one sheet with a board under it, and then pulling another out from under his knee and drawing on that, and muttering.

"—not *up*," he was saying. "That's the trouble. That's a rig. A rig wants up. It's a person standing on air. A cart doesn't want to stand, it wants to *sit*, it wants to sit on something that's always there, the way a boat sits on—" He stopped. He looked at the ceiling, at the painted birds. "Less like a rig," he said. "More like a boat. More like a—"

Hale went back out.

The fourth bell went. The fifth. The boy didn't hear either of them; Hale could tell by the muttering, which didn't stop. At the sixth Hale went in with the lamp, because the light was going yellow and the boy was drawing with his nose almost on the paper. He lit it and set it on the floor within reach, and the boy said "Oh, thank you," without looking up, and then, a moment later, still without looking up, "Sorry. Thank you, Hale."

Coming back out, Hale started to wonder about the dinner.

He'd heard about the dinner. Everybody below stairs had heard about the dinner. The long room had been open since noon with the leaves in the table, and there'd been carts at the kitchen door all afternoon, and a man from the city come up with three kinds of flowers. Half the court was going to be in it. Hale had been told his part: the boy was to be ready, and walked down, by the ninth bell. Nobody had said what the boy was to wear. Hale had looked at the borrowed silk, rucked up to the knee over the splint and now with ink on it, and had decided it was above his rank.

At the seventh, he decided it wasn't. He was gathering himself to go in and say something when he heard a step coming down the corridor from the stairs, a quiet, even, unhurried step, and turned, and straightened all the way up from his boots to the top of his head before he'd even properly seen who it was.

Count Lairdgren came down the corridor with his hands empty and his coat buttoned, exactly as he always did, as if he'd been walking down it for a hundred years.

Hale had seen him perhaps a dozen times in thirty years. He'd never seen him look a day different. A small man; you forgot that, until he was in front of you, and then you couldn't think why you'd forgotten. There was a fleck of something white on his left cuff. Flour, Hale thought. He'd been in the kitchens.

He hadn't come down this corridor. Not this afternoon. Hale would have sworn to it.

"Sergeant," said the count, pleasantly, passing.

Hale wasn't a sergeant. He didn't say so. He said "My lord," to the air above the count's head, and the count, without stopping, without even turning, lifted one finger a very little. Hale shut his mouth on the rest of it.

The count went in.

"Burks!" Hale heard. "Oh, it's you. You've been ages. I'm sorry. Did they hate it?"

"They've installed it, sir," said the count, in a voice Hale had never heard him use: mild, deferent, the voice of a man who carried things. "It took some little while. Cook didn't care to have it lying loose. She had the carpenter in to screw it to the inside of the big larder box, the one for the fish."

"Oh!" said the boy. "Oh, that's a good idea. That's better than mine. Does it work? Is it cold?"

"Most effective, sir. Cook has already filled it."

"With what?"

"The butter, sir. And the cream for tonight. And, I believe, her own supper, with her name on it."

The boy laughed, delighted. Hale, standing at his post, looked at the far wall, and kept his face where it was.

Then there was a silence. Hale knew the kind. He'd heard it on a hundred doors: the silence of somebody looking up for the first time in hours and seeing that the windows have gone dark.

"Burks," said the boy, in a different voice altogether. "What bell is it?"

"Gone the seventh, sir."

"Oh, *no*." A great rustle of paper. "Oh, no, no. Dinner's at nine. Connie said nine. And I haven't—I can't go in *this*, I've had it on since the bath, there's ink on it, and everything else I've got's been in a—it's in the—I haven't got anything. I haven't got *anything*." A pause. "I'll have to say I'm ill."

"There's no need for that, sir," said the count. "If you'll allow me a moment."

He came back out. He nodded to Hale, a very small nod, as between two men with a job of work in front of them, and went off up the corridor the way he'd come, at exactly the same pace, and turned the corner; and Hale, after a moment's thought, went in and began, quietly, to pick up paper.

The count was back before he'd finished. He had clothes over his arm: dark trousers, a shirt with a soft collar, a coat of deep blue cloth with a nap on it like a mole's back, plainer than anything Hale had seen going into the long room all afternoon and better, in the way that some plain things are better. Hale had no notion where he'd got them. He didn't ask himself.

They got the boy off the floor between them, which was easy; there was nothing of him. The shirt went on. The coat went on, and fitted, more or less, across the shoulders, which surprised Hale and didn't seem to surprise the count. On its breast, over the heart, was the king's falcon, silver on purple, small as a child's palm. Hale saw it and kept his face where it was. The boy didn't look down. Then they came to the trousers, and stopped.

The trousers wouldn't go over the splint.

They tried. The boy sat on the edge of the bed with his good leg in, and Hale held the bad foot as gently as he'd ever held anything, and the count worked the cloth up over the bandage an inch at a time, and at the ankle, where the iron strips stood out, it stuck. It wasn't going to go. You could see it wasn't going to go. The boy looked at it, and went pink, and said, "It's all right, I'll wear the silk ones, nobody will notice," in the voice of somebody who knew perfectly well that everybody would.

The count didn't answer. He let go of the trousers. He took a small knife out of his waistcoat pocket, a slim, plain thing with a bone handle, opened it, knelt on the carpet, and, holding the cloth taut with his other hand, cut the left leg of the trousers straight up the outside seam from the hem to a hand's breadth below the hip, in one long smooth stroke, without a waver, as if the seam had been waiting for it.

Hale heard himself breathe in.

"Oh," said the boy, faintly. "Oh. Burks. Those were somebody's."

"They were, sir," said the count, and folded the knife.

After that the trousers went on as easily as a blanket. The count gathered the two cut edges together over the splint; and then from another pocket he took a little twist of narrow silk ribbon, black, very fine, and a needle as long as a finger, and began, kneeling there, to make holes along both edges of the cut and lace them together across the bandage, crossing and crossing, the way a boot is laced, all the way up the outside of the boy's leg from ankle to hip.

Hale watched him do it. He couldn't have looked away if he'd been ordered to. Count Lairdgren, on his knees on the carpet, with a needle, lacing a boy's trousers.

It took him about as long as it would have taken Hale to find the needle. When he stood up, and stepped back, the boy looked down at himself and his mouth fell open; and so, a little, did Hale's.

It didn't look like a repair. It didn't look like anything that had been done in a hurry. The black ribbon crossed and crossed down the dark cloth, close and even, with a sliver of white bandage showing between each lacing like a slashed sleeve; and at the ankle it finished in a small flat bow; and it looked, Hale thought, like a thing a young lord might do on purpose, and have copied by every other young lord in the city by the end of the week.

"That's *beautiful*," said the boy. "Burks. Where did you learn to do that?"

"Here and there, sir."

The boy turned his foot to look at the bow. Then, all at once, his face fell straight through the floor.

"Oh, *no*," he said. "The Morgans."

The count waited.

"I promised them a present. Trice's mother and father. Eric wants to fly. I said, at lunch, I said I'd bring a shield and a rig, I've got spares, only—" He was looking round the room as if a present might be lying about in it. "Only I can't just *hand* them over, can I. At a dinner. Out of my pocket. With the cords all tangled. Have you—" He stopped, and went red, and visibly decided to ask anyway. "Have you got anything I could put them in? Anything. A box. I'd be very grateful."

"I'll see, sir," said the count, and went out again.

He was gone, Hale reckoned afterwards, about as long as it takes to boil an egg. He came back with a box.

Hale had spent thirty years in the palace. He'd stood guard on a good many beautiful things, and had learned to look at them without seeing them, which was the job. He looked at this one and saw it. It was about the length of his forearm, of some pale close-grained wood that had gone the color of honey, and every face of it was carved: branches, twisting and crossing, with leaves on them so thin the lamp shone through at the edges, and here and there among the leaves, hanging, a pear. The lid fitted so well it sighed when the boy opened it.

The boy turned it over in his hands, slowly, and ran his thumb along a branch, and didn't say anything for a while. Then he said, quite quietly, "Who *made* this?"

"I couldn't say, sir."

"Somebody who knew what they were doing." The boy held it up to the lamp. "Look. Look at the joints. You can't see them. You can't even *find* them." He looked up at the count with real reverence. "I couldn't do that. Not in a year. Thank you. I'll bring it back."

"There's no need, sir."

Two shields went in, and two rigs, from the trunk, laid in their cords on a folded cloth, one of each for the duke and one of each for the duchess. Then the boy wanted something to tie it with, and the count had silk, a length of it, purple, the deep plum purple of the royal guard's sash, and Hale felt his eyebrows go up. But the boy only said, "Oh, *purple*," in the voice of somebody being handed a sweet, and wrapped it, and tied it up with a ribbon to match, and was pleased with it, and that was all.

The count found a walking stick, too: blackthorn, with a plain silver knob, just the right height. Hale didn't see where it came from. He was getting used to that.

The eighth bell had gone. The count stood back and looked the boy up and down once, from the bow at the ankle to the hair, which was standing up at the crown again; and he reached out, without a word, and flattened it with his palm, the way you'd pat down a dog's ear. It stayed down. Hale hadn't seen anything stay down on that head in three days.

"Down the stair at the end, sir," said the count. "Then left, and left again at the tapestry with the horses, and follow the long gallery to the end. You'll hear it."

"Left, left, horses," said the boy, with his eyes shut. "Thank you, Burks. Really. For everything. I'm sorry it's always clothes."

"It's no trouble, sir," said the count, and to Hale, as he went out: "Sergeant."

Hale stood up straighter, which was all he could do, and said nothing at all; and the boy, busy getting his stick and his box arranged, didn't look up.

Hale took him down.

It went better than he'd feared. The stick did a good deal of the work, and Hale did most of the rest, and the boy did the stairs on his good foot one at a time with the box under his arm and his tongue between his teeth and said "sorry" on every one. They went left, and left again at the horses, and came out into the long gallery, and the long gallery was full.

People. Silk and velvet and bare shoulders, and the smell of pomade and roses and hot candle wax, and a great slow river of talk going all one way. Hale knew what to do with crowds. He stopped at the edge of it, and took his arm back, carefully, the way you let go of a ladder, and put the boy's hand on the knob of his stick.

"That's me, sir," he said. "Can't go in the long room. Not on this duty." He nodded at the crowd. "Follow them. They're all going the same way you are."

The boy looked at the crowd. He looked rather small, at the edge of it. Then he took a breath and got the box settled more firmly under his arm.

"Thank you, Hale," he said. "Really. I'm very grateful."

"Sir," said Hale; and watched him go, the dark-blue back and the black lacing and the purple parcel, into the river of people, until a lady's headdress with feathers in it came between them and he was gone.

* * *

Collette Coltress had been announced.

It had happened nearly a quarter of a bell ago and she was still feeling it, faintly, in her knees: *the Count Thompson, and the Baronetta Collette Coltress*, rolled out over the heads of a hundred people in a voice like the bottom of a barrel, and then three steps, and the pause, and everyone not looking at her in the particular way that meant they'd looked. She'd practiced the pause in her bedroom all of last week. She'd been told it was to be a quiet evening, in the long room; *the family, and a few others*. Her mother had read the card out loud twice and then sat down.

It was not a quiet evening. There were a hundred people in the long room, more, and every one of them was somebody.

Collette knew who. That was the thing about being from a very small family that wanted very much to be a larger one: you learned the rest by heart, from the cradle, the way other girls learned their prayers. She stood beside Count Thompson, a little behind his elbow, where a duty partner stood, holding a glass of something cold and pale she wasn't going to drink, and counted. The old Count of Salt and his second wife, who'd been his first wife's cousin. Lady Arden, in a shawl, in this heat, which meant she had one of those famous cooling plates at home and wanted everybody to wonder. Both the Harrow sons. Three of the four marcher counts, and the fourth's wife, alone, which was interesting. The ambassador from somewhere with a beard.

And there, not ten feet away, in the middle of a ring of very large men with their backs to everyone, so that all you could see of him was the top of his head, the heir to the kingdom.

Collette had been standing near the Prince Alphonse for nearly a quarter of a bell. She hadn't got over it yet.

Count Thompson hadn't said much. He didn't, she'd been told: her mother had been told, by his mother, who'd been very gracious about it, and made it plain that this was a kindness and a courtesy between houses, and nothing at all beyond. Collette didn't mind. He was tall and grave and good-looking in a way that seemed to cost him effort, and he'd said, "You look very well, Baronetta," at the foot of the stairs as though he'd checked first that it was true, and then he'd brought her this glass, and stood beside her, and watched the door. He watched it the way men in her father's stories watched a road.

At the door, the voice rolled out again.

"*His Grace the Duke of Whitcombe, and Her Grace the Duchess.*"

A tall gray man and a tall gray woman came three steps in, and paused, and were looked at, a little. Collette looked too. A duke was a duke. But they were country, and old country at that, and not *in* this year, and after a moment the talk closed back over them like water over a stone, and they went on to the line.

The herald turned to the next.

Collette saw him stop.

He was a magnificent man, the herald, built like a door, in a tabard with the falcon on it, and he'd been at that door since before she was born; he knew everyone. And he was looking down, now, at whoever stood in front of him, and his great face had gone perfectly blank. It was the blankness of a man who has opened his mouth for the hundred-and-third time that evening and found, for the first time, nothing in it.

Collette went up on her toes.

It was a boy. That was her first thought. A boy, slight, in dark blue, much smaller than anyone else in the doorway, with a stick in one hand and a parcel in purple silk under the other arm; somebody's page, she thought, or somebody's son, sent in on his own by mistake. His hair was black. He was looking up at the herald with an expression of polite, patient, apologetic interest, as if he were waiting for a shop to open.

The herald looked at his list. He looked at the boy. He looked at his list again.

One of the royal guards came off the wall, then, in purple and black, and crossed behind the herald, and bent, and said something into his ear. It was very short. Collette couldn't hear a word. She saw the herald's eyebrows go up, and stay up, and then come down again, very slowly, in a way that suggested he'd been told something and also told not to think about it.

The herald straightened. He filled his great chest.

"*Tor*," he said.

That was all. No title. No house. He didn't say *Master* or *Scholar* or *of* anywhere. One word, dropped into the long room like a stone into a well.

The boy came three steps in.

He did it well. Collette, who'd practiced, watched him do it and saw that it was hard: the stick went down, and the good foot, and then the other one, which was stiff, which went down as if he didn't trust it, very carefully, toe and heel. But he didn't limp. He kept his back straight and his chin up and walked his three steps like a soldier coming in on parade with a bad ankle, and stopped, and stood, with the stick in one hand and the purple box tucked under his other arm, and paused, to be looked at.

And the long room went quiet.

Not all at once. It went from the door outward, the way a ripple goes, as the name got to people. It went quiet in front of Collette, and beside her, and behind her; it went quiet in the ring of large men round the heir, a few of whom turned their heads; it went quiet across the whole of the long room to the windows, until she could hear the candles. A hundred people stood with glasses in their hands, looking at the door.

The boy in the doorway looked back at them. She saw him see it. She saw his face go, very slowly, from polite to puzzled to a sort of pale, frozen, tremendous courtesy, as if he had walked into the wrong house and was going to apologize to everyone in it, one by one.

Then somebody near the windows laughed at something else, too loudly, and the talk came back. It came back all at once and louder than before, and every bit of it, Collette could hear, was about him.

*Tor*.

She thought about it. The river in the sky, which she'd seen from her bedroom window that morning, a long silver thread across the hills, and her mother had made the whole household come and stand on the roof. The rigs the city guard wore. The shields. The tanners' yard, and the story of the tanners' yard, which had got bigger every time she heard it, until in the last version he'd lifted the whole midden into the sky with one hand and walked out of it holding a child in each arm. The Tor plates. *Tor*. She'd thought, everyone had thought, if they'd thought about it at all, that he'd be—

"Is *that* him?" she said, before she could stop herself. "He looks about fifteen."

"He's older than he looks," said Count Thompson. He hadn't taken his eyes off the boy since the herald spoke. His face hadn't moved, but something in it had settled, the way a man settles when a rider he's been waiting for comes round the bend at last. "He generally is."

The boy had worked out the line. Collette watched him do it. He looked round the room once, and found the welcoming table at the end of it, where the king and queen sat side by side under the falcons with the duke's party at their elbow, and found the line of guests going up to it two by two to make their bows; and he went and stood at the end of it, behind the Whitcombes, and was quite still.

He watched. She'd never seen anybody watch like that. He watched the Whitcombes make their bows as though he were going to be examined on it afterward: the angle of the duke's back, where the duchess put her hands, how long they stayed down. His lips moved. She thought he might be counting.

It was in the line that the limp began.

It wasn't much. It was only that each time the line shuffled forward a step, the stick took a little more of him, and the stiff foot took a little less, and when he stood he stood on the other one, and shifted, and shifted back. The box was under his arm; then it was clutched to his chest; then under the other arm, with the stick changed hands, awkwardly, and back again. Once, when the line stopped for a long time, she saw him put his free hand up to the collar of his shirt and touch something under it, one thing and then another, quickly, as though to make sure they were both still there. Then he took his hand away, and stood up straighter, and looked at the back of the Duke of Whitcombe's head as if it might tell him something.

"What's he holding?" said Collette.

"A present, I'd think," said Count Thompson.

"For the *king*?"

"Possibly." He considered. "Possibly not."

They'd come to the front.

Collette watched him bow, and nearly forgot to breathe. It was the Whitcombes' bow, exactly: the same angle, the same pause, the hands in the same place, made by somebody who'd learned it four minutes ago and meant every inch of it. He said something; she couldn't hear what, but she saw the shape of *Your Majesty*, twice, once to each of them. The queen inclined her head. The king smiled, a broad and genuine smile that Collette had never seen him use on the duke of anywhere, and said something back.

Then the boy did something nobody does.

He held out the box. He held it out across the welcoming table, to the king, with both hands, his stick hooked over his wrist, and said something more, earnestly, at some length, with a little gesture down at his leg.

There was a small, frozen moment along the whole of the welcoming table. Collette saw the queen's mouth do something complicated and then stop. She saw the guard on the king's left take half a step forward, and his hand go, not to anything, but very near to something.

The king, looking somewhat surprised, took the box.

He turned it over in his hands, the purple silk, the ribbon. Then he passed it to the guard, who took it as if it were full of bees, and held it out at the very end of his arms, and gave the boy over the top of it a long, flat, thorough look that went from his hair to his boots and back.

The boy smiled at him gratefully. He seemed to think the guard was being helpful.

And then the king was leaning forward and pointing, past the line, past the whole long room, at the ring of large men in the middle of it, and the boy was nodding, and bowing again, the Whitcombe bow, slightly less steady this time, and turning, and coming down the room.

Coming toward them.

He came slowly. The stick went out, and the good foot, and the other; and he'd stopped bothering not to limp, now. People made way for him. They made way the way you'd make way for something carried, without looking as if they meant to, a little farther than they needed to; and closed up behind him, and turned to watch him go, and talked. He didn't seem to see any of it. He was looking ahead, at the heir's ring of backs, with his eyes narrowed a little, like a man looking for a gap in a hedge.

There wasn't one. The men round the prince were very large and very close and very interested in whatever the prince was saying, and they stood shoulder to shoulder like the trees in a wood. The boy came up behind them and stopped. He looked at the backs. He went a little way to the left on his stick, and looked, and came back, and went a little way to the right. He went up on the toes of his good foot, and came down again with a small wince.

Then he looked round, and saw Count Thompson, and his whole face cleared. He came and stood beside them, at a respectful distance, a little behind the count's shoulder, and looked up at him hopefully, and waited to be noticed.

The count was watching the ring of backs. He didn't notice.

Collette did. He was right beside her, after all, and he'd been looked at by the entire long room, and he was standing there with his hands folded on the top of his stick like a boy outside a schoolroom door. She turned to him with her best smile, the one for when one has not been introduced and is going to manage it anyway.

"Baronetta Collette Coltress," she said, and dipped.

"Oh!" The boy bowed. It was a different bow from the one at the table; quicker, warmer, a little too deep, as if he were bowing to somebody's aunt. "How do you do. I'm Tor. I'm very glad to meet you."

"I *know*," said Collette, and then went pink, and then decided not to care. "I mean, everyone knows. Everyone heard. Isn't it *wonderful*?" She lowered her voice and leaned in to him, because she had to tell somebody, and he was there. "I shouldn't be here at all, you know. Not really. We're nobody. My father's a baronet. We had the card on fourth day and my mother had to sit down. And then I come in, and look—" she tipped her head, very discreetly, at the ring of shoulders— "the Prince Alphonse. *There*. I've been as close to him as this for a quarter of a bell. He's laughed *twice*."

The boy looked at the ring of shoulders, and then at her, and smiled. It was a nice smile. It was the kind of smile, she thought later, that people give you when you've told them their own brother is handsome.

"Alphonse?" he said warmly. "Oh, he's great. He's really great. You'll like him."

Collette stared at him.

"I was going to go and talk to him," said Tor, with the air of someone confessing a difficulty with a gate. "Only I can't get in. Everyone's so *big*." He looked up at Count Thompson's shoulder, rather wistfully. "I was hoping—would you mind very much catching Tovey's eye for me? Only he's taller. If we went in together, I think we could barge in. If it wasn't rude."

Collette, who had spent the whole of the last quarter bell trying to think of a way to barge into exactly that ring of shoulders, and had concluded it couldn't be done by anyone below the rank of a duke, found that her mouth was open, and shut it.

Then she put her hand on the count's sleeve, very lightly, and said, "My lord."

Count Thompson looked down. He saw who was beside her; and Collette, watching, saw something happen in that grave, careful face that she'd have given her new gloves to have caused herself. It wasn't a smile, quite. It was what comes before one, in men who don't smile often.

"Scholar Baker," he said.

"Tovey! Hello." The boy beamed at him. "Hello. Sorry. I didn't want to interrupt. You look very well. It's good to see you. Are you here for long? I didn't know you were coming."

"Nor did I, until fourth day." The count's eyes went, once, down to the black lacing, and the white bandage showing through it, and the stick, and came back up, and his face didn't move at all, but his voice did, a very little. "You're walking."

"Mostly." The boy waved the stick. "It's nothing. Burks did my trousers."

He'd stepped in closer, to talk, as you do. And Collette, who had been leaning in to him herself a moment ago to tell him about her mother sitting down, was still leaning, rather; and the boy, turning to say something to her too, found her shoulder a great deal nearer his face than he'd expected, and the low back of her gown, and her perfume, and went, before her eyes, a slow and thorough scarlet, from his collar up to the hair that somebody had flattened down.

"Oh," he said. "Sorry. I'm *so* sorry. Excuse me."

And he stepped smartly back, away from her, a good long step on the stick, the way you'd step back from a fire; and walked straight into somebody behind him.

Collette heard the stick knock against something. She saw him stagger, and catch himself, and turn, already apologizing.

"I'm sorry, I'm so sorry, I didn't—" He stopped. His face lit up. "*Trice!*"

It was the Ducharina Patricia Morgan.

Collette knew her, of course. Everybody knew her: the duke's daughter, the queen's niece, cousin to half the long room, who was at school at Lairdgren with the prince and was said to be the cleverest girl at court and the one you least wanted to owe money to. She was in a gown of dark blue tonight, a shade or two brighter than navy, high at the neck, very plain. She had a glass in her hand. She'd been going somewhere, fast, across the room, toward the doors to the terrace, and the boy had stepped back straight into her path.

She looked at him.

Collette was never afterward able to say exactly what was wrong with the look. Only that it went through him. It went through him to the wall behind, and the wall behind that, as if there were nothing at all standing in front of her but air, and she was looking for something a long way off and hadn't found it.

Her face was very still and very white. Her mouth was held, held hard, the way you hold a door shut against a wind. And her eyes, Collette saw, were bright; too bright, and red at the rims, under the powder, which was new, and had been put on quickly, and not quite well enough.

"I beg your pardon," said the Ducharina Patricia Morgan, quite steadily, to nobody, in the voice one uses to a stranger who has trodden on one's hem.

And she stepped round him, and went on toward the terrace doors.
