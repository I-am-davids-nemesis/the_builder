The bathing room was down the corridor and round a corner, and it was full of steam before Tor got there.

He went on Hale's arm, hopping, in the shirt that had been white, holding his breath a little so as not to smell himself, which didn't work. The room was tiled from floor to ceiling in small green squares like the skin of a cucumber. There was a bath sunk into the floor, wide enough to row a boat in, already full and smoking gently, with three steps going down into it, and a bench along one wall with towels folded on it in a stack as high as his chin. A little high window let the noon sun straight down through the steam, so that the whole room glowed like the inside of a lantern.

There was a man standing by the bench.

"Burks!" said Tor.

He was exactly as he'd been in the small house, that morning a lifetime ago: small and neat, in a plain dark coat, with his hands folded in front of him. He was hardly taller than Tor. He had the look, standing there in the steam, of a man who had been in the room for some time and would have been there whenever Tor arrived.

"Sir," said Burks.

Hale let go of Tor's arm. He let go of it rather fast, and stood up very straight, and said, "I'll be outside, sir," and went, and shut the door behind him with enormous care.

"I didn't know you were here," said Tor. "At the palace, I mean. I thought you were at the house. Did they send you? I'm sorry. You needn't. I can manage." He looked at the bath, and at the three steps, and at his leg. "Mostly."

"Of course, sir," said Burks, and came and took his elbow, and sat him down on the end of the bench as if Tor had asked him to.

Then he knelt, and began on the bandages.

He did it without any fuss at all. He found the end of the binding where it was tucked in above the knee, which Tor had looked for twice in the last ten days and never found, and unwound it in long smooth turns, and folded it as it came, so that it piled up on the tiles beside him like a ribbon on a shop counter. The padding came next, gray now and gone flat. Then the iron strips, one and then the other, lifted away and laid down side by side with a faint clink.

The air touched Tor's leg, and it was cold, and he looked down.

He hadn't meant to. He'd had a sort of agreement with the leg, the last ten days, that he wouldn't look at it and it wouldn't ask him to; and now there it was, bare to the knee in the green light, and it wasn't keeping its side.

The foot was the wrong color. Not the color it had been when he woke; worse, or better, he couldn't tell: purple gone to green at the edges and to yellow beyond that, all up the outside of the shin and across the top of the foot, like an apple dropped from a height and left in the grass. The ankle was thick. It didn't look like his ankle. It looked like somebody else's ankle that had been put on him by mistake.

And round it, all the way round, there was the rope.

Not the rope itself. The mark of it. A band, as wide as two of his fingers, gone dark and shiny and crusted along both edges, where something had pulled and pulled and gone on pulling while he hung. It went round once and crossed itself, there, on the inside, where the knot had been. He could see the twist of the strands in it. He could see, quite clearly, the place where it had bitten deepest, where he'd been heaviest.

He hadn't felt the knot go tight. He remembered that. He remembered the dark, and the smell, and his arms full of something wet and kicking, and the whole of him being pulled upward by the one foot, slowly, slowly, as if he'd been a bucket. He hadn't felt the rope at all.

He could feel it now. It didn't hurt, exactly. It sat there round his ankle and was looked at.

"Oh," said Tor.

His voice came out a little high. He cleared his throat and tried again, and that one didn't come out at all.

Burks didn't say anything. He went on kneeling there on the tiles with the folded bandage beside him, and after a moment he put one hand, quite lightly, flat on the top of Tor's good foot, and left it there. It was warm. It wasn't anything. Tor looked at the ankle a while longer, and then at the hand, and then up at the steam, and let his breath out.

"That's quite something, isn't it," he said.

"It is, sir."

"I didn't know it was like that." He laughed, which surprised him. "I'd have been more careful going down if I'd known I'd have to look at it afterward."

"I expect so, sir," said Burks, and took his hand away, and stood up. "The water's warm."

It was. It was a great deal more than warm. Getting into it was a business: Burks helped him with the shirt, and Tor, crimson, helped himself with everything else, and then there were the three steps, and the leg held out stiffly in front of him like a gate, and Burks's arm under his, quite steady, steadier than anyone's had been in ten days, Hale's included. Then the water came up round him all at once and Tor said "*Oh*," in quite a different voice, and sat down on the bottom step up to his chin, and shut his eyes.

He didn't know how long he sat there. Burks didn't seem to mind. When Tor opened his eyes again, there was soap on the edge of the bath, and a cloth, and a brush with a long handle for his back, set out in a row, as if they'd grown there; and Burks was at the far side of the room with his back politely turned, doing something with the towels.

Tor scrubbed. He scrubbed until he was pink and then until he was red. Ten days came off him in a gray film that spread out across the water and sank. The ink came off his fingers, mostly, and the copper smell out of his hair, and he went under once, all the way, holding his nose and the leg out, and came up streaming and feeling, for the first time since the midden, entirely like himself.

Afterwards there were towels, a great many, and clothes laid out on the bench that weren't his: trousers of loose dark silk, wide in the leg, with a drawstring, and a tunic to match, long and soft and cool. They slid over the leg without catching. Tor held up the trousers and looked at them with deep suspicion and then put them on, and they felt like nothing at all, like wearing a breeze.

Then Burks knelt again, with the padding and the iron strips and a fresh roll of bandage, and put the leg back together.

Tor watched him do it. He couldn't help it. The doctor had done it in the first night, Rolph had said, while Tor was still out, and it had taken him and two orderlies most of a bell and a good deal of language. Burks did it alone, kneeling on the wet tiles, in about the time it took to tie a pair of boots. The padding went where the padding had been. The strips went exactly where the strips had been, to the hair. The bandage went round and round, firm and smooth, the turns laid each half over the last like shingles on a roof, and the end tucked away above the knee where nobody would ever find it.

"You've done this before," said Tor.

"Once or twice, sir."

"It's better than the doctor's. Don't tell him." Tor turned the foot a little on the stool, cautiously. It held. "Were you a soldier?"

Burks considered this, kneeling, with his head on one side.

"Not as such, sir," he said; and stood up, and dried his hands, and opened the door for Hale.

* * *

The queen had chosen the little dining room because it had the smallest table in the palace, and because it faced east, so that once the sun was past noon it was the only room on the floor that didn't feel like the inside of a loaf.

It was still warm. Constance didn't mind warm. She'd had the shutters left open on the court, which had gone into shade, where somebody was watering the orange trees in their tubs, and the smell of wet earth came up through the windows. She'd had them set two places, not at either end, which would have meant shouting, but side by side at one corner, so that she could reach him if she had to.

She'd had a good deal of practice reaching boys at table.

He came in on the guardsman's arm, and the first thing she saw was that he'd been crying, or near it, and the second thing was that he'd scrubbed his face so hard afterward that nobody would ever be able to prove it. He was in silk, black-blue, much too big. His hair was wet and combed flat and already starting to stand up again at the crown. He looked about twelve. He looked, she thought, considerably better.

"Connie," he said. "Good afternoon. You look very nice." And then, appalled, "I mean, you always— good afternoon."

"Sit down, dear, before you fall down."

He sat. The guardsman got the leg up onto a stool and went away, and the footmen came in with the soup and went away, and Constance let him eat half a bowl before she said anything at all, which she considered heroic.

"You've made a river," she said.

Tor looked up from the soup with his spoon in his hand.

"It's been coming in all morning," said Constance. "Riders, mostly. The Ford men on the council keep getting up and going to the window, as if they could see it from here. Old Lord Harrow wept. He'll deny it." She broke a roll. "It's a new river. It can't go about without a name. People are already calling it something, of course, in the taverns. The River Tor."

The spoon went down.

"Oh, *no*," said Tor.

"It's rather a good name. It's short."

"No, Connie, really." He'd gone pink to the hair. "Please. You can't. It's— it's not a *name*, it's me. Nobody names a river after a person. Imagine having to *live* on it." He looked round the room, as if there might be a better name lying about somewhere in it. "It should be something—something proper. Something that sounds like a river. The—"

He stopped. Constance waited.

"Not the King's River," he said. "That's taken. That's the one by the city. But—" and his face cleared, all at once, the way it did over copper. "The falcons! It's held up by falcons. Three of them. And the falcon's Rich's, isn't it, it's on everything, the gate, the guards, the spoons." He held up his spoon, which had a falcon on the handle, as evidence. "The Falcon River. That's good. That's much better. People will like that. They'll hear about a river in the sky that the king made to stop the drought, and they'll call it the Falcon River, and it'll be his, and that's—that's right, isn't it? That's what a king's for. Nobody wants a river named after a student."

"The king," said Constance, "did not make it."

"He sent the birds."

"He sent the birds." She set the roll down. "You heard about Ford at a dinner, Torrance. At a dinner. And the next thing anyone knew, you were planning it from a sickbed with your leg in irons, before you'd even had a drink of water. Nobody asked you. Rich didn't ask you."

"He did, though," said Tor, earnestly. "You were *there*. In the dining room, the night of the Ward dinner. He said it would take a river. Of water. To fix it. Those were his words, nearly. I only did what he said."

Constance looked at him.

She did it for long enough that he must have felt it, because he looked back; and she watched him look, and saw something happen in his face that she hadn't seen before. It was quite small. He went red. Not pink, which he went when anyone so much as passed him the salt; red, all at once and all the way down into the collar of the borrowed silk, and he stopped, with his mouth a little open, in the middle of whatever he'd been going to say next, and looked at her, and then very quickly at his soup.

Poor lamb, Constance thought. He'd been back among the living for three days, after six days gone, and she was sitting there arguing with him about the naming of rivers. He was exhausted. It was in every line of him.

"Anyway," said Tor, to the soup, rather fast. "Anyway. I ought to go and look at it. The river. The bit at the gorge especially, where it comes down. I ought to see how it's sitting. If I took a cart, I could keep the leg up in it. Or I could fly, I could fly lying down, I've never tried that, but there's no reason—and then there's the carts. For the Debris. The floating ones, I said I would, I ought to have started weeks ago, and they'll be wondering—"

"No," said Constance.

He stopped.

"No," she said again. She'd put her hand flat on the table. She hadn't decided to. "You will not. You will not take that leg and make it your reason to go and work yourself into the ground. I have watched you do it for six days, Torrance. I have *watched* it. I went in on the fourth day and you looked straight through me." She heard her own voice, and brought it down, and didn't apologize for it. "You promised me. In that room. Not to strain yourself. You will sit, and you will eat, and you will let that leg mend, and the Debris will wait. They are a merchant house. They have been waiting for things since before your grandfather was born."

There was a silence. In the court below, the man with the watering can had stopped watering.

"Could I have some water?" said Tor, in a very small voice.

It was so transparent that she nearly laughed. She didn't. She reached past him to the box at the corner of the table, a squat thing of dark wood with a lid that fitted close and a lining of something padded, which the kitchens packed every morning with ice from the cellars; and lifted the lid, and the cold came out of it in a soft breath across the cloth, and she took out the jug, beaded all over, and poured him a glass.

He wasn't looking at the glass. He was looking at the box.

Then he laughed. It was a short, startled, private laugh, as if somebody had told him a joke from a long way off, and he put his hand over his mouth.

"Sorry," he said. "Sorry. It's nothing. It's only—that's a very good box."

"It's a box, dear. The kitchens fill it."

"Yes. With ice. From the cellars. Every day." He was still looking at it. "And then the ice goes in the box, and the kitchens are hot, and the ovens are hot, and there's all that heat going up the chimneys, and all that cold coming up the stairs in buckets, and they never—" He stopped. He took a drink of water. "Could I have one?" he said. "A box like that. In my room. Only a small one. Not for work. Not exactly. I only want to check something."

Constance had three children, all of them liars of considerable skill, and she knew *not exactly* when she heard it.

She looked at him over the rim of her glass. He looked back, all innocence, a smear of ink still on one knuckle that the bath had missed.

She lifted two fingers from the tablecloth. She didn't say anything.

Somewhere behind her, a footman went out of the room without a sound.

Tor watched him go. Then he looked at the two fingers, and at her, and she saw him remember where he'd seen it done before. He knew perfectly well what it meant. It was going to happen now. It was probably happening already.

"Thank you," he said, a little helplessly.

"Eat your soup," said the queen.

* * *

The door opened while he was eating it, and her sister came in like weather.

Mercy never came into a room; she arrived in it. She was in a traveling dress of dove gray that had plainly been on since dawn and was still, somehow, perfect, and she had her bonnet in one hand by its strings and her gloves in the other, and she was talking before the door had finished opening.

"Connie, there you are, they said the east room and I said she'll never, and here you are, it's like an oven in the west wing, how do you bear it—" and then she was across the room and had both of Constance's hands and was kissing the air beside each of her cheeks, one, two, the way they'd done it at twelve to annoy their mother. She smelled of road dust and the lavender water she'd been wearing since she was fifteen.

Behind her, with a great scrape of chair and a clatter of spoon, Tor was getting up.

He did it the hard way, as he did everything, hand on the table, hand on the chair back, the splinted leg swinging down off its stool like a dropped oar; and then he was on his one good foot with his napkin still in his fist, very straight, bowing a little from the waist and wobbling. He was looking at Mercy with a faint, puzzled frown. Constance knew that frown. It was the face of someone who'd seen a face before and couldn't think where. Mercy had their mother's eyes, and the Morgan house had given her a daughter with the same ones.

"Sit down, Torrance," said Constance, "you'll fall in the soup."

He didn't sit down.

"We've only just got in," said Mercy, letting go of her. "Eric's gone straight to Rich, of course, they'll be in the map room till midnight. I wanted a bath and I wanted a lie-down and I said no, first, I want to see this *river*. Is it true? It's in the sky? Somebody at the last inn said you could drink it." She laid her gloves on the table. "I said, then I must have some. I've been told it's purer than anything. Perhaps it cures things. One never knows with magic water. At my age I'll take what I can get." She looked round. "Have you seen my eldest? I've been through four rooms."

"In the market, I'd think," said Constance. "There's a ship in from Afrak. Bolts of the striped stuff, and that blue they do. The girls have been talking of nothing else since breakfast." She picked up her glass. "Patricia, Karina, Varley, and Sara Debri. And Alphonse is carrying the parcels."

"*Alphonse* is?"

"He volunteered."

"Did he," said Mercy, with enormous meaning. "For which of them?"

"Mercy."

The boy was still standing. He'd got hold of the back of his chair with both hands now, and he was looking from one of them to the other, and the frown had turned into something else; something that went slowly pink at the edges, as if he'd only just noticed that he was a young man in a borrowed silk tunic, standing on one leg in front of two women he'd been told nothing about, and that one of them was holding a bonnet and the other was the queen. He cleared his throat. Nobody heard it. He waited, holding on, while Mercy said something about the roads and Constance said something about the heat, and then there was the smallest gap, the width of a breath, and he put himself into it.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Excuse me. I'm sorry. I don't think I know your name."

Both of them looked at him.

"Oh, I am sorry, dear," said Constance. "How rude of me." She set her glass down and made the introduction properly, from her chair, as her mother had taught them both to do in another life. "My sister. The Sitting Baroness, Merciful Thorgood Morgan." And then, turning her hand toward him with exactly the same weight, not a grain more: "Torrance Baker."

That was all.

She watched Mercy hear it. *Torrance Baker*: a commoner's name, said plainly, for a small boy in borrowed clothes at the queen's own table. Mercy's eyebrows went up, very slightly, and then her manners came down over them like a shutter, and she smiled at him as she'd have smiled at a ward of the house, or a page with promise, kindly and without interest.

"Master Baker," she said.

"My lady," said Tor, and bowed, and wobbled, and Constance took pity and pointed at his chair until he sat in it.

She was enjoying herself. She was aware of it, and she wasn't going to stop. She'd watched the whole of the court spend a month trying to work out what to say to this boy, and how much, and with what in their hands; and here was her sister, who wanted something, she could see it in the set of the bonnet strings, sitting down across from him with no idea at all who he was. Constance wanted very much to see what he'd do with her.

* * *

Mercy took the chair at the end, and a glass of the water, and drank it, and pronounced it extraordinary, and talked about the roads.

She talked about the roads for some time. She talked about the inn at Coldwell, and the heat, and how brown everything had gone east of the hills, and how her steward had wept on her doorstep about the pears. Constance let her. She knew her sister, and she knew that the pears were the reason for the bonnet strings, and that Mercy would come round to them in her own time, as a hawk comes round, wider and then narrower.

The boy listened. He ate his soup, carefully, and drank his water, and listened, with his head a little on one side. Once or twice his eyes went to the ice box and stayed there, and his lips moved; and once he said, very quietly, apparently to the spoon, "*Both* sides, though. That's the thing. If you put them back to back," and then seemed to remember where he was, and went back to listening.

"It's the orchards, Connie," said Mercy at last. "I'll be honest. I didn't come all this way for a drink of water." She turned her glass round on the cloth. "You know what the barony's like. Pears and apples, top to bottom, the whole length of the valley, it's all we've ever been good for. And this year they're *perfect*. I've never seen them like it. Not a mark on them. The trees are bent double. It's the heat; it's done something to them. And it's all going to rot where it hangs."

"Surely not all of it."

"Half. More. We can't get it in. We can't pick it fast enough, and when we do, we can't get it anywhere before it turns. You can't send a pear two hundred miles in this. It goes to cider in the cart." She sat back. "I'd thought of the dryers. Everyone's heard of the dryers. Dry them on the farm and you could send them to the moon. But the Debri house has stopped taking orders for them. They're full up with something else, something better, I gather, that pays more, and they've no hands to spare for a few baronies' worth of fruit. I wondered—" She lifted one shoulder. "I wondered if you had a word with anyone there. You know everyone."

"You might ask Alphonse," said Constance. "He's been very thick with Sara Debri lately." She let that sit a moment, for her sister's benefit. "Or Patricia, for that matter. She and Sara are as close as two pins."

There was a clatter. The boy had dropped his spoon.

He was staring at Mercy. His mouth was open. He had gone the color of the wine.

"You're *Trice's* mother," he said.

Mercy looked at him.

"I'm sorry," said Tor. "I'm sorry, I should have—only you've got her— and I couldn't think who— Patricia. You're Patricia's mother." He'd got halfway up again, on the table. "I'm so glad to meet you. Really. I'm *very* glad. She's been so kind to me. She's—she's very good to me, she's one of my best friends, she—" He seemed to hear himself, and stopped, and sat down. "I'm very glad to meet you," he said again, to the table.

"Well," said Mercy, a little blankly. "Thank you, Master Baker."

"He's never that polite to Richard," said Constance.

"*Connie*," said Tor, aghast.

"You aren't, dear. You call him Rich and argue with him about rivers."

He opened his mouth and shut it again, and Mercy, who had been looking from one of them to the other with her head tilted exactly like her daughter's, laughed for the first time since she came in; and the talk went on.

It went where talk between sisters goes. It went to the pears, and from the pears to the apples, and from the apples, by way of Mercy's steward, to the peaches on the south wall, which were the size of a baby's head this year and would all be wasted, and then to the river in the sky, which Mercy had seen from the road, a long faint line across the blue like a pencil stroke, and had not believed. It went to the guards who flew now, over the city walls, who'd made the horses shy all along the north road; to the cost of the things; to what everyone said they'd cost. It went, at last, as Constance had known it would, to Eric.

"He's beside himself," said Mercy. "He's wanted to fly since he was six. He talks of nothing else. Every time one of those guards goes over he stands in the yard with his mouth open like a boy. I tell him he's a grown man with a duchy and he'd break his neck, and he says that's what the shields are for." She shook her head. "I don't suppose there's one to be had in the kingdom for under the price of a good farm, and a waiting list as long as my arm besides."

"Oh, he can have one of mine," said Tor.

Both women turned.

"I've got spares," said Tor. "In my trunk. A couple of rigs, I think, three, and the shields to go with them; I always bring spares. He can try it. Or keep it, if he likes it. Or—" his face brightened—"he could just ask Trice. She'd lend him hers. She'd *teach* him."

Mercy put her glass down.

"You'd lend him," she said slowly, "a Tor rig."

"Well, yes. If he wants one."

"Master Baker. Do you know what those *cost*?"

"Not really," said Tor, honestly. "Sara does all that. I'm sorry."

Mercy looked at him as if he'd taken off his head and offered her that. Then, a moment later, something else crossed her face, and Constance watched it arrive. "And Patricia," said Mercy. "Patricia can *fly*?"

"Oh, very well," said Tor warmly. "Much better than me. She's got a feel for it. She went straight up the first time, she didn't even wobble. She'll go round the tower twice before breakfast if Rolph dares her." He waved his hand at the rigs, the shields, the cost, the whole of it. "Honestly, it's no trouble. I brought extras for presents. Everybody keeps not taking them." He considered. "Rich isn't allowed. The council won't let him, they said if he fell off they'd have to have a war about it. And Connie won't, because of the heights."

Constance, who had been holding her napkin to her mouth for some time, held it a little tighter.

"You're afraid of heights?" said Mercy to her, as if she'd only that moment heard of it, though she had pulled her down off the hayloft ladder at eight.

"I'm not *afraid*," said Constance, into the napkin. "I'm sensible."

"She won't go up the bell tower," said Tor, kindly. "But it's all right. Lots of people don't like it. So he could ask Trice. Or if he wanted a proper teacher, Rolph's very good, Rolph's the best of all of us at— Alphonse," he said hastily, catching the queen's eye, "Prince Alphonse, I mean, he'd show him. Or Tovey. Count Thompson. He's one of the best flyers there is. Though he's busy now, I think, he's got a county to run. He's not lazy like me."

There was a short silence while both sisters took this in, in their different ways.

Then the boy put his hands flat on the table, on either side of his empty bowl, and Constance saw his face change; and she put the napkin down.

She'd seen it before. She'd seen it in the small house the first week, and again in the sickroom, with a stylus in his teeth and a leg in irons. It was the look he wore when an idea had come into him all the way and was standing up inside him, looking out.

"Your fruit," he said to Mercy. "The pears. How long have you got? Before it turns?"

"Two weeks for the pears," said Mercy, startled. "Less for the peaches. Why?"

"Because I could probably get you dryers," said Tor. "And rigs, or—or something like rigs, that carry instead of fly, for the fruit, so you could get it off the trees and to where it's going before it goes to cider. I've been meaning to build those anyway." He took a breath. "Only, I'd want half."

Mercy's eyebrows went up.

"Not for me," said Tor quickly. "Not half of all of it. Half of what would have spoiled. The part you'd have lost anyway." He'd gone pink again, but he kept going. "It's Ford. The river helps, it'll help the crops next year, but this year's are mostly gone, and they're already short, the villages, and the prices are going up, I heard it at the inn. If they could have the fruit, dried, it'd keep all winter. It'd stop—" He stopped himself. "It'd help a great deal. That's all. It isn't for me."

Mercy looked at him for a long moment.

Then she smiled, a slow, wide, delighted smile, and leaned forward a little on her elbow, and let her eyes go soft, the way she'd done at seventeen to every young lord at their mother's table, and to a good many old ones; and Constance, who had seen it done to a bishop, watched her sister do it, beautifully, to a boy in borrowed silk who looked about twelve.

"Could you really, though," said Mercy, low and warm and amused, "do all of that? Little you?"

Constance set down her glass.

She didn't do it loudly. She didn't need to. She'd spent a good many years learning how a queen puts a glass down, and every head in that room turned to it, including the footman's.

"Mercy," she said. "If Torrance says he can do a thing, where magic is concerned, then he can. You may take it as said by the crown."

Mercy looked at her.

It took a moment. Constance watched it travel: the commoner's name, the borrowed clothes, the spare rigs in a trunk, the dryers, the river in the sky; and Trice's letters, all spring, about a boy at school called Tor, whom she'd pictured, Constance would have bet her pearls on it, as six feet tall and twenty-five.

"Oh," said Mercy faintly. "*That* Tor?"

"Yes," said Constance. "That Tor."

The boy looked between them, puzzled. "I'm sorry," he said. "Is there another one?"

* * *

Mercy recovered faster than most. She had always been the quicker of the two of them; it was only that Constance had married the king.

"I can't promise you anything for Eric," she said, sitting up, a different woman, all the softness gone and something like Trice's expression in its place, the one that counted. "The wheat's his. I'll not speak for him in his own fields. But I'll say this." She tapped one finger on the cloth. "If you can do all that for the orchards, Master— Tor, then I'd be very surprised if he couldn't find a tenth of the wheat for your Ford as well. An eighth, if I'm the one who asks him. It keeps better than pears."

Tor looked as if he'd been handed a pony.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, *thank* you. That's wonderful. That's—" He'd half pushed his chair back already. Constance saw his hands go to his pockets, as if there might be copper in them, and come out empty. "I could start this afternoon, the dryer's only—"

He stopped. His lips moved. "No," he told himself, under his breath, quite firmly. "Think, Tor. The template's at the Debris. All three went. It'd be twice as quick from the template as from my head, and Sara could send it up tomorrow." His eyes went, very briefly, to the ice box at the corner of the table, and back. "And anyway," he said, aloud, to no one, "I've got the box to look at."

"*Not exactly* for work," said Constance.

He went scarlet.

"May I be excused, Connie?" he said. "Please. If that's all right. My lady." He bowed to Mercy, sitting down, which ought to have been impossible. "I'm very glad to have met you. Really."

"You may," said the queen.

He got himself up, table and chair and good foot, and the guardsman was in the doorway before he'd called for him, and took his arm. He'd got as far as the door when Constance spoke again.

"Torrance."

He turned, holding on.

"Dinner," said Constance, "is at the ninth bell of the night. The family's dining in the long room, and Mercy and Eric, and a few others." She picked up her glass. "You're expected."

"Oh," said Tor. He looked down at the borrowed silk, which was all he had that hadn't been in a midden. "Yes, Connie. Thank you. I'll be there."

The door closed behind him, and the guardsman's slow step and the boy's hop went away down the corridor together, a long way, and turned the corner.

Mercy was looking at her across the table.

"Connie," she said. "What are you up to?"

Constance drank her water. It was very cold, and very pure, and she had to admit there was something in it.

"Eat something, dear," she said. "You've been on the road since dawn, and it's going to be a long night."
