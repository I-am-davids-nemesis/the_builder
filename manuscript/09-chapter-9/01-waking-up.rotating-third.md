The ceiling was the first thing, and it was wrong.

It was too far off. Ceilings at Lairdgren were near enough to hit with a boot, and the one in the small house, which Tor had privately thought very grand, had a border of plaster leaves round it and stopped there. This one had a sky on it. Somebody had painted the whole of it, blue going to gold toward the corners, with a long slant of birds crossing it and a few small, well-behaved clouds; and somebody else, a good while later, had painted over a crack in one corner with a little more sky, and the blue didn't match.

Tor looked at the crack for some time. It was the only thing up there he understood.

Then he turned his head, and the bed went on. It went on past his hand, and past where his hand would have reached if he'd stretched it, out to a far white edge and a carved post as thick as a young tree, and then a long way of polished floor before anything that could be called a wall. He could have slept the whole family in it, with the twins crosswise at the bottom. He thought of saying so to someone, and there wasn't anyone, so he didn't.

He tried to sit up, and his leg told him not to.

He didn't make a sound. It was more that the whole of him stopped at once and waited, the way the kitchen at home stopped when somebody dropped the big crock and nobody had looked yet. After a while he let his breath out. He got his elbows under him, slowly, and then his hands, and worked himself up against the pillows a little at a time, and looked down the bed.

Somebody had built a cage round his leg. That was how it looked. Two strips of dull iron ran down either side of it from above the knee to past the heel, and over them, round and round, went bandages, tight and white and neat as a loaf done up in cloth for market, with padding underneath that he could feel at the edges, soft. His foot stuck out of the end. It was his foot. It was an odd color, and he didn't look at it long. The whole thing throbbed: not sharply, and not anywhere in particular, only steadily, all over, in time with something; and after a moment he understood that the something was him.

"Right," said Tor. "Well. That's that, then."

He'd looked once. That was enough. He looked at the rest of himself instead. He was wearing a shirt that wasn't his, long and loose and very fine, with strings at the neck instead of buttons and sleeves that came down past his fingers. He was clean. Someone had washed him, all of him, and even under his nails. He decided not to think about that either.

The room was cool.

That was the strange part, once he noticed it. The shutters were closed on every window, and the afternoon came through the slats in long hot stripes across the floor, and he could hear the city out there baking, a far-off mutter of carts and pigeons and somebody's dog. But in here it was cool as the cellar at home, and the air smelled of nothing at all, which in the capital in summer was very nearly a miracle. On the wall across from the bed, between two tall windows, at about the height of a man's head, hung a square of copper on a nail, its lines faintly bright.

Tor knew it at once. He knew the one line in the bottom corner that went in a little and came out again, where he'd sneezed.

"Oh, hello," he said to it.

It was one of his spares, out of his own trunk. Somebody had gone through the trunk and found it and brought it all this way and hung it up, the right way round, and set it to cool, which half the third years at Lairdgren still couldn't manage. He felt obscurely that he ought to thank whoever it was. He'd have to find out who.

Then he found out that he was thirsty.

It came all at once, the way you find out you've been standing in a draft: his mouth was dry as a sack, and his tongue felt like a piece of felt that had been left on a windowsill. And behind that, now that he'd noticed the one, came the other, which was worse, and growing more urgent by the moment.

He looked round. There was a tall chest with brass handles. There was a chair with a tapestry seat, all roses. There was a little table by the bed with nothing on it but a folded cloth. There was a door, tall and dark and a long way off. There was no jug. There was no basin. There was no small, discreet door in a corner that might lead somewhere useful. It was the emptiest grand room he'd ever been in, as if someone had come through it and taken away everything a person might want, so that he'd have nothing to get up for.

"Think, Tor," he said. "Focus now."

He worked it out like a sum. The chair was an arm's length and a bit from the bed. From the chair to the bedpost, a step. From the post to the wall, two. Then along the wall to the door, eight, if they were hops, which they would be.

He got the leg off the bed with both hands under the knee, the way he'd have lifted a sack of flour that had split, and set it down on the floor heel first. The floor was cold. The leg had a great deal to say about the trip, and he waited, holding on to the sheets, until it had finished saying it. Then he stood up on the good one and caught the back of the chair.

The chair went with him. It went across the floor in a long, screeching scrape that rang off the painted sky like a dropped tray, and Tor went after it, hopping.

"Sorry," he told it. "Sorry. Sorry. Nearly there."

It wasn't nearly there. He gave up on the chair at the bedpost and held on to the post instead, breathing, and then let go of the post and got to the wall and put both hands flat on it, and went along it sideways, hop and lean, hop and lean, past the tall chest and its brass handles, with the sweat starting cold under the borrowed shirt. The door was heavier than it looked. He got his whole weight on the handle, and it gave, and the door swung in toward him, and he held on to the frame and looked out.

* * *

Hale had been on that door since the night bell, and he'd heard the story four times.

He'd heard it first in the guardroom, from a man who'd heard it from a man in the city guard; then on the stair, from a maid; then from Gerry Pask, beside him, who'd had it from his sister, who sold eels on the river stairs and had been nowhere near it. In the first telling there'd been a well and a lad and a lot of dirt. By Gerry's sister's, the dirt had gone up into the sky as high as the palace dome and hung there while the man in the middle of it pulled two children out of the ground one-handed, and spoke to the earth, and the earth answered him. Hale didn't believe the half of it. He'd stood his whole watch beside the door all the same, at a slightly better attention than usual, with his back to it and a picture in his head of whoever might be lying on the other side. A big man, he'd thought. A man like a door himself.

So when the handle turned behind him and he swung round, his hand already halfway up to his chest in the salute he gave perhaps twice a year, his mouth started a word on its own.

"Coun—"

He stopped it. He'd no idea what it had been about to do.

"Master Tor," he said, and felt his ears go hot, and decided that hadn't happened.

Beside him, Gerry looked at him sideways and then very carefully didn't.

The hero of the tanners' yard was holding on to the doorframe with both hands. He came up to about Hale's breastbone. He was in a nightshirt that came down past his knees, and his left leg was out in front of him in its splint, held clear of the floor like a heron's, and his face was the color of old candle wax and shiny with sweat, and his black hair stood straight up on one side where the pillow had had it. He looked at Hale, and at Gerry, and at their purple and black, and at the swords at their belts. He looked at the corridor behind them, which was long and empty and ended in a window.

Something went across his face then that Hale had seen before, on recruits marched up before the sergeant on their first morning. It went quickly. What came after it was courtesy.

"I'm sorry," said Master Tor. "Excuse me. Am I allowed out?"

Hale didn't follow him. "Out, sir?"

"Only I wasn't sure." The boy, the man, whatever he was, was holding the frame very tight, and being very polite about it. "If you're here to keep me in, I'll go back. I don't mind. I only wanted to ask something first."

Gerry made a small noise.

"We're here to keep folk *out*, sir," Hale said. "Orders. Nobody's to disturb you."

"Oh!" The face changed completely. "Oh, good. That's very kind of them. Thank you." A pause, in which Hale watched him notice that he was, at that moment, disturbing two members of the royal guard. "I'm sorry. I'm disturbing you."

"No, sir."

"I'm afraid I'm going to do it a bit more." He took a breath. "Could I have some water, please? If it's no trouble. And—" He looked down the corridor and back, and went, all at once and completely, the color of a radish. "And is there a privy anywhere? I'm sorry. I couldn't find one in there. I looked."

Hale had been asked for a great many things on a great many doors, over the years, by people a great deal less polite. He'd never been asked for anything in a voice like that, as if the asker fully expected to be told no and would thank him for it. It was nerves, he decided. You saw it in the young ones after a fight, sometimes: they came out of it shaking, and sorry, and very careful with everybody.

"Gerry," he said.

Gerry was already stopping a maid at the turn of the corridor, a girl with an armful of sheets, who listened and stared past him at the doorway and went off at a run. The privy was the second door down, which was near enough that the door was still in sight from it, which made it the door's business. Hale gave the boy his arm.

He'd meant to take some of the weight. There was no weight to take. Master Tor came off the doorframe and onto Hale's forearm like a coat being hung on a peg, and hopped, and hopped, gripping, with his tongue between his teeth, down the corridor. Hale had carried his own niece farther than that, and she was six.

When they came back, there was a jug on a tray on the floor by the door, sweating with cold, and a cup beside it, and the maid hovering at a distance as if the jug might do something. Master Tor drank three cups straight off, standing on one leg in the corridor with his free hand flat on the wall, and then let his breath out in a long sigh, and his shoulders came down from round his ears for the first time, and he smiled at the maid, who fled.

"Thank you," he said to Hale. "Really. Thank you. I wasn't sure, you see. Whether I was in trouble."

"No, sir," said Hale. "Not that I've heard."

"That's all right, then."

He didn't go back in. Hale had thought he would. He stood in the doorway holding on, and looked down the corridor to the window at the end, where the light was, and seemed to be making up his mind about something.

"Could I see the king?" he said.

Gerry made his small noise again.

"King Richard," said Master Tor, as if there might be some confusion. "Not now. Whenever's convenient. It's about the drought. In Ford." He was going pink again, at the edges. "I know he's very busy. You needn't say it's me, if that's awkward. Only that it's about Ford."

"I'll pass it up, sir," said Hale. "I can't promise you anything."

"No. No, of course not. Thank you."

He let Hale help him back as far as the chair with the roses on it, and Hale, without being asked, turned the chair to face the door and found a footstool from somewhere behind the chest for the leg. When he went to pull the door to behind him, Master Tor said, "Oh, could you leave it? Please. Only—" and stopped, and looked up at the painted sky and round at the long, empty, cool, magnificent room. "It's a very big room," he said.

Hale left it open.

* * *

The great bell went for the next hour, deep enough to set the shutters humming, and somewhere after it Master Tor said, from his chair, "I'm sorry. Do you think somebody might fetch my things?"

Hale turned. "Your things, sir."

"My working things. From my room in the small house. There's a box." He sketched it in the air with both hands. "Wooden. Not the big trunk, the box, the one that looks as if a cart went over it, because one did. It's got copper in it, and the inks, and my styluses in a roll, and the acid, but that's stoppered, it won't spill. Whoever goes will know it." He thought. "It smells a bit."

They couldn't leave the door. That was the whole of their orders, and the orders had come down from a long way above the sergeant. Gerry caught a page at the turn of the corridor, a small boy in livery too big for him, and gave him the message, and the boy repeated it back, *box, cart, copper, smells*, and went. Hale went after him three steps, as far as the corner, and said something else to him, low, and came back to his place. Gerry didn't ask what. Hale didn't say.

The page took most of an hour. It was a long way to the small house and back, and he was a small page.

Master Tor filled the time.

He didn't seem to know he was doing it. He sat in the rose chair with his splinted leg up on the stool and his hands knotted together in his lap, looking at the wall, and after a while his lips started to move; and after a while longer, sound came out of them, as if he'd forgotten there was a door, or that it was open, or that there were two men standing just outside it with nothing in the world to do but listen.

"It's not the water," he said. "Water's easy. There's all the water you like. There's a whole sea of it, sitting there doing nothing, right next to Ford, and Ford's dying of thirst. That's the stupid part. It's like starving in a bakery." He rubbed his face. "It's the holding. A line that long has to be held at the ends. And in the middle, I think. It's got to be held by something that stays where you put it."

Hale looked straight ahead down the corridor. Gerry looked straight ahead down the corridor.

"Something big," said Master Tor. "Big and heavy. Heavier than heavy. Something you can't pick up and carry off and sell. People will sell anything. Old Mister Pruett at home sold a gate once." He paused. "It wasn't his gate." He went on frowning at the wall. "And something nobody will meddle with. Nobody will want to knock it over or try what happens if you scratch a bit off. Something people will look at and think, no, I'd better not. Something that looks as if it belongs to someone." A sigh. "Metal, I suppose. Everything I've made since spring has been copper, and copper's never let me down, so I'd rather not start arguing with something new halfway across a county. A great lot of metal, then. Where do you even get a great lot of metal that nobody's using?"

There was a silence. A pigeon landed on the windowsill at the end of the corridor, thought about it, and left.

"The king asked me to," Master Tor told the wall. Then, more honestly: "Well. He said it was a problem. At dinner. That's asking, really, isn't it? From a king." He thought about it. "I think it's asking."

Hale glanced sideways. Gerry had stopped pretending. He was standing exactly as before, with his eyes front and his hand on his belt; but his head had come round a quarter inch toward the open door, and stayed there.

When the page came back, he had the box in his arms, and he set it down on Master Tor's knees as if it were a sleeping cat. Master Tor said "Oh, *thank* you," with a warmth that made the page go scarlet to the ears, and opened the lid, and a sharp, sour, metallic smell came out of it into the corridor; and after that, for a long while, he didn't talk to anyone. He had a slate on his good knee and a stylus in his teeth and copper squares laid out on the little table he'd dragged over by one leg, and his head was on one side.

Hale didn't mention what he'd said to the page at the corner. Nobody had asked him.

* * *

It was two hours by the bell before they came, and Hale heard them a long while before he saw them.

He heard a scrape, far off, round the turn of the corridor; then a grunt, and a man's voice saying something short that wasn't meant for a palace; then a second voice, dry and level, saying "*Language*, Baines," and the first voice saying, "Sorry, Sergeant." Then round the corner came a falcon.

It was bronze, and it was enormous. It stood with its wings half open, as if it had come down on something and not yet folded them, and in its talons it gripped a shield with the old arms worn almost smooth; and it was green-black with age along every feather, except at the tip of the beak, where years of hands had rubbed it to gold. It stood as tall as Hale's shoulder. It must have weighed as much as a cart horse. And it was floating.

Not far. Two inches, perhaps, off the flags. Hale bent his head and saw, under its base, the edge of a square of copper; and then another; and then a third, the three of them laid side by side under the bronze plinth like plates under a pudding, and the whole of it riding along the corridor on them, slow and smooth and slightly lopsided, with two sweating footmen walking beside it with their hands flat on its flanks, steering.

Then came a second one, the same. Then a third.

Hale had never seen them before in his life, which meant they'd come from somewhere in the palace he'd never been, which was most of it. He didn't ask where.

Last of all came Sergeant Finely, long and gray and unhurried, with the knot of his rank on his shoulder and his hands behind his back, looking at the falcons the way he looked at recruits: as if they had done nothing yet to disappoint him, but would.

The doorway was the worst of it. The first falcon went through with a hand's breadth to spare on either wingtip. The second scraped. Finely shut his eyes briefly on the sound and opened them again. The third went through like a cork into a bottle, and the footmen turned it, gently, and brought it to rest beside the other two in the middle of that long, cool, empty room, where they hung side by side two inches off the floor in the striped light, three bronze birds with their wings half spread, and filled it.

Master Tor was staring at them. He had the slate in one hand and the stylus still in his mouth, and his face had gone the way faces went in the temple when the choir came in.

"Those are my plates," he said, round the stylus. He took it out. "Those are my *plates*. Under them. Look. Three each. Oh, the poor things, they're doing their best." He bent to see. "They're barely off the ground. How did you get them up the *stairs*?"

"With difficulty, sir," said Finely.

He came and stood before the chair, and looked down at Master Tor, and Hale, from the door, watched him take in the nightshirt and the leg and the size of him; and his long face did nothing at all.

"Sergeant Finely, sir," he said. "Will these be appropriate?"

But Master Tor was no longer in the chair. He'd got out of it somehow, Hale didn't see how, and gone down on his good knee on the floor beside the nearest falcon with the splinted leg stuck out sideways behind him like a rudder, and laid his hand flat on the bronze. He held it there. His eyes had gone a long way off.

"Oh," he said, softly. "Oh, they're *lovely*."

"Sir."

"They're perfect." He looked up at Finely, and his face was alight. "If the king lets me. If he says yes, they'd be perfect, they'd be exactly— look at this. Feel it. No, really, put your hand here." Finely, after a moment, put his hand there. "That's all one pour. All of it, wings and all. Somebody poured that in one go, a long time ago, and it's been sitting still ever since, holding itself together. You can feel how still it is. It'd hold anything." He took his hand away and put it back, as if he couldn't help it. "It's the most interesting thing I've ever had to put a field on. By miles. And nobody'll steal one. Look at it. You'd need a cart and six men and a very good excuse."

"Quite so, sir," said Finely.

"And anyway," said Master Tor, quite seriously, kneeling on the floor of the palace in a borrowed nightshirt with his hand on the king's bronze falcon, "you'd have to be pretty daft to steal something with a river tied to it."

Finely looked at him for a long moment. Then he looked across the room at the door, and at Hale in it; and Hale, for want of anywhere better, looked up at the painted sky, and the crack in the corner with the wrong blue on it.

He'd stood his watch expecting a man like a door. He found he didn't mind in the least what he'd got instead.
