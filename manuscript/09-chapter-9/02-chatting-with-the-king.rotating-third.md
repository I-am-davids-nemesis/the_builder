Hale saluted.

Rolph had walked past Hale on doors for most of his life, and had seen him salute perhaps three times, and never like that: the whole arm, the heel down hard on the flags, the eyes fixed on a point somewhere above the king's left ear. His father nodded to him as he went by, the small nod he gave furniture he was fond of. Then he ducked his head under the lintel, out of long habit, though the lintel was a foot higher than he needed, and went in, and the rest of them went in after him.

There were a good many of them. Rolph hadn't counted until he was in the room and saw them all reflected in the long polished floor at once: his father, enormous, in his plain dark morning coat; his mother on his father's arm, in gray, with the copper square on its string tucked out of sight under her collar; Karina behind her, in pale green, already looking round the room for something to have an opinion about; Varley beside Karina, holding on to her sister's sleeve, which she hadn't done since she was nine; and, last, a little apart, in the robes he wore at court and nowhere else, cream with a broad yellow band down the front, Smythe of Westland.

And himself. He stood just inside the door with his hands behind his back, which he only noticed once they were there.

Tor was in the bed.

He was sitting up against a slope of pillows that would have done for three, with the coverlet over his knees and the splinted leg lying out along the top of it like something he'd been asked to mind for a friend. There was a slate on his lap. There were copper squares laid out all down the coverlet in rows, and a stylus behind his ear, and another one in his hand, and ink on two of his fingers and the end of his nose. He had a borrowed nightshirt on, laced at the neck, and it was far too big for him, and his hair had dried at an angle. He looked about fifteen. He looked, Rolph thought, very much better than he had in Rolph's lap in the tanners' yard, with his mouth full of earth; and that was not saying a great deal.

He looked up. His whole face opened.

"Oh!" said Tor. "Oh, *hello*! Rich. Connie." He said both names carefully, the way he said a new sigil the first few times, to be sure of it. "Hello, everyone. Come in. Do come in." He tried to put the slate down and found nowhere to put it, and put it on his own chest, and held it there. "Mind the bird."

His father, who had been about to walk into a falcon, stopped.

Rolph looked at Smythe. *Rich*, in front of Smythe. But Tor hadn't seen him; Tor was looking at the family, the way he always looked at the family, as if they were the only people in a room; and Smythe, standing a little apart in his cream and yellow, had heard it, and his face did nothing at all.

There were three of them. Rolph had heard they'd been brought, and hadn't believed it, and here they were. One stood at the foot of the bed with its back to the room, its half-spread wings blocking most of the view of the door, like a large and disappointed governess. One had been set down square in front of the tall chest with the brass handles, so that nobody would be opening that chest again without help. The third was by the window, with a stripe of hot light from the shutters falling across its shield, and somebody (Rolph had a fair idea who) had hung a wet cloth over one of its wingtips to dry.

"Sorry," said Tor. "I had them moved. I wanted to feel each of them by itself, and they kept interfering. Not interfering. *Talking*. Sort of." He waved a hand, and a copper square slid off the coverlet onto the floor. "You'll want to step round that one, Rich."

The king stepped round it.

"Before anything," said Tor. "Please. I'm sorry. I've been wanting to ask since I woke up, only there wasn't anyone I could ask who'd know." He put the slate face down on the coverlet, which was a thing Rolph had seen him do perhaps twice in two years. "The children. From the yard. Simon, and the little girl. Are they all right?"

Rolph saw his mother's face soften, and saw her not let it.

"Both of them," said the king. His voice was the slow, mild one, the one he kept for horses and small grandnephews. "Perfectly well. The girl's a cut on her chin and a great deal to say about it. The boy's back helping his father already, and getting under everybody's feet." He paused. "It turns out the girl was a friend of his sister's. She fell in first. Nobody knew. The boy saw her go, and went in after her, and didn't call anyone, because he'd been told to mind her and thought he'd be in trouble for losing her down a midden." Another pause. "He got wedged at the bend."

"Oh," said Tor. "Oh, the poor *lamb*."

"His mother has sent you a pair of gloves," said the king. "They're on the table in the guardroom. Finely has looked inside them twice for knives and seems a little disappointed." He looked down at Tor from his great height, and Rolph watched him choose his words, which he did rarely and well. "People are very impressed."

"Well, so they should be!" said Tor, warmly.

Rolph's father blinked.

"I mean it," said Tor. He'd gone a little pink with the force of it. "Anyone grown would go down after a child. That's just what you do. You don't even think about it, you just go. But he's *seven*. Seven-year-olds don't go down holes after people. They go and get their mother, or they cry, or they hide in the woodshed till it's over. I hid in the woodshed when my brother fell in the millpond, and he only went in to his knees." He shook his head. "He ought to have something. A medal, or—do they do medals for seven? Somebody should tell his mother."

There was a short silence. The king looked at Tor. Tor looked back at the king with great earnestness and some ink on his nose.

Rolph laughed.

He hadn't meant to. It came up out of him before he could stop it, the way it did at school, and he got a hand over his mouth too late, and his mother gave him a look over her shoulder, and he turned it into a cough, badly. Karina had her head down. Varley was watching Tor with the expression she wore at the menagerie, in front of the big cats.

"Sorry," Rolph said. "Sorry. Something in my throat." He cleared it. "What are the birds *for*, Tor? You've got them stood about like you lost a bet with a statue."

"Oh!" said Tor. "Well. Where did they *come* from? That's what I'd like to know. Nobody would tell me. A sergeant brought them up on my plates, the poor things, three under each, and asked whether they'd do, and went away again."

"They stood on the old gate," said the king. "Before my great-grandfather raised the wall. Two hundred years, give or take, looking down the south road at people coming in." He considered the nearest one, the one with the wet cloth on its wing. "Since then they've been in a cellar under the east stair, being dusted. I gather somebody thought they'd been bored long enough."

That was all he said about it. Rolph knew his father; it was all he was ever going to say about it.

Tor didn't seem to need any more. He had turned to look at the falcon by the window, and his face had gone the way it went in front of new copper.

"They're to hold the river," he said. "For Ford. The new one. In the air. It can't just *be* there, you see, a line that long, it has to be held, it has to be fastened at the ends to something that stays still. So: one at the coast, there, where it starts, where it takes the water up." He laid a hand flat on the coverlet. "One at the head of the river, up in the hills, so the water comes down into it from above and fills it, and the level comes up all down the valley." The other hand, a long way off, on his own good knee. "And one in between. In the middle. To keep the field going the whole way, so it doesn't sag." He looked up. "That's three. That's why it's three."

"In bed," said Rolph's mother.

Tor looked at her.

"You mean to do this," said the queen, "from that bed. With that leg." She had let go of the king's arm. "Torrance. You were carried in here two nights ago on a door. Dr. Marsh has said that you are to rest, and I heard him say it, and I'm fairly sure he meant it, because he said it to me twice."

"Yes, ma'— Yes, Connie," said Tor.

"Rest."

"Yes, Connie."

"Rest is not," said his mother, "the same as river."

Tor looked down at his hands. Rolph knew that look. It was the one Tor wore when someone above him in a room said a thing he didn't agree with: he was being very still and very polite, so as not to disagree too loudly by accident.

"Only it's Ford, Connie," he said, to his hands. "They had the last of the corn in before the heat. And then nothing. There's three villages on the lower river drinking out of one well now, and the well's going brown; I heard it at the inn on the way up. It won't wait for my leg." He hesitated, and then looked up at her. "And I have to lie here anyway. I can't do anything else. And—it's only—when I'm working properly, all the way down, I don't really feel things. Anything. I don't feel *hungry*." He put his hand, without seeming to know he'd done it, on the bandage above his knee. "It'd be better than the drops, I think. The doctor's drops. They make the ceiling go round."

There was a silence in which Rolph watched his mother hear what had just been said: that the drops didn't work, and that he'd been lying there with the ceiling going round, and that he hadn't told anyone.

"You'll eat," she said at last. "When food is brought, you'll eat it."

"Yes, Connie."

"You will not stay down there for days."

"No, Connie," said Tor, who had stayed down there for four days in the spring and had to be fed bread by hand. Rolph had done the feeding. He kept his face very straight.

"And you will not," said his mother, "*strain* yourself. Promise me."

"I promise," said Tor, with enormous relief; and she sighed, and put her hand on the king's arm again, and that was that. Rolph, who had seen his mother lose perhaps four arguments in his life, three of them to his father, stood and watched her lose to a boy in a nightshirt, and could not decide whether she'd lost it or let it go. With his mother you often couldn't.

Something moved at the edge of the group.

Smythe of Westland had stepped forward a pace. He didn't do it to be noticed; he never did anything to be noticed. He bowed to Tor: not deep, not shallow, the bow of one man to another across a field. The light caught the top of his head, which was bald and brown as a nut, with a narrow ring of white hair round the back of it like frost on a hedge.

His mother saw it and turned. "Oh! I'm so sorry. Torrance, this is Smythe of Westland." A small pause, and Rolph heard her decide how much to say. "He has the army."

"All of it?" said Tor, before he could help it.

"Most days," said Smythe.

His voice was quite ordinary. That was the thing people always forgot about Smythe and were surprised by again each time: he had the kingdom's soldiers in his hand, all of them, from the frontier forts to the last recruit in the last guardroom, and he talked like a man buying rope. Rolph had once, at nine, been sent to fetch him from his rooms, and had found him in a room smaller than Rolph's own dressing closet, with a bed, a chair, a table covered in reports and a single candle, eating bread and cheese from a cloth. He had thought at the time that Smythe must be being punished for something. He'd since understood that Smythe simply didn't see what else a man needed, and that this was the most frightening thing about him.

"I'd be grateful for some of your time, Master Baker," said Smythe. "There are one or two things I'd like to ask you about. Privately, if you're willing. Later." The faintest shift at the corner of his mouth. "When you're not busy saving a county."

"Oh," said Tor. "Yes. Of course. Whenever you like, sir."

Smythe bowed again, briefly, and stepped back, and was at once part of the wall.

Rolph watched it arrive. He watched Tor look at the wall where Smythe had been, and then at the king, and then at the queen, and go slowly, from the collar of the borrowed nightshirt upward, the color of a radish: *Rich*, he was thinking; *Connie*; in front of the *army*.

"Your Majesty," said Tor, to the king, rather loudly. And to the queen, at once, so as not to leave her out: "Your Majesty."

"Torrance," said the king gravely.

Tor looked after him. Then he looked down at the coverlet and said, under his breath, so quietly that only Rolph, at the bedside, could have heard: "He can't *draft* people who can't walk. Can he?" A pause. "Run away. That's my battle cry. *Run away.* That'll go down well."

Rolph bit the inside of his cheek, hard.

"Tor," said Karina.

She had come round the foot of the bed, past the disappointed falcon, without anyone seeing her do it, which was a thing she'd been able to do since she was small. She stood by the bedpost with her hands clasped in front of her, and her eyes were very bright.

"I wanted to say," she said. "We all heard. Everyone's heard. About you going down into the—into the *hole*." She gave the word a little delicate pause, the pause of a princess who knew perfectly well what a midden was and was not going to say so in front of her mother. "It was terribly brave. Everyone says so."

"Oh," said Tor. "No. It wasn't. It was only that I was the smallest."

"And they say," said Karina, and leaned in, just a little, "that you went into a *rage*. A real one. A combat rage. Right there in front of everybody. Is it *true*?"

The room flinched.

Rolph felt it go through all of them at once, the way a stone goes into a pond: his mother's hand tightening on his father's sleeve; Smythe's eyes coming up off the floor; his own shoulders going up round his ears before he could stop them. You didn't. You simply didn't. You could talk about a man's debts at table, at a pinch, or his wife, if you were very sure of your company. You did not ask a man to his face whether he had lost himself, because a rage was the one thing in the world a man couldn't help and everyone was ashamed of, and asking was like asking whether he'd wet himself.

Varley hit her.

Not hard. It was a sort of slap at Karina's elbow, with the flat of her hand, the kind she'd been giving her sister in the backs of carriages for years. "*Karina!*" she hissed. "You can't *ask* people that!"

"I'm only asking—"

"You *can't*! You can't just—Mama, tell her."

"I think," said their mother, "she's been told."

Tor was looking from one of them to the other with total bewilderment.

"A rage?" he said. "Me?" He looked at Rolph for help. "Is that what they're saying? Already? It was only yesterday. The day before. It's already—that's what happens, isn't it, with a story, at home it was the same, Mister Pruett's pig got out once and by the next market day it had eaten a child." He shook his head. "No. It wasn't anything like that. I didn't do anything, really. I only did a bit of magic. At the end."

"A bit of magic," said Rolph.

He heard his own voice come out flat, and didn't fix it.

"Tor," he said. "You had a shield up."

"No, I didn't. I'd thrown mine. To the girl."

"I *know* you'd thrown yours. It took a strip of your ear with it; I saw the ear. You didn't have a shield on you. You didn't have a *button* on you; you were in your drawers. And when they dropped you out of that hole, there was muck coming down all round you off the sides like rain off a roof, and none of it was touching you. I saw it bounce." He found he'd taken a step closer to the bed. "And then you sat up and put your hand out, and I felt it. Through the ground. Through my *boots*. Like standing next to the oven door when somebody opens it." He swallowed. "I know what that feels like, Tor. From the inside. That's why I told everyone to run."

"Screamed," said Karina, helpfully.

"*Told everyone to run*," said Rolph. "And then the whole pit went up in the air, all of it, twenty feet across, and came down everywhere, and the girl came out of the middle of it, and I've never in my life seen a shield do that. Nobody has." He stopped. "So."

Tor had gone quiet. He was looking at his hands on the coverlet, the inky fingers, and turning one of the copper squares over and over.

"I was a bit upset," he said at last. "With the dirt. I'll grant that." He turned the square over again. "It was being very—it just kept *coming*. And she was under it. And it didn't care. So I was cross with it. A bit. Maybe." He looked up, and his face was entirely reasonable. "But it can't have been a rage, Rolph. Not a real one. That's a noble thing. That's in the blood; everybody knows that. People like me don't get them. We haven't got the—whatever it is. We get *cross*."

Nobody answered him.

Rolph stood by the bed and felt the thing he'd felt through the soles of his boots in the tanners' yard, the heat coming up out of the ground and the sense of something enormous gathering itself, and set it beside the small, reasonable face on the pillows, and they would not go together. He looked away from Tor, for something else to look at, and found that Smythe had stopped being part of the wall and was looking at Tor too, with no expression at all; and beyond Smythe, his father.

His father laughed.

It was the real one. It came up from somewhere deep in that great chest and filled the room, and Varley jumped, and the falcons, Rolph would have sworn, rang very faintly with it.

"Don't worry about it," said the king. "Don't worry about any of it. You're in my house and you'll get well in it." He looked round at the birds, and at the copper on the coverlet, and back at Tor. "Anything you need, for the river or for yourself, ask. Ask me, or ask Finely, or ask the man on the door, and it'll come. I'll make sure they know." He put out one enormous hand, and after a moment Tor understood he was meant to shake it, and did, his own hand vanishing to the wrist.

"Thank you, Your Majesty," said Tor, with a glance past him at the wall.

"Smythe," said the king.

"Sir," said Smythe.

They went out together, the king ducking again under the lintel he didn't need to duck under. Hale's heel hit the flags outside like a dropped plank.

* * *

His mother didn't go.

Rolph had thought she would. She went as far as the window, and turned one of the shutter slats to look at the light, and stayed there with her back to the room in a way he'd seen before and didn't trust. Karina didn't go either. Karina had recovered completely. She came round the bedpost and sat down on the very edge of the bed, by Tor's good knee, arranging her skirt, as if she'd been invited.

"You mustn't mind Varley," she said. "She's very young."

"I'm *sixteen*," said Varley.

"She's very young," said Karina, "and she's never met anyone who's been down a hole." She tipped her head and looked at Tor through her lashes. "Does it hurt dreadfully?"

"Not dreadfully," said Tor, who had pulled the coverlet up to the middle of his chest without seeming to notice he'd done it. "Only some."

"You poor thing. You're terribly brave about it." She leaned over and moved one of his copper squares a quarter of an inch, for no reason at all. "I could sit with you. While you work. I could read to you. Alphie says you like to be read to."

"I never said that," said Rolph.

"He said you fall asleep to it, which is the same."

"I'll bring you the ice," said Varley suddenly. She'd come up on Tor's other side, red to the ears, but she'd come. "From the cellars. In a bowl. For your leg. Nobody'll think to, they'll just bring broth. And I know where the good pillows are, the ones with the down, not those." She looked disdainfully at the slope of pillows behind Tor's head, which Rolph would have said were the best pillows in the palace. "I'll bring you those."

"I was going to bring him pillows," said Karina.

"You were going to *read* to him."

"I can do both."

"Thank you," said Tor, looking from one to the other like a man watching tennis, with his coverlet at his chin. "Thank you, both of you. Really. That's very kind. I don't need pillows. I've got a great many pillows. I'm sorry."

"He's *sorry*," said Karina to Varley, delighted. "Isn't he lovely?"

"Kari," said Rolph.

"Your ears have gone, Alphie."

"Kari. He's *hurt*."

"I know. That's why I'm sitting with him." She smiled at Tor. "Isn't it?"

"I," said Tor, "think I have some ink to mix."

Rolph put his hand over his face. He could feel exactly how red it was through his palm. He stood there in the middle of that long cool room, among the falcons, with his sisters on either side of his best friend's bed like a pair of very well-dressed vultures, and his best friend holding his coverlet up to his chin and apologizing for having enough pillows; and he wished, quite sincerely, for the floor.

At the window, his mother laughed.

It was a small, warm, entirely unsympathetic laugh, and she didn't trouble to hide it.

"Oh, darling," said the queen. "*That's* what you get for bringing someone home."

"Mother."

"I did warn you."

"You did *not* warn me."

"Didn't I?" She came away from the window. "Then I'm warning you now. Girls. That's quite enough. He has a river to build, and you've had a far better morning than you deserve." She put a hand on Karina's shoulder, and Karina, sighing, rose; she put a hand on Varley's, and Varley went the color of a radish and was steered toward the door. On the way past the bed the queen stopped, and bent, and with one finger, very lightly, wiped the ink off the end of Tor's nose.

"Eat," she said.

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"He's gone, dear," said the queen.

Tor looked at the door, where Smythe was not. "Yes, Connie," he said.

Karina put her head back round the door when the rest of them were already in the corridor. "I'll come and read later," she said, and was gone before anyone could answer.

Rolph stayed where he was.

Tor let the coverlet down, slowly, an inch at a time, as if it might be needed again. He looked at the door. He looked at Rolph.

"Your sisters," he said carefully, "are very kind."

"They're *awful*," said Rolph, into his hand.

"They're not. They're—" Tor thought about it. "Very kind. And quite fast." He picked up his slate, and turned it over, and looked at what was on it as if he'd been away from it a long time, and his shoulders came down. "Rolph. Do you think he really wants to draft me?"

"Smythe? No."

"Are you sure?"

"Tor," said Rolph, "if Smythe wanted you in the army, you'd be in the army. He wouldn't ask. He'd just tell you, and you'd go, and you'd find out you didn't mind." He came and sat down in the rose chair, heavily, and stretched his legs out among the falcons. "He wants to *ask* you something. That's all. He only ever says what he means."

"Oh," said Tor. "Good." He frowned at the slate. "What, though?"

Rolph didn't answer. He looked at the falcon at the foot of the bed, with its back to them, and its wings half spread over a shield the weather had worn smooth, and the place at the tip of its beak where two hundred years of hands had rubbed it gold; and thought about the oven door opening, and the ground coming up, and the reasonable little voice saying *we get cross*.

After a while Tor's stylus began to move. After a while longer, Rolph found a book on the floor under the chair, one of Tor's, with a burn on the cover, and opened it, and didn't read it.
