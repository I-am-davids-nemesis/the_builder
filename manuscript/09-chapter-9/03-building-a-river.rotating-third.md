On the first morning Master Tor asked for the birds to be brought closer, and it took four footmen and most of the first hour.

He wanted them round the chair. Not near it: round it, close, the three of them in a ring with their backs to the room and their wings half open over him, so that from the door, when it was done, Hale could see nothing of him at all but the splinted leg sticking out between two bronze tails, propped on its stool. It looked like a hen house. It looked like a hen house built by someone who had been told about hens and never seen one.

"Thank you," said the voice from inside it. "That's perfect. I'm sorry about the floor."

The floor had a long pale scratch across it now, from the second falcon, which had come off its plates for a moment by the bed. Hale had heard the steward's man draw breath at the sound from all the way down the corridor.

"Will you want anything, sir?" he said.

"No. Thank you. I'll just—" A pause. "I'll just start, I think."

Hale went back to his place, and Gerry looked at him, and he looked at the window at the end of the corridor, and that was the morning.

He looked in at the turn of each hour, which wasn't in his orders and which nobody had told him not to do. At the first look, Master Tor had a slate on his good knee and was drawing on it, quick and small, rubbing out with the side of his hand. At the second, the slate was on the floor face down and both his hands were flat on the falcon in front of him, low down on its breast, above the shield. At the third, the hands were still there, and he was talking to it, too low to hear.

"Sir," said Hale, at the noon bell. "There's your dinner."

There was a pause long enough to count in. Then: "Oh! Sorry. Yes. Thank you. Sorry." And a hand came out between the wings and took the bowl, and the bowl went in.

It came out again later, a third eaten. When he shifted the leg on the stool, which he did now and again without seeming to know he did it, the breath went out of him in a small hiss, and he said "Sorry" to nobody, and the hands went back to the bronze.

His Highness came at the evening bell. Hale gave him the full salute, and got a vague wave for it, and the prince put his head in at the door and said, "Tor? Have you eaten? Mother wants to know. I'm to say you're to say yes."

"Rolph!" said the hen house. "Hello! Yes. Some. Come and see, it's going to be— no, don't, sorry, you'll have to stand there, I've got them just so. It's going *well*, I think. It's very quiet in here."

"It's very quiet everywhere, you've got three bloody great birds round your ears." The prince stayed in the doorway with his shoulder against the frame and talked for a quarter of an hour, about nothing much, and the voice in the middle of the falcons answered him every time; only, Hale noticed, every time a little later than the time before.

* * *

The girl had been taking the trays up since the second night, because she was the one who'd brought the jug, and nobody else wanted to.

It wasn't that he was frightening. She'd been frightened for the whole of the first day, waiting for him to be, and then he'd smiled at her over the cup and said thank you as if she'd brought him a horse, and she'd run, and been ashamed of running for two days after. It was the room. She didn't like going into the room. She'd been in and out of grand rooms since she was eleven, with sheets and with trays and with the little brush for the corners, and she'd never once been in one that felt as if it were holding its breath.

She took the soup in at the noon bell on the second day. He was where he'd been, in the chair, inside the birds, and she had to go sideways between two wingtips to reach him, holding the tray up, and stand there at his elbow until he noticed her.

"Oh," he said. "Thank you."

He took the bowl in both hands. He had black under his nails that wasn't dirt, and a line of dried ink across one cheekbone that she'd have dearly liked to get at with a cloth.

"Thank you," he said again, a while later, to the soup. She didn't think he knew he'd said it the first time.

She stood. It was the steward's rule that the bowl came back with the tray, and the bowl was in his hands, and he was looking past her at the bird. Not at it. Into it, she'd have said, if she'd had to say; the way her gran looked into the fire. After a time he remembered the spoon. She watched him eat the whole bowl that way, one spoon and then a long wait and then another, and she'd have liked to sit down and couldn't.

The prince came while she was there. He said "Tor," from the doorway, and "Tor," and Master Tor said "Mm," and "Yes," and once, a long while after the prince had asked him anything, "No, it's quite warm, thank you." His Highness looked at her across the wings, and she looked at the floor, and he went away.

It was in the evening, when she went in for the tray, that she noticed he'd stopped wincing.

She'd been watching for it without knowing she was. He'd done it all day yesterday and all the morning, every time he moved the bad leg: a little catch, a little hiss, sorry. Now he moved it, she saw him move it, he drew it right off the stool and put it back, and there was nothing. Not a flicker. His face stayed exactly as it was, which was nowhere.

On her way out she told Master Hale, who was on the door again, because he always seemed to be. He looked in at the leg for a long moment, and then at her, and didn't say anything, and she went down to the kitchens with the tray feeling she'd told on someone.

* * *

On the third day Master Tor stopped seeing them.

Hale didn't notice it all at once. He went in at noon with the bowl, the way the girl did, sideways between the wings, and stood at the boy's elbow and said "Sir," and waited; and said "Sir"; and after a while understood that he could stand there till the night bell. The eyes were open. They were on the bronze. They didn't move to him, and when he put the bowl into Master Tor's hands, the hands held it, and went on holding it, the way a shelf would.

So he fed him.

He'd fed men before. Not often, and not in a palace. He took the spoon and filled it and put it to the boy's mouth, and the mouth opened, and closed, and swallowed, and the eyes never once came off the bird. It was like feeding a fledgling. It was like feeding a fledgling that was thinking very hard about something else.

At the second bell after noon, the girl came running for him, white, because Master Tor had got up.

He'd got up out of the chair on his one leg and stood there, holding a wing, in the middle of the birds; and when Hale came in, he didn't turn. Hale went to him and took his arm, and he came. That was the strange part. He walked, or hopped, wherever Hale steered him, down the corridor and in at the second door and out again and back, and when Hale turned him at the chair he sat, and when Hale let go of him his hands went straight back to the bronze, flat, as if they'd been waiting there on their own.

"It's like he's gone somewhere," the girl whispered at the door.

"He's working," said Hale, who didn't know.

There was a smell in the room that afternoon. Hot metal, Hale would have called it, like a forge yard on a still day, sharp at the back of the nose. But he'd put his hand on the nearest falcon, going past, and the bronze was cool as a cellar step.

His Highness came at the evening bell, as he did. He said "Tor," at the door, and came in when there was no answer, and went sideways between the wings the way the rest of them did now, without being told. He talked to him. Hale couldn't hear what. Then he stopped talking and sat down on the floor, there among the birds, with his back against a plinth and his arms on his knees, and stayed like that till the light went, saying nothing, while Master Tor looked at the bronze over his head.

When he came out, he had his face arranged. He looked at Hale, and Hale looked at the corridor.

"He knows you're there, Highness," Hale said. He didn't know that either.

"No," said the prince. "He doesn't." He looked back through the door. "He's fine. He does this. He's done it before." And then, as if it had been dragged out of him, "Not this long."

He came back the next evening, and the evening after. He never got an answer again, and he never once didn't come.

* * *

By the fourth day it was ordinary, which the girl thought was the worst thing about it.

They did him the way you did a room. Bowl in at noon and spoon it; the walk at the second bell; bowl in at evening and spoon it; the walk. Master Hale on the arm, her on the other side in case, round the wingtips, mind the stool. Nobody said *sir* any more, because there was no point. The page brought fresh water twice a day and set it inside the door and left as fast as the page was able. A footman came in with a broom and swept carefully round the chair as if it were a fixed thing, a stove, and went out again. She caught herself, once, dusting the bird by the window, and stopped, and felt peculiar.

The room had changed. She couldn't have said how. The light came through the slats as it always had, and the cooling plate on the wall hummed to itself, and the birds stood as they'd stood. But the air near them was thick. It was like the air in the yard behind the kitchens before a storm, close and heavy, the kind that made the hair on your arms stand up and the dairy girls snappish. Only it never broke. She'd go in thick and come out thick, and the sky outside the shutters stayed a hard blue all day, without a cloud in it.

The prince came at the evening bell and sat on the floor.

Master Tor didn't see him.

She took the tray out, and went down three flights, and sat on the bottom stair for a minute before she went into the kitchens, which she had never done before in her life.

* * *

There was the line.

There had been the room, at some point, and a ceiling with a sky on it; there had been soup. They'd gone. There were the three of them, and the line between them, and nothing else that he needed.

The bronze was old. It had been poured once, all in a piece, and then it had stood two hundred years in the wind looking down a road, and it had settled into itself the way an old house settles: everything leaning on everything, nothing loose. He could feel where the cold had been in it, winters ago, and where the sun had lain. He didn't need that. He let it go by. He needed the stillness, and the stillness was all through it, deep, all the way down to the feet, and he could lay a field into stillness like that the way you lay a stone into mortar: once, and it stays.

So: once.

The first one was the long pull, the one that had to reach. It lifted, at the far end. It had to take up more than he'd ever asked of anything, and keep taking it, day after day, and leave the salt behind, and not get tired. He'd made it the night before. Or the day before. He'd made it, and it was sitting in the bird with its edges loose, waiting. He took the edges and turned them in. He pressed them down. He went round it, all the way round, and pressed, and pressed, until there wasn't an edge anywhere, until it was one thing and the bird was one thing and there was no telling where the one stopped.

Then the second. The second only had to hold, but it had to hold in both directions at once, back to the first and on to the third, and it had to do it across a distance he could feel the shape of but not the size, and it had to not sag. He'd worried about the sag. He remembered worrying. He couldn't remember why it had seemed hard; it was quite easy, once you saw that it wanted to be a bridge and not a rope. He let it be a bridge. He locked it.

The third was the one that put the water down. It had to be gentle. It had to be the gentlest thing he'd ever made, in a way, because all the rest of it would come pouring at it from a long way off, and it had to take all that and set it down in the river without hurrying, the way you'd set down a full bowl. He was a long time on the third. He went over it and over it. He found a place where it would have been rough, if you'd asked it for too much at once, and he smoothed it, and found another, and smoothed that.

He tried them together.

They spoke to each other. Across the room, across the hand's breadth of air between the wingtips, they found each other and took hold, and pulled, very slightly, the way three people pull on a sheet to fold it, and the line went taut between them, and hummed.

It was right.

He went round once more anyway, all three, pressing. There was a sound, somewhere, a long way off, deep, like a bell. He let it go by. He went round again.

* * *

On the seventh morning, Master Tor fell asleep.

Hale had come in with the bowl at the noon bell, and the girl behind him, and there he was: in the chair, among the birds, with his head gone over sideways onto a bronze wing and his mouth open, snoring. His hands had come off the falcon at last. They lay in his lap, palms up, like something set down and forgotten.

The air was clear. Hale noticed it as he would have noticed a door shut somewhere that had been banging for days. He hadn't known how much he'd been noticing it.

They didn't move him. The girl wanted to, and Hale said leave him, and they left him. He slept through the noon bell, and the one after, and the one after that, folded up in the chair with the splint stuck out; and at the fourth bell he woke up all at once, sat straight, said "*Food*," quite loudly, to no one, and looked round in astonishment at Hale in the doorway.

"Oh," he said. "Hello. Sorry. Is there any food?"

There was. The kitchen had been sending up a bowl at every meal for six days and getting it back a third eaten, and Hale had a word with the page, and the page had a word with the kitchen, and what came up the stair, in the end, was a tray with two loaves on it, a cold fowl, a crock of butter, a bowl of the soup, a bowl of something with plums in it, and a jug of milk; and Master Tor ate all of it. Hale watched him do it. He ate in a kind of quiet, steady, total way, like a man shoveling, and when it was gone he looked at the tray, and then up at Hale, and went pink.

"I'm sorry," he said. "That was a lot. Was that a lot? I'm really very hungry. I don't know why."

"Six days, sir," said Hale.

Master Tor looked at him blankly. Then he looked at the shutters, and the light through them, and then down at himself, at the shirt, which had been a good deal cleaner when he'd last looked at it.

"Oh," he said. "*Oh*. Was it?" He thought about it. "That's quite a long time."

Then he fell asleep again, sitting up, with a plum stone in his hand.

His Highness came at the evening bell, and stopped in the doorway, and looked at the empty tray and the chair and the boy asleep in it; and his shoulders, which he'd been carrying up round his ears for four days like a man walking in the rain, came down. He didn't sit on the floor. He went and stood beside the chair and looked down at Master Tor with an expression that Hale, from the door, decided wasn't for him, and looked away from.

Master Tor opened his eyes.

"Rolph!" he said. "Oh, hello. When did you get here?"

There was a pause.

"Every day," said the prince. "For a week."

* * *

They came for the birds on the evening of the seventh day, after he'd slept again and eaten again and slept: a dozen of the royal guard in full kit, under a captain Hale knew by sight and stood up straighter for, and with them four riggers out of the river yards, big quiet men in leather aprons who had moved church bells and millstones and once, the oldest of them said, a whole organ, and who stood in the door of that long room and looked at the three falcons the way Hale's father had looked at a horse.

Master Tor had them all in. He'd had Hale turn the chair round to face the room, and he sat in it with the leg up and a slate on his knee, washed out and a bit hollow under the eyes, but himself; and he told them.

"The first one goes up the river," he said. "Right up. As far up as you can, as long as there's still river under it. Higher than the last farm, so every farm gets some. Everyone below it can take off what they need for their ditches, and the rest goes down." He drew a line on the slate. "The second one, halfway. Halfway between the first one and the sea. It doesn't matter exactly where, it only has to be roughly in the middle. That's the one that keeps it up. It holds it. So the water doesn't go wandering off, or come down in the wrong place on somebody's barn."

The captain wrote that down. Hale saw him write *barn*.

"And the third one by the sea. As near as it'll go." Master Tor looked at the riggers. "As near as you can get it without the sea coming and taking it. I don't know how near that is. You'll know better than me."

"Tide line's the trouble," said the oldest rigger, after a moment. "You put that much weight on sand, below the wrack, she'll sink to her knees by spring. And then there's folk." He looked at the falcon. "Lot of folk on a beach."

"There's the sea fort," said the captain.

They all looked at him.

"On the point. Below Ford, where the road comes down." The captain had a finger on the slate now, a long way to the left of Master Tor's line. "It's on rock. The wall runs right to the water; they built it so. There's a regiment in it that's had nothing to do since my grandfather's day but polish its pikes." He looked up. "Put her on the sea wall. Nobody'll lay a hand on her there."

"Oh," said Master Tor. "*Oh*. Yes. That's much better than anything I'd have thought of. Thank you." He wrote *fort* on the slate, and underlined it, and looked as pleased as if he'd been given it. "And they could turn it off, couldn't they. If it rains."

"Turn it off, sir?"

"If the weather turns. When the rains come. You'd not want it running then, you'd have a flood. It's like a dryer. A food dryer, or a clothes dryer, you've seen those? You just tap it. Tap it to start it, tap it again to stop." He looked round at them, encouraging. "That's all. You tap it."

There was a silence of the kind that comes when a dozen grown men have all been told the same simple thing and each of them is waiting for one of the others to ask.

"Tap it where, sir," said the captain.

"Anywhere."

The silence came back.

"Anywhere at all," said Master Tor. "It doesn't matter. It's the whole bird. You could tap it on the tail." He saw their faces, and Hale watched him see them; he looked from the captain to the riggers and back, and something kind and a little helpless came into his own. "Well," he said. "The shield, then. In the middle of the shield, there, where the arms were. Tap it there."

The captain wrote it down, with visible relief. *Middle of shield.*

"Gently," said Master Tor. "It's very old."

They took the birds out on the plates, the same way they'd come in, the footmen steering and the riggers walking alongside with their big hands flat on the bronze. The first went through the door with a hand's breadth to spare. The second scraped. The third went through like a cork out of a bottle; and then the room was only a room again, very long and very empty, with a pale scratch across the floor and three square marks in the dust where the plinths had stood and a chair in the middle of them, with a boy in it, looking at the door.

"Good luck," he said, to nobody in particular, when they'd gone. Then, quieter, to himself, "Please don't flood."

* * *

It took them three days.

Hale heard the most of it at second hand, the way you heard anything in the palace, from the guardroom, from the stair: that the plates had carried the birds down to the river stairs and onto a barge; that the riggers had sworn the whole way to the first landing because the plates wanted to ride level and the barge didn't; that the first falcon had gone up into the hills on a wagon bed, floating an inch above the boards, with six of the guard riding round it and the farm people coming out of their houses to stare. That the captain had set it where the river came out of a gorge, on a shelf of rock over the water, and stood there with his helmet off and tapped it in the middle of the shield, gently, and nothing whatever had happened.

That was the first day's news, and Master Tor, told it by Gerry, went quite white and said, "No, that's right, that's all right, it's waiting for the others," and asked for his slate, and didn't eat his dinner.

On the evening of the tenth day, Sergeant Finely came up the corridor.

Hale heard him before he saw him, the way he always did: a long, level, unhurried step on the flags. Finely had been gone the three days with the party, and he looked it. There was dust in the creases of his face and dust in the knot of his rank, and his boots had been somewhere that wasn't a palace. He stopped at the door, and nodded to Hale, and went in.

Master Tor was in the bed. The prince was in the rose chair with his feet up on the footstool that was meant for the leg, reading. They both looked round.

"Sir," said Finely. "Highness."

"Is it—" said Master Tor, and stopped, and had to start again. "Did it—"

"All three are set, sir," said Finely. "The first above the gorge, the second at Halloway crossing, the third on the sea wall at the fort. The colonel there sends his compliments, and says it's the first time in twenty years anything's been put on his wall that he didn't have to paint." He paused. "We tapped the last one this morning at sunup. In the middle of the shield."

"Gently?"

"Very gently, sir."

"And?"

Finely considered. Hale had seen him consider a great many things, in a great many years, and he'd never once seen what was going on behind it.

"It goes up off the sea like smoke," said Finely, "the whole width of a road. You can see the sun through it. And then it goes inland, high, higher than the gulls, and you can follow it along the sky by eye. And at the gorge it comes down." He stopped. "It comes down like a waterfall that hasn't got a cliff, sir. Into the river. I tasted it. It's sweet. There's no salt in it at all."

The prince had put his book down.

"And the river," said Master Tor. He had both hands round the edge of the coverlet. "Is the river all right? Is it too much? Is there flooding?"

"No, sir. It was low. It was very low. It's come up to a bit over what the ferryman at Halloway calls proper, and stopped there, and he's been out on his landing all day looking at it."

"No flooding."

"No, sir."

"None at all? Not at the bottom? Not where it goes into the—"

"None, sir. I rode the length of it."

Master Tor let go of the coverlet. He lay back, very slowly, onto the pillows, and looked up at the painted sky, and the slant of birds across it, and the crack in the corner with the wrong blue on it; and he let his breath out, all of it, in one long go, as if he'd been holding it for ten days.

"Oh, good," he said, to the ceiling. "Oh, that's good. Thank you." And then, after a moment, lifting his head: "Sorry. You did say no flooding."

"I did, sir."

"Right. Good. Thank you."

The prince had his hand over his mouth.

Finely stood a moment longer at the foot of the bed, dusty and level and long, looking down at the boy in it; and then his nose moved, very slightly, the way it moved at the end of an inspection, when he'd found the one thing.

"If I may, sir," he said. "Will you be wanting a bath?"

Master Tor looked at him. Then he looked down at himself, at the shirt that had been white ten days ago, and lifted one arm a cautious inch, and put it down again at once.

"Oh, *no*," he said. "Oh, I'm so sorry. Yes. Please. Yes."
