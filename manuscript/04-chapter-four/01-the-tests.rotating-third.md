On the first morning, the boy was at the building master's door before the building master was.

Wensa had allowed for a number of things. She had allowed for him to run: the Hollin road, the wood below the sheep field, the long way home to wherever he said home was. She had allowed for him to bar himself in his room, and for him to go looking for someone, a contact in the town, a letter left under a stone. She had allowed, at the far end of the list, for him to come for her.

She had not allowed for him to be standing in the north corridor at a quarter before the sixth bell, washed and combed, satchel on his shoulder, hands folded in front of him, waiting for a class.

She took a place at the far end, by the window, where the light would be behind her, and opened her ledger.

He saw her at once. He turned his head as she arrived, the way a man turns toward a sound he has been listening for all night, and for a moment his face did nothing at all. Then he bowed. It was not a student's duck of the head. It was a bow from the waist, the kind they taught to pages.

"Good morning, Mistress Wensa," he said, down the length of the corridor.

She inclined her head.

He faced the door again, and he did not look at her for eleven minutes. She knew it was eleven because she counted. He didn't fidget, or look at the stair, or at his hands. He stood the way a man stands in a queue for bread when he expects at any moment to be told there is none left.

The building master came up the stair at the sixth bell exactly, and stopped, and looked at Baker, and at her, and at Baker again.

"You're early," he said.

"Yes, sir," said Baker. "You like us early."

The building master appeared to consider whether this was true. Then he found his key. Baker held the door for him, and before he let it close he looked back down the corridor at her. It was not a glance. It was a look, steady and complete, as though he were fixing her position.

She wrote nothing down.

* * *

The ledger was bound in brown calf and ruled in red, and after the hilt at her belt it was the most useful thing she owned. A woman with a ledger can stand anywhere. She can stand in a corridor, or a doorway, or at the end of a yard, and anyone who sees her will assume she is waiting for someone to bring her a figure. Wensa had carried one like it for nineteen years, and in all that time she had not once been asked what she was writing.

She was not writing anything. There was nothing to write.

He kept the routine. He kept it the way recruits keep it in their first month, when they are still frightened of the sergeant: completely, and a little early. The building master at the sixth bell. Meditation at the seventh, in the long quiet room under the eaves, which was the one room in the school she could not follow him into, because anyone who sat down in it was expected to close her eyes. She waited on the bench outside. On the second morning the meditation mistress came out at the end of the hour, found her there, looked at her for some time without saying anything, and went back in. Theory at the ninth. Lunch at the noon bell. Combat in the afternoon, with Kolb. Supper. The library until the tenth bell, and then his room, and the bar dropped behind the door.

He was early for all of it. On the third day the theory master told her in the staff room, without being asked, that Baker had handed in an essay four days before it was due and wanted to know whether that was allowed.

She ate when he ate. She took the end of the next table, with her back to the wall, where she could see him and both doors. He greeted her at every meal. "Good morning, Mistress Wensa." "Good afternoon, Mistress Wensa." On the second day he got up halfway through his porridge, crossed to her table and put the salt down by her hand, because she hadn't any. He went back to his seat before she could decide what to say about it.

She noted the courtesy. She had met men who were that polite, and they had all been trained to it, or had wanted something very badly, or both.

The roommate sat with him at every meal. On the second day the roommate sat facing her instead of facing Baker, and looked at her over his porridge for a long time, steadily, without expression. She returned the look as she had returned all of his looks for two years, briefly, and then let her eyes go past him to the door. He didn't look away first. That was new. She noted it. She noted, as well, that he had taken the eggs again and nothing green, and that he had not finished his milk.

* * *

The tests began on the third day.

She had expected them. She had asked the storekeeper, the evening before, what Baker had bought, and the storekeeper had read it out to her from his book with the air of a man who had never in his life been asked to read out a student's purchases to a member of staff and hoped not to be asked again. Copper, twenty squares, the thin gauge. Acid. A pot of resist. Candles, two dozen, tallow. A bag of straw.

"Straw," said Wensa.

"For the smell, he said, mistress. He said it smokes nicely when it's damp."

She considered what could be done with straw. It was not a long list.

He did the first of it in the practice yard, after combat, while the others were still putting the wasters back on the racks. The thaw had come in the week before, and the sand was dark with it, and the water butt at the corner was full to the brim and running over. She sat on the bench beside it, with her ledger on her knee, and he looked across at her once, bowed slightly from where he stood, and then went and hung three copper squares on cords from the arm of the pell.

Kolb came over, wiping his hands.

"You've got a shadow, Baker."

"Yes, sir."

Kolb looked across the yard at her. She looked back. He grinned, which he did rarely, and turned to the pell.

"Which one?"

"The middle one, please, sir. The left one's no good, I don't think. But I'd like to know how it's no good."

Kolb unhooked the hilt from his belt. The blade was simply there, the way it always was: four feet of dull gray, there and not there, and the light didn't seem to know what to do with it. Wensa felt her own hand go to her belt and made it lie flat on the ledger instead.

"Turn it," the boy was saying, half to himself. "Don't stop it. You can't stop a line. Turn it."

Kolb cut.

The blade met something a hand's breadth from the copper. It did not stop. It slid, the way a knife slides off a frozen loaf, along a curve nobody could see, and went on past the square and took the top six inches off the pell instead. The wood fell into the wet sand with a soft sound. The square swung on its cord, unmarked.

Kolb looked at the pell for a while.

"Huh," he said.

"I'm sorry about the pell, sir."

"Got more pells." Kolb weighed the hilt in his hand. "It went somewhere, though. Didn't it. Stand next to that and you'd lose an ear."

"Yes, sir. That's what I thought. I'll have to think where it goes."

Wensa had not moved.

She had seen a cutter used on a man once, at a door, a long time ago. There was no armor for it. There was nothing at all that could be put between a cutter and the person behind you, except yourself, which was what a guard was for, and had always been.

She looked at the copper square, swinging, catching the light.

"Again, sir? The middle one?"

"Stand back, then."

* * *

On the fifth day the square stopped it.

The blade came down and met the field, and stayed there. It did not slide, or skid. It simply stopped, a hand from the copper, as if it had come to the end of itself. Kolb leaned on it. Kolb put his other hand on the hilt and leaned on it with his whole weight, and his boots went back an inch in the sand, and the square hung in front of him, turning a little on its cord, perfectly calm.

He let the blade go. He looked at Baker.

"Huh," he said again, in quite a different voice.

They did force after that, with the waster and then with the iron bar, and Kolb was not careful with either. The yard rang with it. Twice the pell jumped in its socket. On the ninth or tenth blow from the bar, one of the squares split, with a high clean sound like a dropped plate, straight across the middle; the field went out of it at once, and the next blow knocked both halves into the sand.

Baker went and picked them up, and turned them over in his hands, and Wensa watched him say something to them, quietly. Then he took a stub of pencil out of his tunic and wrote on his wrist.

"Too thin," he told Kolb. "It's like a crust. The filling's fine, but the crust has to hold the shape. I'll go up a gauge."

Kolb looked at him for a moment as though he might say something about crusts. Then he put the bar back on the rack.

* * *

The fire was at night.

Baker had a workroom on the east stair, one of the small ones, booked under his name from the ninth bell to the twelfth. She stood in the doorway at the eleventh. He was on the stool at the bench, with the brazier lit beside him and a square on a cord round his neck, and when she appeared he got up so quickly that the stool went over.

"Good evening, Mistress Wensa." He picked the stool up. "Would you like this? It's quite safe. I've checked it."

"No."

"I'm only doing fire, ma'am. And then smoke. You might like to stand back a little, for the smoke." He looked at the brazier, and at her, and then, with great care, as if he were offering her the better seat at a table: "Or you could watch from the corridor. It's nearer the window."

She stayed in the doorway.

He began with a candle, held under a square on the bench. The flame bent away from the copper, and leaned, and lay down sideways along nothing, as if a wind were blowing that didn't move the air in the rest of the room. Then the brazier. He touched the square at his throat, and sat down next to the coals, close, closer than anyone would sit, and held his hands out over them as if they were a fire on a cold night at home, and the coals glowed, and he did not so much as squint. After a minute he took a pair of tongs and laid a coal on his own knee. It sat in the air a hand above his trousers, smoking slightly, and he looked at it with a kind of tender interest.

Then he put the straw on.

It was damp, as advertised. It smoked at once, thick and yellow-white, and the smoke rolled up out of the brazier and filled the little room from the ceiling down in less time than it took her to step back. Her eyes ran. She backed into the corridor with her sleeve over her mouth and stood there, coughing, and looked in.

He was sitting in the middle of it, inside a clear space the shape of an egg.

The smoke lay against the outside of it, thick as fleece. It folded round the curve and clung there, and inside, in the clear, the boy sat on his stool with his hands on his knees, breathing. Slowly, in and out. Wensa watched him do it four times. Then he laughed, a short surprised sound she had never heard from him, and said to nobody, "Oh. Oh, that's *it*. The air's smaller. Of course it is."

She coughed.

He looked round at the sound, and saw her in the corridor with her eyes streaming, and was on his feet at once, apologizing, wading out through the smoke with the egg moving round him, so that the smoke parted ahead of him and closed up behind. He got the window open and stood there flapping at it with his satchel, saying he was sorry, he was so sorry, he should have thought, he should have *said*, until the smoke had thinned enough to see the far wall.

"I did warn you, ma'am," he said, at the end of it. "About the smoke." He seemed to feel this made it worse.

* * *

The wax was the last.

It was the seventh night, or the eighth; she had them very clear in her head and it didn't matter. He had a fresh square on the bench, the heavier gauge, and a fat candle burning, and he held the candle over the square, tilted it, and let it drip.

The drops fell, and struck the field, and didn't spread. They beaded. They sat in the air in a little curved row like pearls on a string, smoking, and then slid down the curve and off the edge and across the bench, and dropped off the end of the bench to the floor, and rolled.

Baker said, "*Oh*," very softly, and tilted the candle further.

He did it for some time. He did it faster, and slower, and from higher up, and from so close that the flame nearly touched the field, muttering to himself under his breath, "And again. And— there, look at that. And again." The beads ran off the curve and went pattering over the edge of the bench, and a line of small white pearls of wax went rolling away over the boards.

Then he put the square on its cord round his neck, and touched it, and held the candle straight up over his own head.

The wax ran down the outside of him like rain down a window. It found the curve of the field above his hair and ran off it all round, a little bell of white drips, and dropped and rolled away across the floor in every direction. He stood in the middle of it, holding the candle up like a man at a midwinter procession, and grinned at the ceiling.

Wensa had seen pitch come down from a gatehouse once. She stood very still in the doorway.

One of the beads rolled the whole width of the room, over the boards and the seam between them and the boards again, and came to rest against the toe of her shoe.

He saw it. The grin went out of his face as if someone had blown on it.

"I'm so sorry," he said. He was across the room before she could move, and she very nearly moved, her hand was already at her belt. He went down on his knees in front of her and began to pick the wax off her shoe with his thumbnail, very carefully, the way he might have taken a burr off a sister's stocking.

Wensa looked down at the top of his head.

"There," he said, and sat back on his heels, holding up the little white bead between finger and thumb, as if he were returning something of hers. "It hasn't marked. I don't think. I'm sorry, ma'am. I didn't think it would go so far."

She took her hand off her belt.

"Mr. Baker," she said. "Get up."

* * *

On the ninth night he sat on the stool at the bench with the last square in his hands, the heavy one, and didn't do anything with it at all.

She waited in the doorway. The brazier had burned down. The room smelled of straw and tallow and the cut-penny smell of the etching acid, and there was wax on everything, on the bench, the floor, the window ledge, and on the wall in a long splash where he had tried throwing it.

He turned the square over, once. Then he threaded the cord through the two holes, and tied it, and held it up by the cord and watched it turn.

"That's it," he said, to nobody. "That's the one."

He looked at her then, in the doorway, as if he thought she might want to say something. She didn't.

"It's finished, ma'am. The design." He stood up. "I've to make a template now. That's two days, I should think. And then the copies. And then Master Kolb's test, on the twelfth." He hesitated, and then said it, quietly and politely, in the voice of a man asking whether it is likely to rain: "I expect you'll be there."

"I expect I will."

He nodded, as if that settled something. He put the square in his tunic, and picked up his satchel, and stopped at the door, which she was standing in. She moved aside for him. He went past her carefully, sideways, without turning his back.

"Good night, Mistress Wensa."

She listened to him go down the east stair, step by step, and heard the door at the bottom, and then nothing.

Then she went into the room. She looked at the wax on the walls, and the two halves of the split square, which he had kept, lying on the bench with a note in pencil on each. She picked one up and put it down again.

She opened the ledger, and uncapped her pen, and for the first time in nine days she wrote something in it. Three words, small and neat, at the top of a clean page.

*Ask for one.*
