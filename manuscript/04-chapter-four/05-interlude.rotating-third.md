*Father,*

*The shield passed. You will have heard by now from someone, because half the school has written home about it, and I expect the other half's letters have only been held up by the spelling. I enclose a description. I have not enclosed a price, because you taught me not to put a number in a letter that I couldn't stand behind in a court, and I can't stand behind any number yet. It's too big. I keep getting to the end of it and finding there's more.*

Sara stopped, and read it over, and crossed out *It's too big*, because her father would only underline it and send it back. Then she wrote it in again, smaller, because it was true.

* * *

On the morning after the test, Tor went to the building master at the sixth bell and to meditation at the seventh, and handed in his theory essay, and ate his lunch, exactly as if nothing had happened. Sara watched him do it. She thought he was the only person in the school who had managed it. Everybody else was still talking about the wall.

Mistress Wensa was still following him. She ate at the next table, and stood in doorways, and sat on the bench outside meditation; the only difference was that she'd stopped carrying the ledger. She wore the hilt on the outside of her dress now, plainly, where it could be seen, and students stepped round her in the corridors the way water steps round a stone. Tor still said good morning to her. He said it a little faster than before, Sara noticed, and a little more brightly, and he never turned his back.

"He thinks she's going to murder him," said Trice, at lunch.

"She put the fire out."

"He thinks that was so she could do it properly later." Trice ate a grape. "I asked."

* * *

*The shield will change, I should warn you, before it reaches you. He has already made three new ones. He says the first was wrong in four ways, and he has made one for each way but one, and he will put them together when he's sure. The fourth way he says he can't build yet, so he is practising instead, which you will hear about. I asked him how many ways the second one would be wrong in and he said he didn't know yet, and looked very pleased about it.*

The first new one was for the air.

He tested it in the washing house, because it was the only room in the school with a door that shut tight and a fire that nobody minded, and Davie let him in. Sara went because she didn't trust him not to suffocate. He put the new shield on, and touched it, and sat on an upturned tub in the middle of the floor, and Davie, who had been told what to do and plainly didn't believe it, heaped wet straw on the copper boiler fire and slammed the door on him and stood outside with Sara, holding it shut, looking as if he might be sick.

They counted. At two hundred Sara made Davie open it.

The smoke came out in a wall, and they both went backward coughing, and when it had cleared enough to see, Tor was sitting on the tub inside his egg of clean air, exactly where they'd left him, with his hands on his knees.

"It keeps a bit," he said, sounding delighted. "Like a crust keeps the steam in. It's not much, but it's enough to be getting on with. Two hundred, was it? You could have left it longer."

"No," said Sara, "we could not."

The second was for the ground. He had Kolb hit him with the iron bar in the practice yard, on a patch of sand that hadn't been raked, and this time there were no cracks. Instead the sand went down under him very slightly, all at once, in a wide even circle much bigger than his feet, as if he'd been standing on an invisible plate. "Like a cake on a plate," Tor said. "You don't put a cake straight on the table." Kolb said nothing to this, which Sara had come to understand was what Kolb said when he had no idea what someone meant and didn't intend to ask.

The moment before it was on was the one he couldn't build, and that one, to Sara's lasting regret, became Trice's.

He'd tried, he said at supper. He'd sat up two nights over a shield that would come on by itself, and every way he could think of to do it was worse than having it off. "It would go off for a sneeze," he said. "Or a bird. Or Rolph passing me the bread." So if the shield couldn't be quicker, he'd have to be. He'd never be as quick as Mistress Wensa; he glanced at her table as he said it, and lowered his voice, and said it again. But he could be quicker than he was. He'd need someone to practise on him. Without warning.

"Oh," said Trice. "I can do that."

She threw an apple at him at breakfast the next morning, from three tables away, without looking up from her porridge. It hit him on the ear. Tor said, "Ow," and then, "Oh! Thank you!" and wrote something on his wrist. She threw a boot at him in the corridor before theory, and he got his hand to the copper just as it arrived, and it bounced off the air and went skidding down the flags; she threw a cushion at him in the library, and he didn't. In combat, when he wasn't looking, she threw a small sack of sand off the pell, and his hand slapped his chest so fast that Kolb turned round at the sound, and the sack struck nothing a hand from his shoulder and burst. Every time, hit or not, he thanked her. By the third day half the school was throwing things at him on principle, and his hand had taken to flying to his chest at every sudden noise, a door, a dropped spoon, a sneeze in theory, so that he went about the place looking like a man forever on the point of swearing an oath. On the fourth, Sara watched a first-year throw a pear at the back of his head in the main court while he stood reading a letter from home, and the pear hit him squarely and burst.

Tor stood there, dripping. He looked at the letter.

"Still not, then," he said.

He wiped the pear out of his collar and wrote on his wrist, under the others, and drew a box round it. "That one's not done," he told Sara. "I'll have to think of something else."

Trice went on throwing things anyway. For practice, she said.

The third was the pond.

* * *

*I should tell you that I have seen a man walk into a pond and out again dry, with his hands behind his back, and that there were ducks.*

It was the morning of the rest day, early, with mist lying on the water, and there were more people on the bank than Sara would have liked. Rolph was there, with a towel and a rope. Tovey was there, a little apart, in his cloak, because Tor had asked him, politely and in some terror, whether he would mind standing nearby in case of anything, and Tovey had said, "Of course," and turned up a quarter of an hour early. Trice was there. Mistress Wensa was there, on the far bank, alone.

Tor put the third new shield on, and touched it, and walked into the pond.

He walked into it as if it were a field. He went down the muddy slope with his hands behind his back and into the brown water, and the water went up to his knees, and his waist, and his chest, and it didn't touch him. It stood back from him. It stood back in the shape of the egg, so that he walked down into the pond inside a bubble of clear air, and the water curved over the top of it like glass over a cake, and then closed over his head.

Sara found she'd gone down the bank to the edge without noticing.

He was down there. She could see him, a little brown blur at the bottom of the pond, with the bubble round him silver where the light caught it, walking. Walking slowly along the bottom of the pond, through the weed, with his hands behind his back. Two ducks paddled over to look. They paddled round the place where he was, interested, and one put its head under, and came up again very quickly, and the two of them went away to the other end of the pond with an air of having seen enough.

Rolph had the rope in both hands. Tovey hadn't moved at all.

"How long has he got?" said Rolph.

"He said two hundred," said Sara. "In the washing house. He said we could have left it longer."

"Did you?"

"No."

At a hundred and sixty he came up the other side, just below Mistress Wensa, out of the brown water and up the bank with the bubble round him, and the water fell off it all at once in a sheet, and he was standing on the grass at her feet, quite dry, with weed on his boots.

He looked up at her. He gave a little jump.

"Good morning, Mistress Wensa," he said.

* * *

*You asked for copies of the food dryer for the house, to sell, and they are packed. Thirty. He did them in an evening. I have also seen him do sixty drying fields in two, and most of them he gave away, and I couldn't stop him, and I have stopped trying. I have written them down. I enclose the list. You'll see the payments.*

The list was one of Sara's better pieces of work, and she was quietly proud of it.

*One pie, mutton. One pair of boots, mended (heel). Socks, two pair, gray, knitted by a sister. Hob in the kitchens: bread, a week. Davie: gratitude (considerable). A first-year: an apple, thrown.* It went on for a page and a half. Word had got out after the test that Baker was making drying fields again, and from the second evening there had been a queue outside the room door that went down the corridor and round the corner to the stair. Most of them were scholarship boys, or the younger sons of small houses, or girls from the far wing with laundry nobody else would do. Tor saw every one of them. He asked each one what they needed it for, and listened to the answer, and made them one, and wouldn't take money. When Sara tried to charge for him, standing at the door, he came and stood behind her and looked so stricken that she stopped.

So she took payment in kind instead, and wrote it down, and Tor didn't notice, and the room filled up with pies.

Kolb came on the third evening, through the queue, which parted for him like wheat in front of a cart. He stood in the doorway and looked at Tor.

"Twenty," he said.

"Twenty, sir?"

"For the school. Shields." He considered. "Twenty-five."

Tor made him twenty-five that night. Sara stayed till the tenth bell and watched him do it, hand flat on each copper plate, lips moving, and she heard him tell the eighteenth one, quite kindly, that it was just the same as the seventeenth and there was no point in pretending otherwise. In the morning Kolb collected them in a sack, all twenty-five, and went away down the corridor with it over his shoulder, clanking, like a tinker. He didn't say thank you. He came back, an hour later, and stood in the doorway again, and said, "Good work," and left before anyone could answer.

* * *

*The templates go to you by Haddon's cart on fifth day: shield, drying field, food dryer, all three, under my seal. You'll want to send a builder you trust to take them off the cart, not a clerk. I know you will anyway. I am only saying it so that it's in writing.*

Haddon came up from Hollin on fifth day with the cart and two horses, and backed them into the yard by the main gate, and Tor came down to see the crate packed.

He'd brought the straw himself. It was the end of the bag he'd bought for the smoke, and he laid it in the bottom of the crate in an even bed, and then the templates on top, each wrapped in oilcloth: the shield's copper, and the drying field's blue-painted pine, and the food dryer's plain pale board with Rolph's milk ring sanded off it. He put more straw over them. He tucked it in at the corners. Then he stood back and let Haddon nail the lid down, and when it was done, Sara heated the wax over a candle and dropped it on the join, and pressed her ring into it.

She looked up. Tor was looking at the crate.

He looked at it the way her mother had looked at the carriage, the morning Sara's eldest sister went away to be married. He had his hands in his pockets. He didn't say anything. Haddon swung the crate up into the cart and lashed it down, and climbed up after it and took the reins, and the cart went out of the gate and down the hill toward Hollin, rocking a little in the ruts, with the crate in the back of it under a tarpaulin, and Tor watched it go all the way down the hill until the road bent round the trees.

"They'll be all right," said Sara.

"I know." He was still looking at the bend in the road. "It's silly. It's just a board. I can make another." He took his hands out of his pockets. "It's like sending bread to market. When you're little. You stand at the door and watch the cart go, and it's only bread, and you'll make more tomorrow. But it's *your* bread."

Up by the gate arch, Mistress Wensa had watched the cart go too. She watched it until it went round the bend. Then she looked at Tor, for a moment, and went back inside.

* * *

*As to the money. I have told him what the house proposes and he has agreed to it, and I believe he heard about one word in five. He asked me to see to it, and Rolph and Trice to help, and said he was sure it would be fine. He trusts us completely. I would like it noted, Father, that I find this the most frightening part of the whole business.*

She found out about the box by accident, on the last evening, when she brought him the first of the house's money in a cloth bag and he didn't know where to put it.

He looked at the bag in her hand as if it might bite. Then he went to the foot of his bed and pulled out from under it a box she had never seen before. It was plain oak, quite small, with iron at the corners and no ornament on it at all, the sort of box a careful farmer keeps his deeds in. He'd bought it at the store, he said, a week ago. It had come with a lock and a key.

"But I took the lock off," said Tor. "It wasn't very good. Anyone could have opened it with a hairpin."

He laid his hand flat on the lid. Something clicked.

Sara looked across the room, at the foot of Rolph's bed, at Rolph's lockbox: the same click, the same hand. Rolph, on his own bed with a book, didn't look up. But Sara saw him smile.

The box was half full. There were little cloth bags inside, tied at the neck, gold and silver, and each one had a scrap of paper tied to it in Tor's careful round hand. *Mother.* *Terlee.* *The others, for shoes.* *Davie's mother (if needed).* And one bag, the smallest, by itself in the corner, that said *Spare*.

Tor put the new bag in, beside the others, and looked at it, and looked at the box. He looked at it the way he'd looked at the crate going down the hill, as if it belonged to somebody else, somebody he'd heard of and never met.

"I don't know what to write on it," he said.

Sara looked at the bag. She looked at all of them, and did the sum, and for the first time in her life found she didn't want to know the answer.

"Write *yours*," she said.

Tor considered this. Then he took the stub of pencil out of his tunic, and tore a corner off a page of his theory notes, and wrote on it, and tied it to the bag, and closed the box, and laid his hand on the lid until it clicked. Sara never saw what he'd written. She didn't ask.

That night she finished the letter.

*He has a box now, Father, with a lock of his own making. I think you'd like him. I think he would be terrified of you, and I think he would bring you bread.*

She read it over twice. Then she left it in.
