At a quarter before the sixth bell, the north corridor was empty except for one student. He stood outside the building master's door, washed and combed, satchel on his shoulder, hands folded in front of him. The window at the far end was gray. Somewhere below, a kitchen boy was dragging a pail across flagstones.

Footsteps came up the stair. Mistress Wensa reached the top, walked the length of the corridor to the window, and stopped there, with the light behind her. She opened a brown calf ledger and held it in one hand.

Baker had turned his head at the first step. His face stayed quite still. Then he bowed from the waist, the full bow they teach to pages.

"Good morning, Mistress Wensa," he said, down the corridor.

She inclined her head.

He faced the door again. He did not look at her for the next eleven minutes. He didn't shift his feet or look at the stair. Wensa turned no pages.

At the sixth bell the building master came up the stair, stopped, and looked from Baker to Wensa and back.

"You're early," he said.

"Yes, sir," said Baker. "You like us early."

The building master seemed to consider this. Then he found his key. Baker held the door for him, and before he let it close he looked back down the corridor at Wensa, a long, steady look, as if he were fixing where she stood.

Wensa wrote nothing in the ledger.

* * *

He kept the routine. Building master at the sixth bell. Meditation at the seventh, in the long room under the eaves; Wensa sat on the bench outside the closed door with the ledger on her knee. On the second morning Doris came out at the end of the hour, found her there, looked at her for some time without a word, and went back in. Theory at the ninth. Lunch at the noon bell. Combat with Kolb. Supper. The library until the tenth bell; then the room, and the sound of the bar dropping behind the door.

He was early for all of it. In the staff room on the third day, the theory master mentioned to nobody in particular that Baker had handed in an essay four days early and asked whether that was allowed.

In the cafeteria Wensa took the end seat of the next table, with her back to the wall and both doors in view. Baker greeted her at every meal. "Good morning, Mistress Wensa." "Good afternoon, Mistress Wensa." On the second day he got up in the middle of his porridge, carried the salt across to her table, put it down by her hand, and went back to his seat before she had looked up.

Rolph sat with him at every meal. On the second day he sat facing Wensa's table instead of Tor, and watched her over his porridge for a long time, without expression. She met his eyes briefly and looked past him to the door. He didn't look away. Her glance came back, once, to his tray: eggs, bread, nothing green, a cup of milk he hadn't touched.

* * *

On the evening of the second day she stood at the counter of the student store while the storekeeper read aloud from his book.

"Copper, mistress, twenty squares, the thin gauge. Acid. A pot of resist. Candles, two dozen, tallow." He turned the page with visible reluctance. "And a bag of straw."

"Straw," said Wensa.

"For the smell, he said, mistress. He said it smokes nicely when it's damp."

Wensa looked at the page for a moment longer. Then she nodded and left.

* * *

The thaw had come the week before. The practice yard was dark and wet, and the water butt at the corner had filled and was running over the rim into the sand. After combat, while the class was racking the wasters, Wensa sat on the bench beside the butt with the ledger on her knee.

Baker looked across at her, bowed slightly from where he stood, and hung three copper squares on cords from the arm of the pell.

Kolb came over, wiping his hands on his trousers.

"You've got a shadow, Baker."

"Yes, sir."

Kolb looked across the yard at Wensa. She looked back. He grinned, which was rare, and turned to the pell.

"Which one?"

"The middle one, please, sir. The left one's no good, I don't think. But I'd like to know how it's no good."

Kolb unhooked the hilt from his belt, and the blade was there: four feet of dull gray that the light slid off. On the bench, Wensa's hand moved to her belt, stopped, and went back flat onto the ledger.

"Turn it," Baker was saying, under his breath. "Don't stop it. You can't stop a line. Turn it."

Kolb cut.

The blade met something a hand's breadth short of the copper. It didn't stop. It slid along a curve nobody could see, the way a knife slides off a frozen loaf, went past the square, and took the top six inches off the pell. The wood dropped into the wet sand. The square swung on its cord, unmarked.

Kolb looked at the pell for a while.

"Huh," he said.

"I'm sorry about the pell, sir."

"Got more pells." Kolb weighed the hilt. "It went somewhere, though. Didn't it. Stand next to that and you'd lose an ear."

"Yes, sir. That's what I thought. I'll have to think where it goes."

On the bench, Wensa sat without moving. Her eyes were on the copper square, turning in the light.

"Again, sir? The middle one?"

"Stand back, then."

* * *

On the fifth day the blade came down on a new square and stopped.

It didn't slide. It stopped a hand from the copper and stayed there, as if it had come to the end of itself. Kolb leaned on it. He put his second hand on the hilt and leaned with his whole weight, and his boots slid back an inch in the sand, and the square hung in front of him, turning a little on its cord.

He let the blade go and looked at Baker.

"Huh," he said, in a different voice.

Then the waster, and then the iron bar, and Kolb wasn't careful with either. The yard rang. Twice the pell jumped in its socket. On the ninth or tenth blow with the bar one square split straight across, with a high clean crack like a dropped plate, and the next blow knocked both halves into the sand.

Baker picked them up and turned them over. His lips moved; he was saying something to them. Then he took a stub of pencil from his tunic and wrote on his wrist.

"Too thin," he told Kolb. "It's like a crust. The filling's fine, but the crust has to hold the shape. I'll go up a gauge."

Kolb looked at him as though he might say something about crusts. Then he put the bar back on the rack.

On the bench, Wensa still hadn't written anything.

* * *

The workroom on the east stair was one of the small ones, a bench, a stool, a brazier, a window. A slate on the door said BAKER, 9th–12th. At the eleventh bell Wensa stood in the doorway.

Baker was on the stool with a square on a cord round his neck and the brazier lit beside him. When she appeared he stood up so fast the stool went over.

"Good evening, Mistress Wensa." He picked it up. "Would you like this? It's quite safe. I've checked it."

"No."

"I'm only doing fire, ma'am. And then smoke. You might like to stand back a little, for the smoke." He looked at the brazier, then at her. "Or you could watch from the corridor. It's nearer the window."

She stayed in the doorway.

He held a lit candle under a square on the bench. The flame bent away from the copper and lay down sideways, flat, as if a wind were blowing that touched nothing else in the room. Then he touched the square at his throat and sat down beside the brazier, much too close, and held his hands out over the coals the way a man warms his hands at home. He didn't squint. After a minute he took the tongs and laid a live coal on his own knee. It hung a hand above the cloth, smoking a little, and he studied it.

Then he put the straw on.

It smoked at once, thick and yellow-white, and the smoke rolled up and filled the room from the ceiling down. Wensa stepped back into the corridor with her sleeve over her mouth, coughing, eyes streaming.

Inside, in the middle of the smoke, there was a clear space the shape of an egg, and Baker was sitting in it.

The smoke lay against the outside of the egg as thick as fleece, folding round the curve. Inside, he sat with his hands on his knees and breathed, slowly, in and out, four times. Then he laughed, a short surprised sound.

"Oh. Oh, that's *it*. The air's smaller. Of course it is."

Wensa coughed.

He turned at the sound and was up at once, wading out through the smoke with the clear space moving round him, the smoke parting in front and closing behind. He got the window open and stood there flapping at the smoke with his satchel.

"I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I should have thought. I should have *said*—" The far wall came back into view. "I did warn you, ma'am. About the smoke." He looked as though this made it worse.

* * *

On the seventh night, or the eighth, he had a fresh square on the bench, the heavier gauge, and a fat candle burning. He held the candle over the square, tipped it, and let it drip.

The drops struck the field and didn't spread. They beaded, a little curved row of them hanging in the air like pearls on a string, smoking. Then they slid down the curve, off the edge, across the bench, off the end of it, and onto the floor, and rolled.

"*Oh*," said Baker, softly, and tipped the candle further.

He kept at it. Faster, slower, from higher up, from so close that the flame nearly touched the field. "And again. And— there, look at that. And again." The beads ran off and pattered over the edge of the bench, and a line of small white pearls went rolling away across the boards.

Then he hung the square round his neck, touched it, and held the candle straight up over his head.

The wax ran down the outside of him like rain down a window: off the curve above his hair, all the way round, a bell of white drips, falling and rolling away over the floor in every direction. He stood in the middle with the candle held high, like a boy in a midwinter procession, and grinned at the ceiling.

In the doorway, Wensa stood very still.

One bead rolled the whole width of the room, across the boards and the seam and the boards again, and stopped against the toe of her shoe.

Baker saw it. The grin went out of his face at once.

"I'm so sorry."

He crossed the room fast. Wensa's hand went to her belt, and stayed there, and he dropped to his knees in front of her and began picking the wax off her shoe with his thumbnail, carefully, the way you'd take a burr off a little sister's stocking.

Wensa looked down at the top of his head.

"There." He sat back on his heels and held up the bead between finger and thumb, as if returning something she'd dropped. "It hasn't marked. I don't think. I'm sorry, ma'am. I didn't think it would go so far."

She took her hand off her belt.

"Mr. Baker," she said. "Get up."

* * *

On the ninth night he sat on the stool with the last square in his hands, the heavy one, and did nothing with it.

The brazier had burned down. There was wax everywhere: on the bench, on the floor, on the window ledge, in a long white splash down one wall. The two halves of the split square lay on the bench, each with a pencil note on it.

He turned the square over once. Then he threaded a cord through its two holes, tied it, and held it up by the cord to watch it turn.

"That's it," he said. "That's the one."

He looked at Wensa in the doorway, as if he thought she might want to say something. She didn't.

"It's finished, ma'am. The design." He stood. "I've to make a template now. That's two days, I should think. And then the copies. And then Master Kolb's test, on the twelfth." He paused. Then, quietly and politely, the way someone asks whether it's likely to rain: "I expect you'll be there."

"I expect I will."

He nodded. He put the square inside his tunic, picked up his satchel and came to the door. She stood aside. He went past her sideways, slowly, without once turning his back to her.

"Good night, Mistress Wensa."

His steps went down the east stair. The door at the bottom shut.

Wensa went into the room. She looked at the wax on the walls. She picked up one half of the split square, read the note on it, and put it down.

Then she opened the ledger, uncapped her pen, and wrote, small and neat, at the top of a clean page:

*Ask for one.*
