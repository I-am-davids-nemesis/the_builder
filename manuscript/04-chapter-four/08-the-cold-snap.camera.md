In the women's wing, the ink in Sara's well had frozen solid.

She sat on the edge of her bed in three pairs of stockings and looked at it. The window was white with frost on the inside, long feathered fronds like ferns. Her breath showed. On the other bed was a hump of blankets with one hand sticking out of it, holding a boot.

"Don't," said the hump, "say anything."

* * *

By the second bell, girls stood three deep round the stove at the end of the corridor, in blankets, coats, a brother's old riding cloak. The stove went out twice from the draught.

In accounts, Mistress Wensa taught in her gloves. The figures she chalked on the board were slightly less neat than usual.

At lunch the soup came steaming out of the kitchen and was lukewarm by the time it reached the tables.

By evening the water butt in the practice yard had a lid of ice, and two second-years were standing on it.

At the sixth bell the porter climbed to the women's wing and knocked.

"Young man below, Miss Debri. For you and Miss Morgan. Said if you asked what for, to tell you to bring blankets."

* * *

Rolph stood in the lodge, red-faced, his breath going up in a cloud. He was grinning.

"Tor's done something," he said.

"Is it on fire?" said Trice.

"No. Well. Sort of. Come and see. Bring blankets. You won't need them. But bring them anyway. It's a long way across the court."

The court rang like iron under their boots. The stars were small and hard. Sara walked with her chin in her collar and her hands in her armpits.

At the top of the stair to the men's corridor, she stopped.

Six or seven students in blankets leaned against the corridor wall with their backs and palms flat on the plaster and their eyes shut, like cats against a kitchen chimney. One was asleep on his feet. A door opened further along; another boy came out, looked at them, came over, leaned on the wall between two of the others, and sighed.

It was the wall of Tor and Rolph's room.

"It's coming through," said Rolph. "Feel."

Sara put her gloved hand on the plaster. Then she pulled off the glove and put her bare hand on it, and left it there.

Rolph opened the door. Warm air rolled out into the corridor.

* * *

Inside, the frost on the window had melted and was running down the black glass in drops. Rolph's coat hung over a chair. The candle on the desk was leaning slightly.

On the wall above Tor's bed, at about the height of a man's heart, four ordinary nails held a rough-cut copper plate the size of a book, its edges still bright from the shears. A few etched lines on it, and a downward hook. It didn't glow or hum.

Tor sat under it on his bed with a blanket round his shoulders, eating an apple. He jumped up.

"Miss Sara. Trice. Come in, please. Sit down. Are you cold? You must be cold." He looked at their blankets. "I'm sorry. It's warmer in here. I didn't mean to— Rolph said you'd be cold. In the other wing."

"Tor," said Sara. "What is it?"

He turned and looked at the plate.

"Oh. It's nothing much. I couldn't hold the pen. My fingers had gone. I was trying to do the theory and I couldn't write, so I thought—" He shrugged. "It only took an hour. It's very simple, really. There's hardly anything to it."

"What does it *do*?"

"It moves the warm," said Tor.

They looked at him.

"From down there." He pointed at the floor. "Underneath. The ground. You know the cellar at home? At anybody's home. You go down to the cellar in the winter, and it's warmer than the kitchen. And in summer you go down and it's cooler. It's always the same down there. Always. My mother keeps the butter there for that." He drew a straight line in the air with one finger, from the plate down to the floor. "It just reaches down. Through the building. Into the ground, under the foundations, where it's always cellar. And it brings a bit of that up. That's all. It's not making any heat. It's only carrying it. Like a bucket."

Trice sat down slowly on Rolph's bed.

"It's not *making* heat," she said.

"No."

"There's no fire."

"No. It doesn't need one. That's the nice part, really. It hardly needs anything. Carrying's cheap. It's making that's dear."

Sara stood in the middle of the room with her blanket sliding off one shoulder. Her lips moved slightly. Her hand found the back of the desk chair and held it.

"Tor," she said. "How long does it last?"

"I don't know yet. A long time, I'd think. It's copper. It's not working very hard." He considered. "Years?"

Sara sat down.

* * *

Half an hour later, two boys from the corridor put their heads round the door to say thank you, and Tor went pink and said it was nothing. Rolph had taken off his tunic and sat in his shirtsleeves. Sara had dropped her blanket, then her shawl, and was fanning herself with Tor's unwritten theory essay.

"Tor," said Trice. "Does it stop?"

He looked up. "Stop?"

"Carrying. Your bucket. Does it stop when the room's full?"

A pause.

"Oh," said Tor.

The candle on the desk had folded over in the middle and was dripping onto the wood. Rolph's face shone. Somewhere in the wall behind the plate, the plaster was ticking.

Tor was on his feet. "I didn't put a— I was cold. I only thought about *warm*. I didn't think about *enough*." He looked from the plate to the window. "Rolph, the window, would you—"

Rolph wrenched at the window. It stuck with frost, then gave all at once.

The night came in. Freezing air poured across the room and met the warm in the middle, and for a moment mist curled up off the boards between them.

Tor stood at the open window with the cold streaming past him and the warm streaming out, his hair moving in it. He stopped moving.

Sara watched him. So did Trice. Rolph, by the window, didn't move either.

"It goes out," said Tor.

"Yes," said Rolph. "That's what windows are for."

"No. I mean— the warm. It goes out to where the cold is. It always goes that way. From where there's more to where there's less. And the plate's carrying it *up*, from where there's more, down there, to here, where there's less. But if it was *summer*—" He turned round. "If it was summer, and it was hot in here, and it was cellar down there. Cool. Then there'd be more up here. And it could carry it *down*."

He went to the plate and laid his hand flat on it, eyes closed.

"It's the same plate," he said. "It's just the other way round. You'd turn it round. Not the plate, the— You'd tell it. In the summer it'd take the warm out of the room and put it in the ground. And then in the winter it'd take it back." He opened his eyes. "It'd *pay it back*. Do you see? The ground lends it you in the winter and you pay it back in the summer, and over a year it's the same. It's always the same. It'd hardly need anything at all. You'd hardly need to *feed* it."

The window stood open. Nobody moved to shut it.

Sara was looking at the plate. She stood up.

When she spoke, her voice had changed: slower, very even, very clear.

"Tor," she said. "Marry me."

* * *

Cold air filled the silence.

Tor looked at her, politely and entirely bewildered.

"I beg your pardon?" he said.

"You can't," said Trice.

They both turned. Trice sat on Rolph's bed in her shirtsleeves, dark hair coming loose in the heat, looking at Sara, not at Tor. Her face was calm. Her ears were pink.

"I saw him first," said Trice.

"You did not."

"I did. At the laundry. The very first day. I had you price him." Trice folded her hands in her lap. "You'd never have looked twice. You were looking at the sheets. I asked you what he was worth. I believe that's first refusal, Sara. Your father would say so."

"My father," said Sara, "would say that pricing a thing isn't buying it."

"Your father would say that asking the price is opening the account."

"Then the account's mine. I set the terms."

"You set the *drying field* terms."

"Ten in the hundred," said Sara. "On everything. *Everything*, Trice."

"Not that. That's never been on any list."

"It's on mine."

They looked at each other across the room, both smiling.

Behind them, Tor laughed. It burst out of him, warm and helpless; he put a hand over his mouth and couldn't stop it. He sat down on his bed under the copper plate and laughed until his eyes were wet.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's— Thank you. Thank you, both of you, that's so kind." He wiped his eyes. "You don't have to, you know. Cheer me up. I'm not upset. It's only a plate."

Sara looked at him.

"You're very good at it," said Tor, smiling at them both. "Both of you. You'd almost believe it. But you don't need to be kind about it, honestly. I know how that goes." For a moment his smile stayed where it was and nothing was behind it. Then it came back. "I've asked a girl to a dance before."

Nobody said anything.

"It's all right," said Tor gently. "Really. It was a long time ago. And it's a lovely joke."

He got up and laid his hand on the plate again, frowning. "I'll have to put a stop in it. For *enough*. Rolph, can you shut the window? I think I can make it hold just there."

Sara sat down.

Trice picked her shawl up off the floor and folded it, very precisely, into a small square on her knees. She didn't look at Sara. Sara didn't look at her.

Rolph shut the window slowly. He stood with his back to it and looked at the three of them in turn: Tor with his hand on the plate, eyes half shut, lips moving; Trice folding her shawl; Sara. He looked at Sara longest. He said nothing.

Then he went and sat on his own bed next to Trice, and after a moment bumped her shoulder gently with his.

* * *

At the tenth bell the room had settled to the warmth of a good kitchen after supper. The plaster had stopped ticking. In the corridor, the boys were sitting in a row against the wall now, and two of them were asleep.

Sara stood in the doorway with her blanket over her arm.

"You know they weren't joking," said Rolph quietly, behind her.

Tor, already at the desk, already drawing, answered without looking up. "I know. Weren't they good? You'd almost believe them."

Sara went out and shut the door. She stood a moment in the warm corridor among the sleeping boys. Then she went down the stair into the cold.

* * *

The cold lasted four days.

On the first, Davie carried a second copper plate across the court to the east wing, where the scholarship boys slept four to a room without a stove, holding it in front of him in both hands like a relic.

On the second, Tor made six more. Sara sat at his desk with a list. *Infirmary. Washing house. First-years' long room. Women's wing, both corridors.* At the bottom: *Trice.* *Sara.* Trice took the pen, crossed both out, and wrote them in again the other way round. Sara let her.

On the third, a servant in livery brought a note on paper so heavy it stood up by itself. Tor read it three times.

"It's from Count Thompson," he said. "He says his rooms are quite warm. He's got a hearth. And a man for it." He swallowed. "He wonders, if it's not a great inconvenience to Scholar Baker, whether there might be one sometime for his mother. Who keeps a large house. And feels the winters."

He went white, then pink, and made one that night, and wrapped it himself.

On the fourth day the wind went round to the south. The frost was off the windows by noon. By evening the ice in the water butt had gone soft and gray, and someone had pushed a second-year into it.

The plates stayed on the walls.

"Leave them," said Tor, to everyone who asked him to take them down. "You'll want them in the summer."

That night Sara sat at her own desk, under a copper plate nailed above her bed, and wrote a letter. She wrote three drafts and tore them up. The fourth was short.

*I am not able to put a price on this one, Father. Please come and see it yourself.*

She sat for a long time with the pen in her hand, looking at it. Then she sealed it.
