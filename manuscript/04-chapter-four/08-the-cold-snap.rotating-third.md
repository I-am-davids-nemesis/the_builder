The cold came back in the night, with no warning at all, and by morning Sara's ink had frozen in the well.

She sat on the edge of her bed in every stocking she owned and looked at it. It had been the warmest week of the spring. She had packed her winter cloak in the bottom of her trunk under two summer dresses and a box of letters, and the window had stood open three nights running, and she'd lain awake listening to the lambs. Now the window was white. There was frost on the inside of it, in long feathered fronds like ferns, and when she breathed out she could see it, and on the other bed Trice was a hump of blankets with one hand sticking out, and the hand was holding a boot.

"Don't," said the hump, "say anything."

The women's wing had a stove at the end of each corridor, and by the fifth bell the girls were three deep round every one of them, in blankets, in coats, in somebody's brother's old riding cloak, and the stove at their end had gone out twice because too many people were standing in its draught. Lessons happened. Nobody could hold a pen. In accounts, Mistress Wensa taught in her gloves, and wrote the figures on the board in a hand that was slightly less neat than usual, which was the only sign she gave that anything was wrong. At lunch the soup came out of the kitchen steaming and was lukewarm by the time it reached the tables. By the evening there was ice in the water butt in the practice yard thick enough to stand on, and two second-years had proved it.

At the sixth bell there was a knock at the door of the women's wing, and the porter came up and said there was a young man below asking for Miss Debri and Miss Morgan, and that he'd said, if they asked what for, to tell them to bring blankets.

* * *

It was Rolph. He was standing in the lodge with his face red from the cold and his breath going up in a cloud, and he was grinning.

"Tor's done something," he said.

"Is it on fire?" said Trice.

"No. Well. Sort of. Come and see. Bring blankets," he added. "You won't need them. But bring them anyway. It's a long way across the court."

It was. The court was iron. Their boots rang on it. The stars were out, hard and small, and the air hurt to breathe, and Sara walked with her chin down in her collar and her hands in her armpits and thought, quite seriously, that she had never been so cold in her life. Then they came up the stair to the men's corridor, and she stopped.

There was a row of students along the corridor wall.

They were leaning on it. Six or seven of them, in blankets, with their backs and their palms flat against the plaster, eyes closed, like cats against a kitchen chimney. One of them was asleep standing up. As Sara watched, another came out of a room further along, saw them, hesitated, and then came and leaned on the wall too, between two others, and let out a long sigh.

The wall was the wall of Tor and Rolph's room.

"It's coming through," said Rolph, with enormous satisfaction. "Feel."

Sara put her glove against it. Then she took the glove off and put her hand against it. The plaster was warm. Not hot. Warm, the way a loaf is warm an hour after it comes out, all the way through.

Rolph opened the door, and the warmth came out to meet them like a dog.

* * *

The room was summer.

That was the only word for it. The window was black with night and the frost had gone off the inside of the glass and was running down it in tears. There was a smell of warm wool and warm wood. Rolph's coat was off and over the back of a chair. The candle on the desk had begun, very gently, to lean.

And on the wall above Tor's bed, at about the height of a man's heart, there was a copper plate.

It was the size of a book, roughly cut, with the edges still bright where the shears had been, nailed to the plaster at each corner with four ordinary nails. There was a sigil on it, etched, quite simple, a few lines and a sort of downward hook, and nothing else. It wasn't glowing. It didn't hum. It just hung there on the wall looking like a copper plate, and the room was warm.

Tor was sitting under it on his bed with a blanket round his shoulders, which he didn't seem to need any more, eating an apple.

He jumped up when they came in.

"Miss Sara. Trice. Come in, please. Sit down. Are you cold? You must be cold." He looked at them in their blankets and seemed to realize what he'd said. "I'm sorry. It's warmer in here. I didn't mean to— Rolph said you'd be cold. In the other wing."

"Tor," said Sara. "What is it?"

He turned and looked at the plate, as if he'd forgotten it was there.

"Oh," he said. "It's nothing much. I couldn't hold the pen. My fingers had gone. I was trying to do the theory and I couldn't write, so I thought—" He shrugged. "It only took an hour. It's very simple, really. There's hardly anything to it."

"What does it *do*?"

"It moves the warm," said Tor.

They looked at him.

"From down there." He pointed at the floor. "Underneath. The ground. You know the cellar at home? At anybody's home. You go down to the cellar in the winter, and it's warmer than the kitchen. And in summer you go down and it's cooler. It's always the same down there. Always. My mother keeps the butter there for that." He pointed at the floor again, and then at the plate, and then drew a line in the air between them with his finger, straight down. "It just reaches down. Through the building. Into the ground, under the foundations, where it's always cellar. And it brings a bit of that up. That's all. It's not making any heat. It's only carrying it. Like a bucket."

Trice sat down on Rolph's bed, slowly, still in her blanket.

"It's not *making* heat," she said.

"No."

"There's no fire."

"No. It doesn't need one. That's the nice part, really." He looked pleased, in his careful way. "It hardly needs anything. Carrying's cheap. It's making that's dear."

Sara had stopped hearing him at about *bucket*.

She was doing it already. She couldn't help it. She stood in the middle of the warm room with her blanket sliding off one shoulder and her hand still tingling from the wall, and the figures came up in her the way they always did, unbidden, in her father's voice. A house. Any house. How much wood does a house burn, in a winter? A farmhouse. A manor. A warehouse with a stove for the clerks. The Debri counting house in the capital, with its six hearths and the man whose whole job was carrying coal up the back stair. The school. The *palace*. Wood, and coal, and peat, and the carts that carried them, and the men who cut them, and the price going up every winter and never once coming down. A plate on the wall. Four nails. An hour.

Her hand had found the back of the chair. She held onto it.

"Tor," she said. "How long does it last?"

"I don't know yet. A long time, I'd think. It's copper. It's not working very hard." He considered. "Years?"

Sara sat down.

* * *

It was Trice who noticed it was getting hotter.

They'd been there perhaps half an hour. Two of the boys from the corridor had put their heads round the door to say thank you, and Tor had gone pink and said it was nothing, and they'd gone back to their wall. Rolph had taken off his tunic and was sitting in his shirtsleeves. Sara had let her blanket drop entirely, and then her shawl, and was fanning herself with the theory essay Tor hadn't been able to write.

"Tor," said Trice. "Does it stop?"

He looked up. "Stop?"

"Carrying. Your bucket. Does it stop when the room's full?"

There was a pause.

"Oh," said Tor.

The candle on the desk had folded over at the middle like a tired man and was dripping onto the wood. Rolph's face was shining. The air had the thick, close, bakehouse feel of a kitchen at noon on bread day, and somewhere in the wall behind the plate, very faintly, Sara could hear the plaster ticking.

"I didn't—" Tor was on his feet. "I didn't put a— I was cold. I only thought about *warm*. I didn't think about *enough*." He looked at the plate. He looked at the window. "Rolph, the window, would you—"

Rolph got the window open. It stuck, with the frost, and then gave all at once, and the night came in.

It came in like a blade. Sara felt it go across the room and over her face and down the back of her neck, a long, clean, freezing breath of the frost outside, smelling of stars and iron, and it met the warm in the middle of the room, and for a moment there was mist, actual mist, curling up from the boards between them.

Tor stood at the open window in it. The cold was pouring in round him, and the warm was pouring out past him, and his hair was moving in it.

And he went still.

Sara knew that stillness. Half the school knew it by now. She watched him stand there with one hand still on the window frame and the other hanging at his side, looking out into the dark, not seeing it.

"It goes out," he said.

"Yes," said Rolph. "That's what windows are for."

"No. I mean— the warm. It goes out to where the cold is. It always goes that way. From where there's more to where there's less. And the plate's carrying it *up*, from where there's more, down there, to here, where there's less. But if it was *summer*—" He turned round. "If it was summer, and it was hot in here, and it was cellar down there. Cool. Then there'd be more up here. And it could carry it *down*."

He looked at the plate on the wall. He went to it, and put his hand flat on the copper, and stood there with his eyes closed.

"It's the same plate," he said. "It's just the other way round. You'd turn it round. Not the plate, the— You'd tell it. In the summer it'd take the warm out of the room and put it in the ground. And then in the winter it'd take it back." He opened his eyes. "It'd *pay it back*. Do you see? The ground lends it you in the winter and you pay it back in the summer, and over a year it's the same. It's always the same. It'd hardly need anything at all. You'd hardly need to *feed* it."

The window was still open. The cold was still coming in. Nobody moved to shut it.

Sara looked at the plate.

She thought of the capital in summer. The river stinking in the heat. The warehouses on the Debri wharf, where the fruit came in by the boatload and a third of it was rotten by the time it was sold, and her father walking along the rows with a cloth over his face. The glass halls, where the fine fields were set in ironwood, and the merchants fanning themselves in their silks, and the old ladies fainting in the queue. Houses with the shutters closed all afternoon. Babies crying all night in the heat. Every house in Noram, every winter. Every house in Noram, every summer. A plate. Four nails. And he'd done it because his fingers had gone cold.

She found she was standing up.

She found, too, that her voice had gone quite steady and quite slow, and even, and very clear, the voice she used across a table when there was paper on it, the voice that was her father's, and that she couldn't for the life of her have changed it.

"Tor," she said. "Marry me."

* * *

There was a short silence in the room. It was full of cold air.

Tor looked at her. He looked, Sara thought, exactly as he'd looked in the practice yard when Kolb laughed: politely, completely bewildered, as though she'd said something in a language he knew she spoke and he'd never learned.

"I beg your pardon?" he said.

"You can't," said Trice.

They both turned.

Trice was sitting on Rolph's bed in her shirtsleeves, with her dark hair coming loose in the heat, and she was looking at Sara, not at Tor. Her face was perfectly calm. Her ears, Sara saw, had gone pink.

"I saw him first," said Trice.

"You did not."

"I did. At the laundry. The very first day. I had you price him." Trice folded her hands in her lap. "You'd never have looked twice. You were looking at the sheets. I asked you what he was worth. I believe that's first refusal, Sara. Your father would say so."

"My father," said Sara, "would say that pricing a thing isn't buying it."

"Your father would say that asking the price is opening the account."

"Then the account's mine. I set the terms."

"You set the *drying field* terms."

"Ten in the hundred," said Sara. "On everything. *Everything*, Trice."

"Not that. That's never been on any list."

"It's on mine."

They looked at each other across the room. Sara found she was smiling. So was Trice. It was the smile they used at dinner when somebody's mother had said something unforgivable about somebody else's, and it meant *I'll see you upstairs*, and it meant *I love you*, and it meant *I'm going to win*.

Behind them, Tor laughed.

It was a real laugh, startled out of him, warm and helpless, and he put his hand over his mouth as if to stop it and couldn't. He sat back down on his bed under the copper plate and laughed until his eyes were wet.

"Oh," he said. "Oh, that's— Thank you. Thank you, both of you, that's so kind." He wiped his eyes. "You don't have to, you know. Cheer me up. I'm not upset. It's only a plate."

Sara looked at him.

"You're very good at it," Tor went on, still smiling at them both, with perfect affection. "Both of you. You'd almost believe it. But you don't need to be kind about it, honestly. I know how that goes." His smile stayed exactly where it was, and something behind it went away for a moment, and came back. "I've asked a girl to a dance before."

Nobody said anything.

"It's all right," said Tor, gently, to whichever of their faces he was looking at. "Really. It was a long time ago. And it's a lovely joke."

Then he got up, and went to the plate, and put his hand on it again, and frowned, and said, "I'll have to put a stop in it. For *enough*. Rolph, can you shut the window? I think I can make it hold just there."

Sara sat down again. She didn't remember deciding to.

Across the room, Trice had picked up her shawl from the floor, and was folding it, very precisely, into a small square on her knees. She didn't look at Sara. Sara didn't look at her. They had been friends for nine years, and they both knew what the other was doing, and they let each other do it.

Rolph shut the window.

He did it slowly, and when it was shut he stood with his back to it, and looked at the three of them, one after another: at Tor with his hand on the plate and his eyes half closed, muttering; at Trice folding her shawl; at Sara. He looked at Sara longest. He didn't say anything at all. His face was the face of a man who has been to a great many dinners, and has seen a great many things said at them, and knows exactly what was just said at this one, and has decided it is not, on balance, his place.

Then he went and sat down on his own bed beside Trice, and after a moment, very gently, he bumped her shoulder with his.

* * *

They left at the tenth bell. The room had settled to the warmth of a good kitchen after supper, and stayed there; Tor had stood with his hand on the plate for a quarter of an hour, with his lips moving, and when he took it away the plaster had stopped ticking. The boys in the corridor were still against the wall, but they were sitting now, most of them, in a row, and two were asleep.

Sara was in the doorway, with her blanket over her arm, when she heard Rolph say it behind her. He said it quietly, the way you say a thing you don't expect to be heard.

"You know they weren't joking."

And Tor, already back at the desk, already drawing, answered him quite cheerfully, without looking up.

"I know. Weren't they good? You'd almost believe them."

Sara went out into the corridor and shut the door behind her, and stood for a moment in the warm, among the sleeping boys, before she went down into the cold.

* * *

The cold lasted four days.

By the end of the first, Tor had made a second plate for the east wing, where the scholarship boys slept four to a room and had no stove at all, and Davie had carried it over with the air of a man bearing a relic. By the end of the second he'd made six more. Sara took charge of them, because somebody had to, and because Tor would have given them to whoever was standing nearest. She made a list. The infirmary, first, where the sister had been sleeping in her chair beside the beds to keep the fire in. The washing house, for the sheets that wouldn't dry. The first-years' long room, where someone had been found that morning with frost in his eyebrows. The women's wing, both corridors. She put her own name last and Trice's last but one, and Trice crossed them both out and put them back in a different order, and Sara let her.

On the third day a note came from Tovey, by a servant in livery, on paper so heavy it stood up by itself. Tovey's rooms had a hearth of their own and a man to keep it lit, and he had not, the note made clear, been cold. But he wondered, if it was not a great inconvenience to Scholar Baker, whether there might at some point be one for his mother, who kept a large house, and felt the winters.

Tor read it three times, and went white, and then pink, and made one that night, and wrapped it himself.

On the fourth day the wind went round to the south, and the frost went off the windows by noon, and by evening the ice in the water butt had gone soft and gray and somebody had pushed a second-year into it.

The plates stayed up. People asked him to take them down, now the cold was gone, and he wouldn't.

"Leave them," he said, to everyone who asked. "You'll want them in the summer."

Sara wrote to her father that night. She wrote three drafts of it. In the end she sent the fourth, which was very short, and had no figures in it at all, and ended: *I am not able to put a price on this one, Father. Please come and see it yourself.*

She didn't say anything else. She thought about it for a long time, with the pen in her hand, in the warm, under the copper plate that somebody, without asking her, had nailed to the wall above her bed.
