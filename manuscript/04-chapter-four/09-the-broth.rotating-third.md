The rota was Trice's idea, because somebody had to have one.

She wrote it on the back of one of Tor's crossed-out sheets, in a firm hand, and pinned it to the inside of the door with the bent nail Rolph's coat usually hung on. *Rolph: nights. Trice: afternoons, and after supper until Rolph. Mornings: whoever has no class first, and if nobody, Rolph.* At the bottom, after a moment, she added *Water every bell*, and underlined it twice.

Sara had read it over her shoulder and said, "You haven't put me on."

"You've got the crate," Trice said. "And your father's letter. And the washing-house account."

"I could do an afternoon."

"You could." Trice had pinned the corner flat. "You've got the crate."

Sara had looked at her for a moment, the look that meant a figure had been noted and would be carried forward. Then she'd said, "I'll bring his notes from theory," which was a fair counter-offer, and they'd left it there.

That had been on the second day.

* * *

It had begun on a fourth day, at supper, with nothing to show for it. Tor had come back from combat with his sleeves wet from the water butt and the little drawing of the spoon still folded in his tunic, and sat down at his desk to eat the bread Rolph gave him, and put the bread down, and picked up the pen. Rolph had said goodnight to him at the tenth bell and got no answer, which was usual. He'd woken at the third bell of the night and seen the candle burning down and Tor's hand moving, which was usual too. In the morning the candle had gone out on its own and the hand was still moving, and the sheet under it had gone gray with lines, and the ink in the pot had a skin on it like a pond in a frost.

Rolph had come down to breakfast and found Trice and said, in his ordinary voice, "He's gone under."

"How far?"

"I said his name. I said it quite loud. Then I dropped a boot." Rolph had taken her toast. "He said 'mm.'"

That was the first day. By the second, Bet Haskins had heard. Trice was never afterward sure how; the woman lived under the old wing and came up for meals and nothing else, and yet at the fourth bell of the afternoon there she was at the top of the dormitory stair, gray shawl, lamp unlit in her hand out of habit, asking which door was Baker's.

She stood in the doorway a long while, looking at him. Tor sat at the desk with his back to them. He had a square of copper on the wood in front of him, bigger than a shield, and a second thing beside it no bigger than a hen's egg, flattened, and his right hand went from one to the other and back without hurrying, a stylus in it, then a brush, then the stylus again. His left hand lay flat beside the egg-shaped thing, as if keeping it from rolling away. He did not look round. He had not, as far as Trice knew, looked round since supper on fourth day.

"Well, then," said Bet Haskins.

"Is he all right?" said Trice.

"He's under." Bet Haskins watched the brush. Where it touched the copper the ink went dull almost before it lifted. "Drying on him. Look at that." She said it to herself, not to Trice. Then she said, to Trice, "Don't pull him. He'll come up when he's done, or when he can't go on. Water in his hand every bell. He'll hold a cup if you put one in his fingers; they mostly do. And not bread. He won't chew. Broth, warm, not hot, salt in it. Put the spoon to his lip and he'll take it."

"And if he doesn't?"

Bet Haskins looked at her for the first time. She had pale, unbothered eyes, like water in a pail.

"Then you come and get me," she said, "and don't run on the stair."

That was when Trice wrote *Water every bell* and underlined it.

* * *

On the corridor outside, at the far end by the window, Mistress Wensa had put a chair.

It had appeared on the first afternoon, a plain hall chair with a rush seat, and Wensa sat on it with a ledger on her knee and a pencil, marking first-year accounts. She marked steadily. She did not look up when Trice went past with the water jug, or when Trice came back. She was there in the afternoon and she was there after supper and, according to Rolph, she was there when he went to the privy at the second bell of the night, with a shawl round her and the pencil still going.

"Is she marking," Trice had said, "or is she counting the doors?"

"She's marking," said Rolph. "She's terribly behind. It's very sad."

Trice had let that go. There were some accounts you didn't open in a corridor. She'd known what a chair at the end of a passage meant since before she could read, and so, she'd seen from his face, had Rolph; and neither of them had said so, and neither of them was going to.

* * *

On the fourth afternoon it was her turn, and the room smelled of copper and sour ink and boy.

She'd opened the window first, the way she did every afternoon, and wedged it with a book. The cold snap was a week gone. Outside it was soft and gray and smelled of the sheep field, and somewhere below the wall a lamb was complaining about something. The copper plate Tor had nailed up for the cold still hung over his bed, and on the warm days it did nothing at all that anyone could tell. The plank was under the bed with the dead ones. It was the only thing in the room that wasn't moving.

Tor was moving. He'd been moving for four days.

She stood behind his chair and made the list, because lists were what she did. It was how you watched someone you were going to fight: you put down the habits, one under another, and in the end the column told you where they'd drop their guard. Day one, he'd sat straight. Day two, he'd started leaning to the left, toward the egg-shaped thing, and his left shoulder had come up and stayed up. Day three, he'd stopped blinking as often as people blink; she'd counted. Day four, today, his lips were cracked at the corners, and there was a smear of dried brown resist up the side of his jaw where he'd pushed his hair back with the wrong hand, and his collar had gone dark with sweat and dried and gone dark again. He'd held every cup of water she'd put in his fingers. He'd drunk most of them. Twice he'd put the cup down on the copper, very gently, in the one place on it that wasn't drawn on, and gone on.

He had not eaten anything she could count as food since fourth day.

The broth came up from the kitchens at the fifth bell in a lidded crock, because Hob had been told by Rolph and then by Sara and then by Trice, and had stopped asking. Trice set it on the desk at his elbow, away from the copper, and took the lid off. It was chicken, and barley, strained, with the fat skimmed off and salt in it. Steam came up off it. She tested it on the inside of her wrist the way her nurse used to, and found it too hot, and sat down on the end of his bed with the bowl in her lap to let it cool, and looked at the back of his neck.

"You are," she told it, "a very great deal of trouble."

The hand moved. The brush went in the pot, came out, touched the copper.

"I had to put Rolph on nights," she said. "*Rolph.* Do you know how he sleeps? Like a dropped sack. I came up on the second morning and he was asleep in the chair with his mouth open and your cup was empty and you were holding it out. Just holding it out, Tor. Over the floor. Like a beggar at a gate." She blew on the broth. "I filled it. Obviously. That's not the point."

The brush went in the pot.

"And Sara won't say anything about the rota, which is worse than if she did. And Wensa's got a chair. And Bet Haskins has told the whole staff table, so the building master came up yesterday and stood exactly where I'm sitting and looked at you for ten minutes and then went away again without a word, and Kolb sent a boy to ask whether you'd died, and if not, whether you'd be at practice." She tested the broth again. Better. "I said not. To both."

She got up and pulled the stool round beside his chair and sat on it, close, with the bowl on her knee. Up close, his face was very still and very far off. It had the look Rolph called "the lamp being on," and which Trice, privately, thought looked rather more like a house with all its doors open and nobody home.

"Open," she said, and put the spoon to his lip.

His mouth opened. He took the broth. His throat moved. The brush, in his other hand, never stopped.

Trice sat back slightly.

She had not, she found, quite believed Bet Haskins. She'd thought she would have to coax, or tip, or hold his nose like the nurse with her brother. She hadn't thought he'd simply take it, like a bird in a nest, without any of him coming to the door. It was, she discovered, a thing she didn't much like watching. She did it anyway. She did it eleven times, slowly, a spoon and a wait and a spoon, and talked to him the whole time in a low, even, furious voice that she'd have been ashamed for Sara to hear.

"Four days. *Four.* You can't do this in a chamber, oh no, with a slate and a shutter and a woman who's done it thirty-one years. You do it at a desk. With the window shut. With a door that anyone can walk through, and half the corridor *does*, because you've made them all warm, haven't you, so they come and *look* at you. Open." Spoon. "Do you know what it costs, keeping you alive? I've worked it out. I've done four afternoons. Rolph's done four nights. That's on account. That's on *your* account, Master Baker, and I'm charging interest. Open." Spoon. "And nobody *asked* you to. Nobody needs a man flying by the end of the month. Kolb said *make one*, he didn't say *make one before you've had your supper*—"

On the twelfth spoon, the brush stopped.

It stopped in the air, a finger's width above the copper, and stayed there, and a bead of ink gathered on the tip and didn't fall. Trice held the spoon where it was.

Tor blinked.

It was a slow blink, the first she'd seen in a long while, and after it something came back into his face. Not all at once. It came back the way a room comes back when someone lights a lamp in it: first just the shape of things, then the colors. He swallowed what was in his mouth. He looked at the brush in his hand as though he'd been handed it in the street by a stranger. He put it down. Then he turned his head, very carefully, as if it were heavier than it used to be, and saw the spoon, and the bowl, and her.

"Oh," said Tor. His voice came out cracked and small. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. Was I— were we talking?"

"No," said Trice. "You weren't. Open."

He opened his mouth, out of sheer bewilderment, and she put the spoon in it.

He swallowed. He looked down at himself, at the dark collar and the resist on his hands, and then at the window, at the soft gray light. His brow creased.

"What bell is it?"

"Just past the fifth."

"Oh, that's all right, then." He tried to straighten in the chair, and his left shoulder didn't come down, and his face changed. "Oh. That's— Ow. That's not very— Trice, what *day* is it?"

"The wrong one," she said.

He looked at her.

"You sat down at supper on fourth day," said Trice, "and you've been sitting there since. That's four days, Tor. Four. Rolph's done every night. I've done every afternoon. Bet Haskins came up from the undercroft. Kolb thinks you're dead. Your ink's gone solid, and your water's gone cold, and you've got resist in your hair, and if you'd gone one more day I'd have had to get Davie to help me lift you onto the bed, and *Davie* would have cried. Open."

He opened. He swallowed. He was staring at her.

"And you look," said Trice, "*dreadful*."

"I'm sorry," said Tor faintly.

"Don't be sorry. Be *fed*." She scraped the bowl. There were three spoons left in it, perhaps four. "Nobody asked you to do it all in one go. That's what I keep coming back to. Nobody was waiting. Kolb said *make one*. He didn't say by when. You could have done a bit and gone to bed. You could have done a bit and *eaten something*. You could have—" She stopped, because her voice had gone up, and put the spoon to his lip instead, harder than she'd meant. "Open."

He took it. And then he didn't say anything for a moment, and he didn't turn back to the desk either. He sat with his stiff shoulder and his cracked mouth and looked at her, and his eyes had gone bright, and he blinked again, quickly, twice.

"You stayed," he said.

"It was on the rota."

"You made a *rota*?"

"Somebody had to."

"And you're—" He was looking at her face as though it were a sigil he couldn't quite read. "You're angry. You're angry because I didn't eat."

"I am angry," said Trice, "because you are a great deal of trouble."

"No, but you're—" He stopped. His face did something she'd seen it do in the practice yard, when Kolb's waster was already coming and he'd seen it a little too early: an opening, and then, very fast, a closing. "It's almost," said Tor slowly, "as if you—"

He stopped there.

Trice held the spoon.

She watched him come up to the end of it, the way you watch a horse come up to a gate. She watched him look at it. And then she watched him, quite deliberately, turn aside: his face settled, and softened, and went gentle and grateful and nothing else at all.

"You're a very good friend, Trice," said Tor. "Thank you. Really. That's very kind."

"I am not kind," said Trice. "Ask anyone. Ask Tovey."

"Tovey says you're kind."

"Tovey's my cousin. He's obliged to." She scraped the last of the bowl into the spoon and held it out. "Open."

He ate it. Then, because he was Tor, he said thank you to the spoon, or near enough to it, and then to her again, and then he looked down at the desk and saw the copper, and the egg-shaped thing beside it, and the whole of his face went somewhere else for a moment, out through the open window and up.

"Oh," he said softly. "It's *done*. Trice. I think it's done."

"Good," said Trice. "It can wait."

"But I ought to—"

"It's waited four days. It can wait for you to wash." She put the bowl down on the desk, on the bare corner, and moved the egg-shaped thing to the far side of it with one finger, out of his reach. He watched it go with the expression of a dog watching a bone go into a cupboard. "Wash. Sleep. Eat something with *bits* in. Then you can fly into a tree with my blessing."

He laughed, a cracked, surprised laugh, and winced at his shoulder.

* * *

Rolph came at the sixth bell, with bread and cheese and a jug of milk, and stopped in the doorway.

Tor was asleep. He'd got as far as the bed and no further, and lay on top of the blanket in his stiff clothes with his boots still on and his face turned to the wall, under the copper plate, breathing slowly. The desk was as he'd left it. The copper lay where it had lain, and the small flattened thing on the far side of it, and the crock with its lid off, and the bowl, scraped clean, with the spoon in it.

Trice sat on the stool by the desk with her hands in her lap. She looked up when the door opened.

Rolph looked at the bowl. Then he looked at her face. He looked at it for perhaps a moment longer than he needed to, and then he came in, put the bread and the cheese and the milk down on the desk beside the bowl, and knelt, and started, quietly, to work Tor's boots off.

He didn't say anything. Trice was grateful to him, and filed that too.

She got up and took the rota down off the door, and folded it, and put it in her pocket. On the corridor, at the far end by the window, Wensa turned a page in her ledger. Trice walked past her and down the stair and across the court, where the light was going and the rooks were coming home, and she counted, because she always counted, what was owed.

Four afternoons. Twelve spoons. One sitting-up, which hadn't been on the rota. One *thank you* that had been meant entirely, and one *very good friend* that had been meant entirely too, at the very moment she'd been holding her hand out for something else.

She got as far as the women's wing before she noticed she wasn't adding it up. She was only saying it over.
