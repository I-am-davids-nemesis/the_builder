Before the first bell the room was dark blue. Rolph lay asleep on his back with one arm off the bed and his mouth open.

Tor dressed without lighting a candle. He buckled on his old brown belt, which had a fresh hole bored in it with a knife point. A copper plate a hand and a half wide rode at the small of his back, under his tunic. From the desk he took the small flattened thing the size of a hen's egg, fitted it into his right palm, slipped the loop of cord at one end over his wrist, and pulled it snug. He tugged it twice. Then a third time.

On the bedpost, by its cord, hung the shield. He went out without it.

At the end of the corridor, by the window, stood an empty hall chair with a rush seat. He went past it on his toes.

* * *

The court was gray and wet. The gate was on the latch. Tor slipped through, turned right along the wall, and went down the path through the long grass toward the sheep field. The dew darkened his boots in a dozen steps. Mist lay in the low ground ahead like milk in a saucer.

Halfway down the hill, his right hand flew up and slapped flat against his chest, and stayed there.

He stopped dead. He pressed his hand flatter. He looked down at it.

"Oh, *Tor*," he said.

He stood in the mist a long moment, looking down the hill at the field, and then back up at the school. Then he turned round and went back up.

"Idiot," he said, all the way. "Idiot. You *idiot*. The one thing. The *one* thing. Kolb stood on you for twenty minutes, and you'd go up without it." Through the gate. "You'd go up a *cliff* without it. Into the *sky*." Up the stair two at a time, then the rest of the way on his toes. "You'd deserve it. You'd absolutely deserve it."

Rolph hadn't moved. Tor lifted the shield off the bedpost, put the cord over his head, tucked the plate under his tunic, and laid his hand flat on it and pressed. He stood a moment with his eyes shut.

Then he went out again, on his toes, past the empty chair.

* * *

The sheep field lay below the wall. The mist stood in the bottom of it, and the sheep stood in the mist up to their knees. Lambs were bleating. A ewe looked at Tor and went on chewing.

He walked out into the middle, where the grass was short, and set his feet apart.

"Right," he said.

He pressed his thumb into a hollow on the smooth side of the egg, held it there, and let go.

Nothing happened.

He waited. The ewe chewed. A rook on the wall called and was answered.

"Hm," said Tor.

He turned the egg over, looked at the etched side, and turned it back. He pressed the hollow again, firmly, and let go.

Nothing happened.

He stood quite still. Then his face changed.

"Oh, for—" he said, and pressed it a third time. He stood with his hand held perfectly still in front of him. Nothing happened. His jaw tightened.

"*Come on*," said Tor, and threw both his hands in the air.

He went straight up.

There was no jump and no run. The grass let go of his boots and he went up out of the field as if pulled on a rope, fast, arms over his head, a startled shout trailing out below him. A ring of grass flattened outward from the place he'd stood, like a dropped plate in a pond. The sheep scattered from it in every direction at once. He went up past the top of the wall, past the school roofs, past the top of the bell tower and its weathercock, which was shaped like a fish.

He brought his hands down hard.

He dropped. The field came up at him, the sheep turning from specks back into sheep, his flattened ring rushing up beneath him like a target.

He stopped his hand.

He stopped. He hung in the air above the field, about the height of the chapel roof, perfectly still, arms out in front of him, knees drawn up, mouth open. The ewe directly underneath looked up at him and went on chewing.

He didn't move for a long while. His chest went in and out.

"Slowly," he whispered. "*Slowly*, Tor."

* * *

He moved the egg a hair at a time, the way you'd carry a full cup across a room. He sank a hand's breadth, and stopped. Rose a hand's breadth, and stopped. Shifted his weight and slid sideways through the air like a picture along a wall, and stopped, and said "Sorry" to nobody.

Then, very slightly, he pushed his hand forward.

The field slid away behind him. The wall passed under his boots, then the road, then the hedge beyond it, and he was going gently along over the valley, with the mist lying in its folds below him like wool.

He pushed a little more. And a little more.

The hedges went by faster, then fast, then in stripes. His tunic snapped round him like a flag. His eyes streamed. His mouth was open and he wasn't breathing; he was gulping at the wind, and none of it was going in. He drew his hand back, enough to get a breath.

Then he pushed again, to just short of where his breath stopped, and held it there, eyes screwed up, breathing through his nose in short hard pulls.

Fields, walls, a wood, a stream with the light on it, a tiny man with a tiny cart. Then, over the next hill: roofs.

Hollin.

He slowed by hairs and hung over it in the early light. The chapel with its stubby tower and its lead roof gone silver. The mill and its slow wheel. The old marker stone, a mile out on the road. The well in the square, one side of its stone worn pale. A dog trotting across the square, stopping, sitting down to scratch. Smoke going straight up from four or five chimneys.

"Oh," said Tor, very quietly. "Oh, *look* at you."

* * *

He tried to go home.

He slid backward across the sky, facing Hollin, with Hollin getting smaller in front of him. He stopped.

He moved his hand sideways, and went sideways, still facing Hollin, like a crab. He stopped.

He turned his wrist a little, like turning a key, and turned: once round, and half again, slowly and helplessly, arms and legs out like a spun top, the hills and the sky and Hollin wheeling past him. When he stopped he was facing a hill he'd never seen before. He hung there and swallowed several times.

He did it again, slower. A hair of the wrist, and stop. Another, and stop. The hills crept round him in jerks. He went round the sky over Hollin in a long, wobbling, lopsided circle, too high, then too low, then too far left, a dark shape going round and round above the chapel tower.

Below, in the square, a boy with a bucket at the well stopped and looked up, and stood with his mouth open.

At last the school was in front of Tor, small and far off on its hill, with the sun just coming up behind it and lighting the bell tower.

He laughed, once, out loud.

Then he pushed his hand forward, and didn't stop it.

* * *

He came back over the valley in a long gray blur, eyes streaming, mouth shut. The school came up so fast that he was over the wall before he slowed; he drew his hand back too hard and jolted to a halt over the sheep field. The sheep, just settled back in the middle of it, all looked up at once.

He looked down at them for a long moment.

"Slowly," said Tor. "Slowly, slowly, slowly."

He came down a hand's breadth at a time, stopping after each one, his lips moving.

"Sixty," he said, as high as the wall. "Ninety," as high as a hay cart. "A hundred," as high as Rolph. At a hundred and ten his toes brushed the tops of the wet grass and he stopped dead, and hung there, heels up, for a while. At a hundred and twenty his heels came down, and his knees bent, and he was standing inside his own flattened ring.

He pressed the hollow in the egg.

His knees gave way. He sat down in the wet grass, then lay down in it on his back, arms flung out, the egg still corded to his wrist, and looked up at the sky. Thin, pale, going blue at the top. A few rooks.

Then he laughed.

He laughed out loud, lying in the dew, with his hands full of grass and his eyes running, on and on, until he was holding his ribs. A lamb came up and looked at him from very close, and went away again. There was no one else in the field.

"It works," he told the sky, not loudly. "I went to *Hollin*."

Up on the hill, the bell began to ring the first bell. He lay and listened to it, the stroke and the echo off the chapel roof, until it was done.

Then he got up, brushed himself off, looked down at one boot, which had lost its lace, and went up the hill to breakfast with the lace flapping, saying nothing to anyone.

* * *

In Hollin, at the well, Sam Carter told his mother he'd seen a rook the size of a man go round and round the chapel tower before the first bell, and was sent indoors for lying. By noon the miller had seen it too. By evening it had been a man, then a man with wings, then an Austran, and three people in the Plough swore they'd heard it singing.
