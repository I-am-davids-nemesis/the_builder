A lamp, a desk, a sheet of good paper. Sara's pen moved across it in a fast, even hand.

*Father,*

*The shield passed. You will have heard by now from someone, because half the school has written home about it, and I expect the other half's letters have only been held up by the spelling. I enclose a description. I have not enclosed a price, because you taught me not to put a number in a letter that I couldn't stand behind in a court, and I can't stand behind any number yet. It's too big. I keep getting to the end of it and finding there's more.*

She stopped. She crossed out *It's too big*. She sat looking at it for a moment, then wrote it in again above the line, smaller.

* * *

The morning after the test, Tor was at the building master's door at the sixth bell and at meditation at the seventh. He handed in his theory essay. He ate his lunch. All around him the cafeteria was talking about the wall.

Mistress Wensa sat at the next table. She no longer carried the ledger. The hilt hung on the outside of her dress, where anyone could see it, and in the corridors students stepped round her like water round a stone.

"Good morning, Mistress Wensa," said Tor, quickly and brightly, on his way past. He went past sideways.

"He thinks she's going to murder him," said Trice, at lunch.

"She put the fire out," said Sara.

"He thinks that was so she could do it properly later." Trice ate a grape. "I asked."

* * *

*The shield will change, I should warn you, before it reaches you. He has already made three new ones. He says the first was wrong in four ways, and he has made one for each way but one, and he will put them together when he's sure. The fourth way he says he can't build yet, so he is practising instead, which you will hear about. I asked him how many ways the second one would be wrong in and he said he didn't know yet, and looked very pleased about it.*

In the washing house, Tor sat on an upturned tub in the middle of the floor with a new copper plate on his chest. Davie heaped wet straw onto the boiler fire, stepped out, and slammed the door. He stood outside with Sara, holding it shut with both hands, looking ill.

They counted aloud together. At two hundred, Sara said, "Open it."

Smoke came out in a wall. They both stepped back, coughing. When it thinned, Tor was sitting on the tub inside an egg of clear air, hands on his knees.

"It keeps a bit," he said. "Like a crust keeps the steam in. It's not much, but it's enough to be getting on with. Two hundred, was it? You could have left it longer."

"No," said Sara, "we could not."

* * *

In the practice yard, on a patch of unraked sand, Kolb hit Tor with the iron bar. There were no cracks. The sand sank, very slightly and all at once, in a wide even circle much bigger than his feet.

"Like a cake on a plate," said Tor. "You don't put a cake straight on the table."

Kolb said nothing.

* * *

At supper Tor explained the one he couldn't build. He'd sat up two nights over a shield that would come on by itself, he said, and every way of doing it was worse than having it off. "It would go off for a sneeze. Or a bird. Or Rolph passing me the bread." So he'd have to be quicker instead. His eyes went to Wensa's table, and he lowered his voice. "I'll never be as quick as her. But I could be quicker than I am." He needed someone to practise on him. Without warning.

"Oh," said Trice. "I can do that."

At breakfast the next morning she threw an apple at him from three tables away without looking up from her porridge. It hit him on the ear.

"Ow," said Tor. "Oh! Thank you!"

She threw a boot at him in the corridor before theory; his hand slapped the copper as it arrived, and it bounced off the air and skidded down the flags. A cushion in the library hit him in the face. In combat, a small sack of sand off the pell: his hand hit his chest so fast that Kolb turned at the sound, and the sack struck nothing a hand from his shoulder and burst. Hit or not, each time Tor thanked her and wrote on his wrist. By the third day other students had taken it up, and Tor's hand had started flying to his chest at every sudden noise: a door, a dropped spoon, a sneeze in theory. On the fourth, in the main court, a first-year threw a pear at the back of his head while he stood reading a letter from home. It hit him squarely and burst.

Tor stood dripping. He looked at the letter.

"Still not, then," he said.

He wiped pear out of his collar, wrote on his wrist under the other notes, and drew a box round it.

"That one's not done," he told Sara. "I'll have to think of something else."

Trice threw a spoon at him.

* * *

*I should tell you that I have seen a man walk into a pond and out again dry, with his hands behind his back, and that there were ducks.*

Early on the rest day, mist lay on the pond below the practice yard. Rolph stood on the bank with a towel over his shoulder and a coil of rope. A little apart stood Tovey, in his cloak. Trice. Sara. On the far bank, alone, Mistress Wensa.

Tor touched the copper on his chest and walked into the water.

He went down the muddy slope with his hands behind his back as if he were crossing a field. The water rose to his knees, his waist, his chest, and didn't touch him: it stood back from him in the shape of an egg, curving over the top like a glass dome over a cake, and then closed over his head.

Sara went down the bank to the water's edge.

Under the surface, a small brown blur in a silver bubble walked slowly along the bottom, through the weed. Two ducks paddled over. One put its head under, came up quickly, and the pair of them swam off to the far end of the pond.

Rolph held the rope in both hands. Tovey hadn't moved.

"How long has he got?" said Rolph.

"He said two hundred," said Sara. "In the washing house. He said we could have left it longer."

"Did you?"

"No."

At a hundred and sixty, Tor came up the far side, just below Wensa, out of the water and up the bank inside his bubble. The water fell off it in one sheet. He stood on the grass at her feet, dry, with weed on his boots.

He looked up at her and jumped slightly.

"Good morning, Mistress Wensa," he said.

* * *

*You asked for copies of the food dryer for the house, to sell, and they are packed. Thirty. He did them in an evening. I have also seen him do sixty drying fields in two, and most of them he gave away, and I couldn't stop him, and I have stopped trying. I have written them down. I enclose the list. You'll see the payments.*

The list lay beside the letter. *One pie, mutton. One pair of boots, mended (heel). Socks, two pair, gray, knitted by a sister. Hob in the kitchens: bread, a week. Davie: gratitude (considerable). A first-year: an apple, thrown.* It ran to a page and a half.

In the evenings there was a queue outside Tor and Rolph's door, down the corridor and round the corner to the stair: scholarship boys, younger sons, girls from the far wing with laundry bundles. Tor asked each one what they needed it for, and listened, and made them a drying field. When Sara stood at the door and named a price to a red-eared second-year, Tor came and stood behind her, and looked at her, and she stopped. After that she took a pie instead, and wrote it down.

On the third evening the queue parted down the middle, like wheat in front of a cart, and Kolb stood in the doorway.

"Twenty," he said.

"Twenty, sir?"

"For the school. Shields." He considered. "Twenty-five."

Sara stayed till the tenth bell. Tor sat at the desk with his hand flat on one copper plate after another, lips moving.

"You're just the same as the last one," he told the eighteenth, kindly. "There's no point pretending."

In the morning Kolb collected them in a sack and went off down the corridor with it over his shoulder, clanking like a tinker. An hour later he came back, stood in the doorway, said "Good work," and left before anyone answered.

* * *

*The templates go to you by Haddon's cart on fifth day: shield, drying field, food dryer, all three, under my seal. You'll want to send a builder you trust to take them off the cart, not a clerk. I know you will anyway. I am only saying it so that it's in writing.*

On fifth day a cart came up from Hollin and backed into the yard by the main gate. The carter, a broad man in a leather apron, let down the tailboard.

Tor laid a bed of straw in the bottom of the crate, from the end of a sack: the straw he'd bought for the smoke. He set the three templates on it, each wrapped in oilcloth: a copper plate, a square of blue-painted pine, a plain pale board. He covered them with more straw and tucked it in at the corners. Haddon nailed down the lid. Sara heated a stick of wax over a candle, let it drip on the join, and pressed her ring into it.

Tor stood with his hands in his pockets, looking at the crate.

Haddon swung it up into the cart and lashed it down, climbed up and took the reins. The cart went out through the gate and down the hill toward Hollin, rocking in the ruts. Tor watched it all the way down until the road bent round the trees.

"They'll be all right," said Sara.

"I know." He kept looking at the bend in the road. "It's silly. It's just a board. I can make another." He took his hands out of his pockets. "It's like sending bread to market. When you're little. You stand at the door and watch the cart go, and it's only bread, and you'll make more tomorrow. But it's *your* bread."

Under the gate arch, Mistress Wensa had watched the cart too, until it was gone. She looked at Tor for a moment. Then she went back inside.

* * *

*As to the money. I have told him what the house proposes and he has agreed to it, and I believe he heard about one word in five. He asked me to see to it, and Rolph and Trice to help, and said he was sure it would be fine. He trusts us completely. I would like it noted, Father, that I find this the most frightening part of the whole business.*

On the last evening Sara held out a cloth bag, heavy and clinking. Tor looked at it as though it might bite.

Then he knelt and pulled a box out from under his bed: plain oak, small, iron at the corners, no ornament at all.

"I got it at the store," he said. "It came with a lock and a key. But I took the lock off. It wasn't very good. Anyone could have opened it with a hairpin."

He laid his hand flat on the lid. It clicked.

Sara's eyes went across the room to the foot of Rolph's bed, to Rolph's lockbox. Rolph, on his bed with a book, didn't look up. He smiled at the page.

Inside, the box was half full of little cloth bags tied at the neck, each with a scrap of paper tied to it in a round, careful hand. *Mother.* *Terlee.* *The others, for shoes.* *Davie's mother (if needed).* And in the corner, by itself, the smallest: *Spare*.

Tor put the new bag in beside the others. He knelt there looking down into the box.

"I don't know what to write on it," he said.

Sara looked at the bags for a long moment. She didn't count them.

"Write *yours*," she said.

Tor took the stub of pencil from his tunic, tore a corner off a page of theory notes, wrote on it with his back to her, and tied it to the bag. He closed the box and laid his hand on the lid until it clicked.

That night, under the lamp, Sara finished the letter.

*He has a box now, Father, with a lock of his own making. I think you'd like him. I think he would be terrified of you, and I think he would bring you bread.*

She read it over twice. Her pen hovered over the last line. Then she put the pen down and left it in.
