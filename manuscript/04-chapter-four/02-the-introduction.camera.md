The dining hall at supper was loud and steamy, and smelled of fish. Rain had been falling since the afternoon, the first warm rain of the spring, and it ran down the tall windows in sheets.

At the long table by the kitchen door, Tor Baker sat bent over his plate, with Rolph across from him. At the end of the next table, back to the wall, Mistress Wensa ate her fish one exact forkful at a time, and looked at nothing in particular, and her eyes came back, every few forkfuls, to Baker.

Three tables away, Dorgal Sorvey was cutting his own fish into very even pieces.

"Baker's sweetheart," said the large fourth-year beside him, with his mouth full, "has had the fish."

"Has she, Marco," said Dorgal, not looking round.

"She's had the fish," said Marco again, and laughed.

"Do you suppose," said Dorgal, "that he's told his mother?"

Marco laughed so hard he had to put down his fork.

On Dorgal's other side sat a third man, who hadn't laughed and hadn't eaten. His plate was untouched. He sat with his hands folded on the table in front of it, very still. He wore the school brown like everyone else, but he was too old for it, though not by much: twenty-five, perhaps. He had a long, quiet face that would have passed in any crowd, except for his eyes. Where the whites should have been, his eyes were black, a wet, even black like ink in a well, and the irises sat in the middle of them, gray and ordinary and calm.

He was looking at Baker too.

"He's getting up," said Marco.

Baker was stacking his plate on Rolph's, and the cups on top, and carrying them to the hatch. He said something to Rolph, who nodded and stayed where he was, with half his supper still in front of him. Baker went to the door.

At the next table, Mistress Wensa put down her fork. She didn't get up. Her eyes went past the door where Baker was, to the far end of the hall. She stayed where she was, sitting very straight, with her hand flat beside her plate.

Dorgal looked at her for a moment. Then he wiped his mouth and stood.

"Gentlemen," he said. "I think we ought to give the bridegroom our congratulations."

* * *

The passage from the dining hall ran down to the kitchen yard, and the whole length of one wall was pegs, and every peg had a wet coat on it, steaming. There was a lamp at each end. The middle was dim and close and smelled of wool.

Baker had nearly reached the yard door.

"My dear Baker," said Dorgal.

Baker stopped and turned. Dorgal stood in the middle of the passage, Marco a step behind him on the left, filling it, and the third man on the right, just at the edge of the lamplight, where his eyes didn't show.

Baker put his hands together in front of him.

"Good evening," he said. "Master Sorvey."

"We've been admiring," said Dorgal, coming a step nearer, "your romance."

"I'm sorry?"

"Oh, don't be modest. The whole school's talking about it. Breakfast, lunch and supper. The yard. The library. The workroom at the eleventh bell." Dorgal laid a hand on his heart. "It's touching, Baker, honestly. We're all touched. A lady of her years, and on the faculty, too, walking out with a third-year. Following him about like a governess with a very slow child. Does she hold your hand on the stairs?"

Marco laughed.

"I don't," said Baker carefully, "have a— I'm not walking out with anybody. Master Sorvey. I think there's been a mistake."

"No mistake. We've all seen her. The accounts mistress." Dorgal smiled. "I'll admit, when I heard you'd found yourself a sweetheart, she wasn't who I pictured. But then I thought, well. Be fair. He's a baker's boy. And she's a schoolmistress. She's practically marrying up."

Marco liked that one very much.

Baker didn't laugh, or flush. He stood among the coats with his hands together and looked at Dorgal steadily, with a kind of careful, puzzled attention, like a man trying to read a sign in a language he almost knows.

Dorgal's smile held. His eyes went, for a moment, to his right. The third man hadn't moved. He was watching Baker, and his gray irises in their black were steady and interested.

"Still," said Dorgal, "I suppose a man in your position takes what he's offered. Isn't that right? After the last one." He let it hang. "What was her name? Maria, wasn't it. Is it Maria? I heard she screamed the place down. I heard you hid in your room for a month."

He said it lightly, with a little lift at the end, like the end of a joke. Then he stood quite still. His face kept smiling.

Marco had stopped laughing.

Baker had gone white, from his hair to his collar. His hands came apart, and then he put them back together, one inside the other.

Then he looked past them all, down the passage, to the open door of the dining hall, with its light and noise and the clatter of plates.

"Oh," said Baker quietly.

He looked back at Dorgal.

"Would you excuse me a moment?" he said. "I won't be long. I'm sorry."

He came forward. Dorgal stepped aside. So did Marco. Baker walked between them, politely, not hurrying, keeping to the exact middle of the passage, and went back into the dining hall.

Marco looked at Dorgal.

"Is he coming back?"

"Interesting," said Dorgal.

* * *

He came back. He came down the passage between the coats with Mistress Wensa at his side, and she had her ledger under her arm.

Dorgal straightened his tunic.

Wensa didn't look at Baker as she walked, or at Marco, or at Dorgal. She looked at the third man. Her eyes went over him once, from his boots to his face, and stopped on his face, and stayed. Her shoulders settled.

The third man looked back at her with mild interest.

Baker stopped in the middle of the passage, halfway between them, and turned so that he faced both sides. He was still pale. He put his hands together and spoke clearly and slowly, like a boy reciting in front of the parson.

"Mistress Wensa," he said, "this is Master Dorgal Sorvey, who would like to frighten me. And these are his friends. I don't know their names, I'm afraid. They're helping him."

Marco opened his mouth and shut it.

"Master Sorvey," said Baker, turning, "this is Mistress Wensa, of the royal guard, who is following me." He hesitated. "You wanted to know why. I don't really know, I'm afraid. I think it's better if she tells you herself. She'll be able to explain it much better than I could."

Nobody said anything. Somewhere along the wall a coat dripped onto the flags.

Wensa's face did not change at all. Her hand was not at her belt, quite. It was near it. On the belt hung a plain hilt, cord-wrapped, a hand and a half long.

Dorgal's eyes went to the hilt, and stayed on it.

"Well," said Baker. "Good night."

He bowed, once, to all of them equally, and turned and walked away down the passage toward the kitchen yard.

* * *

He walked steadily between the coats, not hurrying, not looking back. Behind him, nobody spoke. He reached the yard door and put his hand on the latch.

Then he stopped.

He stood with his hand on the latch. His shoulders rose, slowly, almost to his ears.

"Oh, *Tor*," he said to the door, very quietly.

He opened it and went out into the rain.

The kitchen yard was dark and running with water. The door banged shut behind him, and the sound of the passage was gone. Baker crossed the yard fast, much faster than he had been walking, splashing through the puddles with his shoulders still up, past the woodpile and the scullery window and the overflowing water butt, and under the arch toward the dormitories. Under the arch he stopped, out of the rain, and looked back at the kitchen door.

It stayed shut. No light showed round it.

He looked at it for some time. Water dripped off the arch in front of him in a steady line.

"She'll have told them anyway," he said. "Probably. She'd have had to. Wouldn't she."

The door stayed shut.

"Think, Tor," he told himself. Then, after a moment, unhappily: "No. Don't."

He turned up his collar and went on toward the dormitories, walking quickly, and didn't look back again.
