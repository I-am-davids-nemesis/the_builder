The window was still gray when the bar lifted. Tor stood in the doorway, dressed, satchel on his shoulder.

"I'm going down to the chambers," he said. "It might be two days. I'm sorry."

"What might?" said Rolph, into his pillow.

The door closed. Footsteps went away down the corridor, quick and light, and down the stair. Rolph lay still for a while. Then he rolled over and looked at the desk, which was covered in paper, a cup, his own theory notes and a candle stub.

"Oh, *Baker*," he said, to the ceiling.

* * *

The undercroft lay under the old wing, at the bottom of a stair worn into a dip in the middle of every step. It was cool and dry and smelled of stone and lamp oil. There were six oak doors along the passage, each with a slate on a nail beside it. Five slates were clean. The sixth said, in round, careful chalk:

*BAKER, T. — In: first bell, rest day.*

Across from it stood a bench. Mistress Wensa sat at one end, her ledger on her knee.

Rolph came down the stair at the fourth bell with a tray: bread, a heel of ham, a jug of milk, two apples. At the bottom he stopped.

Wensa looked up at him, briefly, and then past him, up the stair.

Rolph's jaw tightened. His hands tightened on the tray. Then both loosened.

"Morning," he said.

He set the tray on the floor by Tor's door and knocked once. Nothing happened. He sat down on the other end of the bench.

They sat. Far above them, the bell rang the fifth hour.

A small, square, gray woman in an apron came along the passage with a lamp. She stopped at Tor's door, put her eye to a little shutter set in the oak at head height, looked for the space of three breaths, and closed it. She took the chalk from the ledge under the slate and made a mark. Then she looked at the tray, and at Rolph, and at Wensa, and at Rolph again.

"He won't eat that," she said.

"I know," said Rolph. "It's for when he stops."

"Well, then." She looked at the two of them on the bench a moment longer, expressionless, and went on down the passage.

The bell rang the sixth hour. Wensa turned no pages.

"He hasn't done anything, you know," said Rolph, to the opposite wall. "He hasn't done one single thing."

"I'm aware of what he has done," said Wensa.

Rolph turned and looked at her. She was looking at Tor's door. Her hand lay flat on the ledger. Rolph opened his mouth, shut it again, got up and went back up the stair.

* * *

At supper the hall was full of it. "Baker's in the chambers." "Since the first bell." "Haskins told the kitchen." "He's making shields for the whole school." "He's making one for the *king*." At Dorgal Sorvey's table, nobody was talking. Dorgal ate with his eyes on his plate, and the large fourth-year beside him had a full plate in front of him and didn't touch it.

At the tenth bell the tray was still on the floor by the chamber door. The bread had gone hard and the milk had a skin on it. Both apples sat exactly where they'd been put. Rolph took it away and put down a fresh one. The slate had twelve chalk marks on it in a neat row.

Bet Haskins came along with the lamp.

"Is he all right?" said Rolph.

She put her eye to the shutter and looked for longer this time.

"He's all right," she said. "He's working." She closed the shutter and made her mark, and stood a moment turning the chalk in her fingers. "Ink's drying on him. Drying as he puts it down. I've seen that a dozen times. Thirty-one years." She put the chalk back on its ledge. "Go to bed, young man."

* * *

Before the first bell of the next day, a class day, Rolph came down again. Wensa was already on the bench. Neither spoke. The slate had twenty-three marks.

At a quarter to the bell, Bet Haskins came along the passage and, this time, knocked softly and opened the door.

Rolph was on his feet.

The chamber was small and bare, with one lamp on a shelf. Tor sat on the stool with his back to the door. In front of him lay a square of copper the size of a book, and both his hands were flat on it, and he didn't move. Around him, on the bench and the floor, lay sheets of paper dense with dark marks on both sides, a pot of resist, a stoppered acid jar and a stack of bare copper plates. The air that came out of the room was warm and close, and smelled of a copper kettle boiled dry.

"Mr. Baker," said Bet Haskins. "It's the class day."

Tor didn't turn round or lift his hands. After a moment he spoke, softly, in a perfectly ordinary voice.

"Rolph?"

"Here."

"Would you go and see the building master? Would you ask him to excuse me? For today. I'm sorry. I'd go, only I can't stop just here." A pause. "Tell him I'm sorry."

Rolph stood in the doorway with his mouth open.

"Yes," he said. "Yes, of course. Of course I will."

"Thank you," said Tor, to the copper.

Bet Haskins closed the door. Rolph went on looking at it.

"Well, then," said Bet Haskins.

* * *

The building master's office smelled of linseed and cold ashes. He stood at the window with a board tipped up to the light, and didn't put it down when Rolph knocked.

"Yes?"

"Sir. I've come about Tor. About Baker, sir. He's in the chambers, sir, he's been there since the first bell yesterday, he's building, he hasn't stopped once, Mistress Haskins says the ink's drying on him, and he asked me, sir, he *asked*, he said would I come and see you and ask if he could be excused today, and to say he's sorry, and he is, sir, he really is, he'd come himself only he can't stop just where he is, and I know it's a class day, sir, but if you'd seen him, honestly, it's not that he doesn't want to come, he's just, he's busy being—"

The building master turned and looked at him over the top of the board.

"He asked?"

"Yes, sir."

The building master went on looking at him for some time. Then he put the board down, took a slip of paper from the desk, wrote two lines on it, signed it and held it out.

"Haskins told the staff table at breakfast," he said. "Yesterday's breakfast. And today's." He picked up his board again. "Give that to Doris. She'll pass it round. And tell Baker that Master Kolb's test is still the twelfth, and that Master Kolb will not have heard about any of this."

"Yes, sir. Thank you, sir."

"Young man."

Rolph turned in the doorway.

"Busy being what?" said the building master, to the board.

Rolph thought about it.

"I don't know, sir," he said. "I don't think there's a word for it."

The building master made a sound that might have been a cough, and tipped the board to the window. Rolph went.

* * *

At the eleventh bell of the second night, Rolph lay on his bed with a book open on his chest, not reading. Footsteps came up the stair. He was off the bed and across the room before the door was fully open.

"Tor. Listen. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I told you to take your time. I *said* take your time, I said it twice, and you went and did it anyway, two days, in the chambers, for a *fruit dryer*, Tor, for *fruit*, because I went on about it, didn't I, I went on and on, and you can't even sit down without—"

He stopped.

Tor stood just inside the door, swaying a little, smiling. He had two days of beard and a black smear across one cheekbone. He held his hands out in front of him, cupped together. Cords hung from his fingers, and on the cords hung small copper plates, and they clinked.

"Ten," said Tor. "It's done, Rolph. They're done. It's the shield."

Rolph looked at the copper.

"The shield," he said.

"For Master Kolb. And for— Well. Ten." Tor frowned. "What fruit dryer?"

"The—" Rolph waved a hand. "The one I asked for. The food one. For the crops. You said you'd do it, and I said take your time, and you went down there before the bell, and I thought—"

Tor began to laugh. He was too tired to laugh much, but it shook him, and he leaned on the door frame, and the shields clinked. He lifted one hand, with three of them swinging from it, and pointed past Rolph at the desk.

Rolph turned round.

On the desk, under a sheet of Rolph's theory notes and a cup, lay a plain square of pale, unpainted wood with a sigil drawn on it in close dark lines. When Rolph lifted the cup, there was a milk ring under it.

"That's the food dryer," said Tor. "The template. I did it on fourth day. Before the shield. I thought you'd seen it."

Rolph looked at the board for a long time. He looked at the ring on it. He put the cup down again, very carefully, on the windowsill.

"Fourth day," he said.

"I was going to say. But you were writing to your mother. And then there was the wax."

"It's been on the desk," said Rolph, "for three days."

"Two and a half," said Tor helpfully, and laughed again, and slid down the door frame until he was sitting on the floor with the shields in his lap.

* * *

Rolph came back from the kitchens with half a loaf, a lump of cheese, a jug of water and a bowl of cold stew. Tor was where he'd left him, on the floor against the door, asleep sitting up.

Getting him to the bed took some time. Tor kept waking halfway, apologizing, and trying to help. At last he sat on the edge of the bed, and Rolph held the cup while he drank all of it, and another. Then Rolph tore the bread into pieces and handed them to him one at a time, and Tor ate each one and said thank you for each one.

"Who are they for?" said Rolph. "Ten."

"People," said Tor, chewing. "Master Kolb's. And people who might need one." He looked at the shields in a heap on the end of the bed, catching the lamplight. "I'll say. When I've— I'll say."

Rolph knelt and started on his boots.

"You asked me," he said, to the laces. "This morning. To go to the building master. You know that? You asked."

"Did he mind?"

"No. He didn't mind."

"Good," said Tor. "I didn't know if you were allowed. To ask. If somebody'd—" He yawned enormously. "I thought, Rolph won't mind. Rolph knows how. And you did, didn't you. You went. Thank you."

The second boot came off. Rolph set the pair side by side.

"Tor," he said. "Of course I went."

Tor was asleep, sitting up, with a piece of bread in his hand.

Rolph took the bread, laid him down and pulled the blanket over him. He gathered the ten shields off the end of the bed by their cords and stood holding them in the middle of the room for a while. Then he went to the lockbox at the foot of his own bed, laid his hand flat on the lid until it clicked, and put them in on top of the gold, and shut it.

Last, he went to the desk. He lifted his notes off the food dryer, wiped the milk ring away with his sleeve, and stood the board up on its edge against the wall, where it could be seen.
