For two days after Kolb's challenge, Tor sat at the desk and drew on cheap gray paper from the store, very fast. He drew, looked, crossed it out, drew it again a little differently, and crossed that out too. He went to classes and came back and drew. The pile of crossed-out sheets on the floor by his chair grew to the height of a boot, then a shin.

On the second evening Rolph came in from accounts and kicked the pile over. He spent a quarter of an hour on his knees putting it back.

"Sorry," he said.

"Mm," said Tor.

"Any good?"

Tor held one up without looking round. It was covered in little arrows. Some went up. Some went round in circles. One, near the bottom, went up, and beside it was a very small drawing of a man with his arms out, looking surprised.

"Three," said Tor. "Three that might. The rest are pushing. It can't be pushing. He wasn't pushing. He was just—" He lifted one hand flat, like a man raising a tray. "Up."

"Right," said Rolph, and lay down on his bed with his boots off.

The window stood open. It was the warmest week of the spring so far; the room smelled of wet grass from the sheep field, and in the evenings the lambs could be heard. Rolph's heavy coat lay folded in the chest at the foot of his bed, on top of the lockbox.

* * *

On the third day, at the ninth bell, Rolph opened the door and stopped.

There was a plank on the ceiling.

It was an arm's length of pale pine, one of the offcuts from under Tor's bed, pressed flat against the plaster in the middle of the ceiling. Nothing held it there. It hummed faintly, like a bee in a jar. Fine white plaster dust sifted down from its edges onto the desk, the floor, and Tor.

Tor stood on his bed with both arms stretched straight up, hanging from the plank by his fingertips, heels just off the blanket, trying to pull it down.

It wasn't coming down.

Rolph stood in the doorway and said nothing for a long time. Tor swayed slightly.

"Hello," said Tor, from up there. "Sorry. I'll just be a moment."

"Take your time," said Rolph.

"It's not very strong," said Tor breathlessly. "I only spent a few minutes on it. So I *should* be able to—" He hauled. The plank stayed where it was. His heels came back down onto the blanket. "There. It's coming. It's definitely coming."

It was not coming.

Rolph came in and shut the door. He went and stood under the plank and braced his feet.

"Get on my shoulders."

"Are you sure? I don't want to—"

"Tor. Get on."

Tor climbed on, with a knee in Rolph's ear and a boot on his collarbone, apologizing. Rolph staggered, steadied, and took hold of his ankles. Tor reached up, got both hands round the plank, and pulled.

The plank came down an inch at a time, humming louder, like a dog on a lead that has seen a cat. Tor brought it down hand over hand to his chest. Rolph knelt. Tor slid off onto the floor, and the two of them sat side by side on the boards in the plaster dust, with the plank across Tor's lap, held down with both his hands, straining gently upward.

"Is it meant to do that?" said Rolph.

"Yes," said Tor. "Well. Partly." His knuckles were white on the wood. "It works. Rolph, it *works*, it goes up, it just goes up by itself, there's nothing under it, it's not pushing on anything at all. Look at it."

"I'm looking at it."

"It only goes *up*," said Tor.

There was a short silence.

"Well," said Rolph, "what did you want it to do?"

"Everything *else*."

* * *

On the fourth day there was a plank wedged into the far corner of the ceiling above the window, tilted, with a half-brick tied to one end of it on a string. It had skidded along the plaster and stuck. It stayed there all day and all night. In the dark, Rolph lay on his back looking up at it.

"It's weakening," said Tor, from the other bed. "By morning, I should think. I'm sorry."

"It's fine."

"If the string goes, the brick'll come down on your chest."

Rolph got up and dragged the chest away from under it.

* * *

On the fifth day, from the stair, there was a crack and a bang and Tor's voice: "*Sorry!*" In the room, a long silver crack ran corner to corner across the lower windowpane. Two planks lay on the floor side by side like a pair of boots, quite still. Tor sat between them with his head in his hands.

On the sixth, a card, a strip of copper and a bird's feather were tied in turn to the back of a plank. Each hung off the end and twirled. The plank went up and stayed up.

On the seventh, Rolph's boots, which had been by the door, stood three feet up the wall, toes pointing at the ceiling, side by side. They stayed there for an hour. Tor apologized to them at intervals.

By the end of it there was a scorch mark on the corner of the desk in the shape of a hand, a sheet of paper pasted over the broken pane, a ring of plaster dust on the floor that sweeping didn't shift, and three dead planks under Tor's bed. Tor had stopped apologizing. He sat at the desk for long stretches with his hands flat on the wood, looking at nothing.

"Supper," said Rolph, and put it in front of him.

* * *

Bread, soup and a pot of tea. Tor ate the bread without looking at it. He poured his tea, put in sugar, and stirred.

He stopped, with the spoon in the cup.

The tea was going round after the spoon, a slow brown swirl carrying a few leaves. Tor moved the spoon, very slightly, the other way. The swirl slowed, turned, and followed it.

He lifted the spoon out and held it up in front of his face. He turned it over in his fingers, and back. Then he went perfectly still. A drop of tea gathered on the end of the spoon and fell into the cup.

Rolph, across the desk with his own soup, had stopped eating.

"Rolph," said Tor.

"Mm."

"What if it wasn't on the board?"

"What wasn't?"

"The telling. Which way to go." He kept looking at the spoon. "I keep trying to put the telling on the plank. The weight, the tail, the second field, all of it. All of it's on the plank. But the tea doesn't know which way to go. The spoon knows. And you hold the spoon." He set it down on the saucer as carefully as if it were glass. "What if you *held* it? Something small. In your hand. And it tells the big one. And you just—" He moved his hand, flat and slow, up, forward and round. "You just *tell* it."

Rolph looked at the hand, and then at Tor's face.

"Tor," he said.

"Mm?"

"Eat your soup first."

Tor looked down at the soup. He picked up the same spoon, the tea spoon, and began to eat with his left hand. His right hand went to the stack of gray paper and the pen, and began, very fast, to draw.

Rolph watched him for a while. Then he got up and went to the door, where his boots had come down off the wall that afternoon, and set them straight. He looked up at the corner above the window, where the half-brick plank had left a long pale scrape in the plaster.

He went back and finished his soup.
