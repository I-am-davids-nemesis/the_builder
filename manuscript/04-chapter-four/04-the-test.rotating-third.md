At lunch Tor was making a will again, and Sara could hear every word of it from the next table.

He wasn't loud. Tor was never loud. But the cafeteria had gone quiet around him the way a room goes quiet around a man with a lit candle in a hayloft, everyone eating a little more slowly than usual and looking at their plates, and his voice carried.

"—and the drying field money goes to my mother," he was telling Rolph. "Miss Sara knows the amounts. And the food dryer's yours, the template, you know where it is. And the ten in your box. Whoever Master Kolb says. And you'll tell him, won't you, that it wasn't his fault? Afterward. I don't want him to feel—"

"Tor," said Rolph.

"He'll feel it. He's not as hard as he looks. You'll tell him I said thank you, for the running. Everything else too. But the running mostly."

"*Tor.*"

"I'm only saying, in case."

Sara looked at Trice. Trice was buttering a roll with great precision and had, Sara saw, stopped listening to anything else in the room.

"He's very calm," said Trice, "about a pell."

"He's always calm," said Sara. "That's how you can tell he isn't."

She looked back. Tor had finished his soup. He was folding his napkin into a small, exact square, and he put it beside the bowl and sat looking at it, with his hands flat on the table on either side, as if it were the last napkin he expected to fold. Rolph was watching him with an expression Sara had seen before on men at her father's warehouse, when a cart had gone into the river with a load nobody had insured.

She did the sum without meaning to. She always did. It was the first thing her father had taught her and the last thing she'd ever be able to unlearn: the board, then the work, then the years. She didn't know yet what the board was. She knew what the work had cost, because Bet Haskins had told the whole kitchen: two days in the chambers, with the ink drying as he laid it down. And she knew what a shield device cost in the glass halls on the river, if it was good, if it was the kind that would turn a sword and last more than a season. She had been nine when her father showed her one. He'd let her hold it for the space of a breath and then taken it back, and told her what he'd paid, and she had not believed him.

"Come on," said Trice. "If we want to see anything, we'll have to be early."

* * *

They weren't early. Half the school was early.

The practice yard was full. There were students along the weapon racks three deep, and students sitting on the racks, and a row of first-years on the roof of the equipment shed with their legs hanging over, until Kolb looked at them, and then there weren't. Nobody stood near the old wall at the far end. It was the one wall in the school that belonged to something older than the school, great close-fitted blocks the color of dried bread, and the pell stood in front of it on its post, and there was a wide empty space of packed sand around the pell, and everyone had arranged themselves round the edge of it as if it were a stage.

The faculty had the bench by the water butt. The headmaster was in the middle of it, gray and spare in his long gown, with his spectacles pushed up on his forehead and his hands folded on top of his stick. The building master stood at one end, with his arms crossed. Mistress Wensa stood at the other, not sitting, with her ledger under her arm and her other hand loose at her side, and Sara looked at the hand, and at the belt, and at the way she stood, and remembered Trice saying, in the first week of term, *That woman doesn't sit like a mistress of accounts.* Sara had laughed at the time. Nobody else on the bench seemed to find it odd that the accounts mistress had come to watch a combat test, or that she hadn't sat down.

Tovey was at the front of the crowd, in his dark cloak with the brooch, and a little space had opened round him, the way it always did. He nodded to them as they came. Sara and Trice went and stood beside him, and the space made room.

"Patricia," said Tovey. "Miss Debri."

"Tovey," said Trice.

"He's late," said Tovey.

"He's never late," said Sara. "Look."

He was coming down the steps from the long hall with Rolph at his side, and a little trail of students behind them, and when he reached the bottom and saw the yard, he stopped dead.

Sara watched him take it in. The racks, the shed, the bench with the headmaster on it, the hundred faces turned toward him. She watched him swallow. Then he came on, across the sand, and Rolph came with him as far as the edge of the crowd and no further, and stood there with his fists in his pockets. Tor walked out alone into the empty space.

Kolb was waiting by the pell. He had a practice weapon over his shoulder, a long, heavy, iron-shod thing like a fence post that had ideas, and the cutter's hilt on his belt.

Tor bowed to him.

"Thank you, sir," he said, quite clearly, so that it carried to the racks. "For everything. I'm ready."

Kolb looked at him a little oddly.

Tor took the shield out of his tunic. Sara saw it for the first time: a plate of copper, the size of a man's palm, etched all over in close dark lines, on a cord. Just copper. Just a cord. He put it over his head. His hand went to it—

And Mistress Wensa was already moving.

Sara never saw her take the rod from her belt. It was simply in her hand, short and black, and she had crossed half the distance to Tor and leveled it at him before his fingers had found the copper. Someone on the racks yelled. Tor's hand closed on the plate.

Nothing happened.

There was a sound like a breath being held. The air in front of Tor shivered, as air shivers over a road in summer, and he rocked back half a step on his heels, and that was all. He stood there with his hand on his chest, blinking.

Wensa turned on her heel and pointed the rod at the pell.

The pell exploded.

There was no other word for it. It went from a post to a cloud of splinters between one moment and the next, with a crack like the sky breaking, and the splinters went up and out and came pattering down over the sand and the front row of the crowd, and a first-year sat down hard, and somebody's hat went into the water butt.

In the silence, Sara heard Tovey let out his breath.

Wensa put the first rod away and took out a second. She pointed it at Tor. Nothing happened, again; Tor's sleeves stirred as though a wind had passed him, and the sand round his boots went smooth. She turned and pointed it at the stump of the pell, at what was left, and beyond it the old wall bowed.

It bowed outward, away from them, in a long slow curve like a sail filling, and the great blocks ground against each other with a sound Sara felt in her teeth, and a crack ran up the middle of it from the sand to the coping, zigzag, branching, and stopped.

"That wall," said the building master, to nobody, "has been there four hundred years."

"Yes," said the headmaster, mildly. "It has."

Along the racks the whispering had started, and Sara could hear it go from mouth to mouth like a dropped coin rolling. *Those are guard rods.* *Royal guard.* *The accounts mistress?* *Wensa?* Sara knew what a guard's rod looked like; every merchant's daughter who had ever stood in a counting house with something worth stealing in it knew. She hadn't thought to see three of them come out of a schoolmistress's belt. Beside her, Trice said nothing at all, in the particular way Trice said nothing when she had been right.

The third rod was red.

Wensa leveled it at Tor and fire came up out of the sand all round him.

It came up in a ring, waist high, then head high, roaring, yellow at the edges and white at the heart, and the heat of it hit Sara across the whole width of the yard like an oven door opening. The crowd went back a step, all together. Through the flames she could see him. He was standing in the middle of it, in a clear space the shape of an egg, and the fire was pressing on the outside of the egg and curling round it, and he wasn't burning. He wasn't burning at all.

Then she saw his hand go to his throat.

She saw him take a breath. A big one, the kind you take before you go under water. And hold it. She saw him look up at the roaring wall of fire, and round, and his lips moved, and she knew him well enough by now to know he was saying something to himself, and she knew what it was, because she'd done the sum herself a moment after he had. The fire was eating the air. And the field let air through. And there was going to be none left to let.

She was moving before she'd thought about it. She'd got three steps out onto the sand when Trice caught her by the back of the collar, hard, and held on.

"*Wait*," said Trice in her ear.

Wensa flicked her wrist.

The fire went out. It didn't die down; it went out, all at once, like a lamp, and left a ring of black glassy sand round Tor, smoking, and in the middle of it Tor, with his hand on his throat and his cheeks puffed out like a boy at a birthday.

He let the breath go. He took another, and another, gasping, and bent over with his hands on his knees.

"Thank you, ma'am," he said, between breaths, to Mistress Wensa. "Thank you very much."

Wensa put the red rod back on her belt. She looked at the boy in the ring of black sand, and at the shield on his chest, and Sara, who had spent her whole childhood watching people's faces as they looked at things they wanted to buy, saw her face change. It was very slight. It was the face of a woman who has opened a letter she was afraid of, and read the first line, and found that the news was good.

Sara let out her own breath. She found that Trice still had hold of her collar, and that Trice's hand was shaking, and she didn't say anything about it.

* * *

"My turn," said Kolb.

He swung the practice weapon down off his shoulder and walked out into the ring.

Tor straightened up. He looked at Kolb, and at the great iron-shod length of wood, and his face went very calm and very polite, and he put his hands behind his back.

"Yes, sir," he said.

Kolb hit him.

He hit him the way Sara had seen him hit the pell on the day he set the assignment, a blow that shook the yard, and the field took it with a sound like a fist on a door, and Tor rocked on his heels. Kolb hit him again. And again. He hit him from the left and the right and straight down from above, as if he were driving a stake, and the sound went on and on, *thock, thock, thock*, and after the first minute Sara stopped flinching, and after the second the crowd began to talk again.

After five minutes, people were sitting down.

After eight, a second-year went to the kitchens and came back with a jug of water and some cups. After ten, there was quite open betting along the racks, though nobody could agree what they were betting on. Kolb was sweating. His bald head shone with it. He was grinning, a fixed, fierce, happy grin that Sara had never seen on him before, and he didn't stop. In the middle of it, Tor stood with his hands behind his back and took every blow, and rocked, and said, every so often, very politely, "Sorry, sir," or "Are you all right, sir? Would you like a rest?" and Kolb said, "*No*," and hit him again.

At fifteen minutes there was a new sound.

Sara heard it before she understood it: a thin dry crunch under the thock of the blows. Tor heard it too. He looked down. The packed sand round his boots had gone hard and gray and flat, pressed down like a threshing floor, and under it something was giving, and as Kolb's next blow came down a crack ran out from under Tor's left heel across the sand, an arm's length, and then another from under his right.

Tor looked at the cracks for a long moment, with his head on one side. Then he looked up and said something to himself, and Sara saw his lips shape it. *Ground.*

At twenty minutes Kolb stepped back.

He was breathing hard. He dropped the practice weapon in the sand, and wiped his face with his forearm, and stood looking at Tor, who stood looking back at him, in his ring of black sand with the cracks running out of it, without a mark on him.

Then Kolb drew his cutter.

The crowd went quiet so fast that Sara heard the water butt dripping. The blade was simply there, four feet of dull gray that the light slid off, and Kolb didn't pause and didn't warn anyone. He swung it at Tor's neck, flat and fast, one-handed, like a man cutting a switch.

It stopped.

It stopped a hand from the copper, in the air, and stayed there, as if it had come to the end of itself. Kolb held it. Tor stood very still, looking at it out of the corner of his eye, with his chin up.

And then Kolb began to laugh.

He laughed the way Sara had heard him laugh once before, a fortnight ago, when they'd complained that Tor had a shield: a big, sudden, delighted bark that went right across the yard and bounced off the cracked wall. He let the blade go. He stood there in the middle of the sand with the empty hilt in his hand, and laughed until he had to put the other hand on his knee.

"Baker," he said. "Gods. *Baker.* You were meant to put it on the *pell*."

Tor looked at him.

"Sir?"

"The *pell*. Hang it on the pell. Test the shield on the pell. I wasn't going to take a cutter to *you*, you lunatic, what do you take me for?" Kolb straightened up, wiping his eyes. "Look at him. He's been walking round for two weeks thinking I meant to kill him. Haven't you? Haven't you, Baker."

Tor opened his mouth, and shut it, and opened it.

"Oh," he said, at last, in a small voice.

The yard came apart. The racks were laughing, and the shed roof, and the faculty bench; the building master had turned away and was looking hard at the wall; the headmaster had his spectacles off and was polishing them, which Sara had learned meant he didn't trust his face. Beside her, Tovey made a sound she'd never heard from him, and put his hand over his eyes. And Sara stood in the middle of it and thought of the napkin folded into a square, and the drying field money to his mother, and *you'll tell him it wasn't his fault*, and found that she wasn't laughing at all. She found she wanted very much to go out there and hit him.

"A builder," said Mistress Wensa, into the laughter, "ought to have faith in his equipment."

Kolb looked at her. She looked back at him, and her face was quite composed, and she was, Sara saw, enormously pleased about something.

"Twenty minutes," said Kolb. He hooked the hilt back on his belt. "And the cutter. It passes, Baker. Well done."

* * *

After that, everybody had ideas.

"He wasn't *wet*," said someone on the racks. "Try him in the pond."

"The pond! Throw him in the pond!"

"Drop him off the bell tower."

"Bury him."

"Poison."

"You couldn't poison him," said a fifth-year, reasonably. "He'd have to eat. Has anyone ever seen him eat?"

"Starve him, then," said Trice. "It's only a matter of time."

Tor stood in the middle of it all, in his ring of black sand, apologizing to everyone, and looking every so often, Sara saw, at the pond, which lay just below the yard on the slope toward the sheep field, brown with the thaw. He was looking at it the way he'd looked at the cracks.

Nobody threw him in the pond.

The headmaster stood up, with the help of his stick, and the yard went quiet for him.

"Mr. Baker," he said. "That was quite remarkable." He put his spectacles back on. "Master Kolb, I believe you ought to go down on your knees and beg Mr. Baker for a few of those. For the school's use."

"Oh," said Tor. "There are ten, sir."

He went across the sand to Rolph, who took a bundle out of his coat and gave it to him. Tor unwrapped it. Copper and cord, a little heap of it. He came back with his hands full, and Sara saw him stop and count them, once, under his breath.

He gave the first to Rolph. Rolph took it and didn't say anything at all. The second to Trice, who looked at it in her palm as though it were a letter in a hand she didn't know. The third he brought to Sara.

"Miss Sara," he said. "If you'd like one."

She took it. It was warm from his hands. It weighed almost nothing. She turned it over and looked at the etched lines on the back, as fine as hair, and felt the price of it climb in her head like a column of figures she couldn't stop adding, past the glass halls on the river, past anything her father had ever let her hold.

"Thank you, Tor," she said.

The fourth to Tovey. "Count Thompson. If— that is, if it's not—"

"Scholar Baker," said Tovey. He took it. He weighed it in his big hand, and looked at it, and then, for a long moment, not at Tor but across the yard at Kolb, and Kolb looked back at him; and neither of them said anything, and Tovey put the shield inside his coat.

"And the rest to Master Kolb, sir," said Tor to the headmaster. "For the school. If that's all right. He'll know who needs them."

Kolb took five shields off him in one hand, the cords tangled through his fingers, and stood holding them and looking at them, and didn't say anything either.

Sara saw Mistress Wensa's eyes go, very quickly, along the line of them: Trice, Tovey, Sara, and last, longest, Rolph. And her face did the thing again, the letter and the good news.

"Tor," said Sara.

He turned.

"Are they still in our deal?" She said it lightly. She made herself say it lightly. "The shields. If people want them."

"Oh." He looked surprised. "Yes. Of course. The same. Ten in the hundred."

Beside her, Trice glanced at her. Sara didn't glance back. Neither of them, since the evening in his room, had said aloud whose ten in the hundred it was, and she wasn't going to start now, in front of a count and half the school.

"It's a lot of money, Tor," she said.

"Is it?" He seemed honestly puzzled. "I don't know what I'd do with it. I've got the drying field money still. Most of it." He frowned. "I bought a box."

"Sell the template," said Trice.

They both looked at her.

"To the Debri house," said Trice. "Not the shields. The template. Let Sara's father's builders make the copies, and you take a share of every one they sell, and you never have to make another unless you want to." She tucked her own shield away inside her collar. "You're faster than anyone they've got, of course. You're faster than anyone anyone's got. But you've also got classes, and you've also got to sleep, occasionally, whatever you think. Let someone else do the copying."

"I don't mind the copying," said Tor.

"You *hate* the copying. You talk to the squares."

Tor looked as if he'd like to deny it, and couldn't, quite.

"But who'd want them?" he said. "Enough to need a whole house making them? A few, I suppose. Guards. Master Kolb's. A few hundred?"

Sara looked at him. She looked at him for long enough that he began to look worried, and then she put her hand on his arm.

"Tor," she said. "Look at me."

He looked at her.

"Every guard," she said. "Every guard in every house in Noram. Every soldier. Every merchant who goes on a road, and every carter who drives for one. Every noble's child, Tor, every one, every boy and girl at the academy, and every one who isn't at the academy because their parents couldn't get them in. Every one of their mothers. Every ship's captain. Every man who carries money for a living, and every woman who walks home in the dark." She stopped. "Everyone who's ever been afraid of a knife. That's who."

Tor stood and took it in. She watched him do it, the way she'd watched him take in the yard: the slow widening of his eyes, the swallow.

"That's a lot of copper," he said, at last.

Trice made a small sound.

"It is," said Sara. "It is a lot of copper. Tor—"

But he'd stopped listening. She saw it happen. His eyes had gone past her, to the black ring on the sand, and the cracks running out of it, and down the slope to the brown pond, and his lips had begun to move.

"The air," he said, to himself. "The air's first. If it burns it all, there's nothing to let in. So it has to keep some. Or make some. And the ground. It held, but the ground didn't, so it has to spread. Like a cake on a plate. And she was quicker than me. She was quicker than me, and that's the worst one, I didn't even have it on. And water." He was looking at the pond now. "What would it do with water?"

"Tor," said Sara.

"Sorry," said Tor, not hearing her. "Sorry. One moment."

He wandered off across the sand toward the pond, muttering, with his hands behind his back.

Sara stood and watched him go. Beside her, Trice had her own shield out again, and was turning it over in her fingers, and her lips were moving too; but Sara could see what Trice was doing, because she was doing it herself. Every guard. Every soldier. Every road. She got to a number, and stopped, because it was too big to be a number, and started again.

"Not yet," said Trice, under her breath, to nobody. "Not yet."

On the edge of the pond, Tor had crouched down and put one finger in the water, and was looking at it.
