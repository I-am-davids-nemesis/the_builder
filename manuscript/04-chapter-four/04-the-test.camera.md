The cafeteria at lunch was quieter than usual. At the long table by the kitchen door, Tor sat across from Rolph, and the students at the tables around them ate slowly and looked at their plates.

"—and the drying field money goes to my mother," Tor was saying. "Miss Sara knows the amounts. And the food dryer's yours, the template, you know where it is. And the ten in your box. Whoever Master Kolb says. And you'll tell him, won't you, that it wasn't his fault? Afterward. I don't want him to feel—"

"Tor," said Rolph.

"He'll feel it. He's not as hard as he looks. You'll tell him I said thank you, for the running. Everything else too. But the running mostly."

"*Tor.*"

"I'm only saying, in case."

At the next table Sara and Trice sat side by side. Trice was buttering a roll very precisely.

"He's very calm," said Trice, "about a pell."

"He's always calm," said Sara. "That's how you can tell he isn't."

Tor had finished his soup. He folded his napkin into a small, exact square, put it beside the bowl, and sat with his hands flat on the table on either side of it. Rolph watched him.

Trice put down her knife. "Come on," she said. "If we want to see anything, we'll have to be early."

* * *

The practice yard was full.

Students stood three deep along the weapon racks, and sat on top of them, and a row of first-years sat on the roof of the equipment shed with their legs hanging over until Kolb looked up at them, and then they climbed down. At the far end stood the old wall, great close-fitted blocks the color of dried bread, and in front of it the pell on its post. Nobody stood near either. A wide space of packed sand lay empty around the pell, and the crowd had arranged itself around the edge.

The faculty had the bench by the water butt. The headmaster sat in the middle in his long gray gown, spectacles pushed up on his forehead, both hands folded on top of his stick. The building master stood at one end with his arms crossed. Mistress Wensa stood at the other, her ledger under one arm, her other hand loose at her side, near her belt.

Tovey stood at the front of the crowd in his dark cloak and brooch, with a little space around him. Sara and Trice came through the crowd to stand beside him.

"Patricia," said Tovey. "Miss Debri."

"Tovey," said Trice.

"He's late," said Tovey.

"He's never late," said Sara. "Look."

Tor was coming down the steps from the long hall with Rolph beside him and a trail of students behind. At the bottom he saw the yard, and stopped. His eyes went along the racks, the shed, the bench, the crowd. He swallowed. Then he came on across the sand. Rolph walked with him to the edge of the crowd and stopped there, hands in his pockets. Tor went out into the empty space alone.

Kolb waited by the pell, with a practice weapon over his shoulder, a long, heavy, iron-shod length of wood like a fence post, and the cutter's hilt on his belt.

Tor bowed to him.

"Thank you, sir," he said, clearly enough to reach the racks. "For everything. I'm ready."

Kolb gave him an odd look.

Tor took the shield out of his tunic: a copper plate the size of a palm, etched all over in fine dark lines, on a cord. He put it over his head. His hand went to it—

Wensa was already moving. A short black rod was in her hand, and she had crossed half the distance to Tor and leveled it at him before his fingers reached the copper. Someone on the racks yelled. Tor's hand closed on the plate.

There was a sound like a held breath. The air in front of Tor shivered, like air over a road in summer. He rocked back half a step on his heels. Nothing else happened.

Wensa turned and pointed the rod at the pell.

The pell exploded. It went from a post to a cloud of splinters with a crack like the sky breaking, and the splinters came pattering down over the sand and the front row. A first-year sat down hard. Somebody's hat landed in the water butt.

Tovey let out his breath.

Wensa put the first rod away and took out a second, and pointed it at Tor. His sleeves stirred as if a wind had gone by, and the sand around his boots went smooth. That was all. She turned and pointed it at the stump of the pell.

Beyond the stump, the old wall bowed outward, away from them, in a long, slow curve like a sail filling. The blocks ground against each other. A crack ran up the middle from the sand to the coping, zigzagging, branching, and stopped.

"That wall," said the building master, to nobody, "has been there four hundred years."

"Yes," said the headmaster mildly. "It has."

The third rod was red.

Wensa leveled it at Tor and fire came up out of the sand all around him, in a ring, waist high, then head high, roaring, yellow at the edges and white at the heart. The heat crossed the yard like an oven door opening, and the whole crowd stepped back together. Through the flames, Tor stood in a clear space the shape of an egg, with the fire pressing against the outside of it and curling round. He wasn't burning.

His hand went to his throat.

He took a breath, a big one, the kind taken before going under water, and held it. He looked up at the wall of flame, and around, and his lips moved.

Sara started forward. She'd gone three steps onto the sand when Trice caught her by the back of the collar and held on.

"*Wait*," said Trice.

Wensa flicked her wrist. The fire went out, all at once, like a lamp, and left a ring of black, glassy, smoking sand. In the middle stood Tor, hand on his throat, cheeks puffed out.

He let the breath go and gasped, again and again, and bent over with his hands on his knees.

"Thank you, ma'am," he said between breaths, to Wensa. "Thank you very much."

Wensa put the red rod back on her belt. She looked at Tor in his ring of black sand, and at the copper on his chest, and the corners of her mouth moved, very slightly.

Trice still had hold of Sara's collar. Her hand was shaking. Sara looked down at it and said nothing.

* * *

"My turn," said Kolb.

He swung the practice weapon down off his shoulder and walked into the ring.

Tor straightened. He looked at Kolb and at the weapon. Then he put his hands behind his back.

"Yes, sir," he said.

Kolb hit him. It was a blow that shook the yard, and the field took it with a sound like a fist on a door, and Tor rocked on his heels. Kolb hit him again, and again, from the left and the right and straight down from above like a man driving a stake. *Thock. Thock. Thock.*

After a minute, the crowd stopped flinching. After two, they started talking.

After five minutes, people were sitting down.

After eight, a second-year came back from the kitchens with a jug of water and some cups. After ten, there was open betting along the racks, though nobody could agree on what. Kolb was sweating; his bald head shone. He wore a fixed, fierce, happy grin, and he didn't stop. Tor stood with his hands behind his back and took every blow, and rocked, and said every so often, "Sorry, sir," or "Are you all right, sir? Would you like a rest?" and Kolb said, "*No*," and hit him again.

At fifteen minutes there was a new sound under the blows, a thin, dry crunch. Tor looked down. The sand round his boots had been pressed hard and gray and flat, like a threshing floor, and as the next blow fell a crack ran out from under his left heel, an arm's length across the sand, and then another from under his right.

Tor looked at the cracks with his head on one side.

"Ground," he said, under his breath.

At twenty minutes, Kolb stepped back. He was breathing hard. He dropped the practice weapon, wiped his face with his forearm, and stood looking at Tor. Tor looked back. There wasn't a mark on him.

Kolb drew his cutter.

The yard went silent; the water butt could be heard dripping. Four feet of dull gray blade was simply there. Kolb didn't pause. He swung it one-handed at Tor's neck, flat and fast, like a man cutting a switch.

It stopped a hand from the copper and stayed there. Tor stood very still, chin up, looking at it out of the corner of his eye.

Kolb began to laugh. It was a big, sudden bark of a laugh that went across the yard and came back off the cracked wall. He let the blade go and stood with the empty hilt in his hand, laughing until he had to put his other hand on his knee.

"Baker," he said. "Gods. *Baker.* You were meant to put it on the *pell*."

"Sir?"

"The *pell*. Hang it on the pell. Test the shield on the pell. I wasn't going to take a cutter to *you*, you lunatic, what do you take me for?" Kolb straightened, wiping his eyes. "Look at him. He's been walking round for two weeks thinking I meant to kill him. Haven't you? Haven't you, Baker."

Tor opened his mouth, shut it, opened it.

"Oh," he said, in a small voice.

The yard roared. The racks laughed, and the shed, and the bench. The building master turned to look hard at the wall. The headmaster took off his spectacles and polished them. Tovey put his hand over his eyes and made a muffled sound. Rolph, at the edge of the crowd, sat down on the sand.

Sara didn't laugh. She stood with her arms folded tight and her mouth pressed shut, looking at Tor.

"A builder," said Wensa, into the noise, "ought to have faith in his equipment."

Kolb looked at her. Her face was composed. Her eyes were bright.

"Twenty minutes," said Kolb. He hooked the hilt back on his belt. "And the cutter. It passes, Baker. Well done."

* * *

"He wasn't *wet*," someone called from the racks. "Try him in the pond."

"The pond! Throw him in the pond!"

"Drop him off the bell tower."

"Bury him."

"Poison."

"You couldn't poison him," said a fifth-year reasonably. "He'd have to eat. Has anyone ever seen him eat?"

"Starve him, then," said Trice. "It's only a matter of time."

In the middle of the ring, Tor apologized to whoever was nearest. Between apologies, his eyes went to the pond, which lay just below the yard on the slope toward the sheep field, brown with the thaw.

Nobody threw him in.

The headmaster rose with the help of his stick, and the yard quieted.

"Mr. Baker," he said. "That was quite remarkable." He put his spectacles back on. "Master Kolb, I believe you ought to go down on your knees and beg Mr. Baker for a few of those. For the school's use."

"Oh," said Tor. "There are ten, sir."

He went to Rolph, who took a bundle from his coat and handed it over. Tor unwrapped it, counted the copper plates under his breath, and came back with his hands full.

He gave the first to Rolph, who took it without a word. The second to Trice, who looked at it in her palm. The third to Sara.

"Miss Sara," he said. "If you'd like one."

She took it, and turned it over, and looked at the fine lines etched on the back for a long moment.

"Thank you, Tor," she said.

The fourth to Tovey. "Count Thompson. If— that is, if it's not—"

"Scholar Baker," said Tovey. He took it and weighed it in his big hand. Then he looked across the yard at Kolb, and Kolb looked back at him. Neither said anything. Tovey put the shield inside his coat.

"And the rest to Master Kolb, sir," said Tor to the headmaster. "For the school. If that's all right. He'll know who needs them."

Kolb took the last five in one hand, the cords tangled through his fingers, and stood looking down at them.

Wensa's eyes went along the line: Trice, Tovey, Sara, and last, longest, Rolph, with the copper in his hand. Her face settled, the way a face settles after good news.

"Tor," said Sara.

He turned.

"Are they still in our deal? The shields. If people want them."

"Oh." He looked surprised. "Yes. Of course. The same. Ten in the hundred."

Trice glanced sideways at Sara. Sara didn't glance back.

"It's a lot of money, Tor," said Sara.

"Is it?" He frowned. "I don't know what I'd do with it. I've got the drying field money still. Most of it." A pause. "I bought a box."

"Sell the template," said Trice.

They both looked at her.

"To the Debri house," said Trice. "Not the shields. The template. Let Sara's father's builders make the copies, and you take a share of every one they sell, and you never have to make another unless you want to." She tucked her own shield inside her collar. "You're faster than anyone they've got, of course. You're faster than anyone anyone's got. But you've also got classes, and you've also got to sleep, occasionally, whatever you think. Let someone else do the copying."

"I don't mind the copying," said Tor.

"You *hate* the copying. You talk to the squares."

Tor opened his mouth to deny it, and didn't.

"But who'd want them?" he said. "Enough to need a whole house making them? A few, I suppose. Guards. Master Kolb's. A few hundred?"

Sara looked at him for a long time. Then she put her hand on his arm.

"Tor," she said. "Look at me."

He looked at her.

"Every guard," she said. "Every guard in every house in Noram. Every soldier. Every merchant who goes on a road, and every carter who drives for one. Every noble's child, Tor, every one, every boy and girl at the academy, and every one who isn't at the academy because their parents couldn't get them in. Every one of their mothers. Every ship's captain. Every man who carries money for a living, and every woman who walks home in the dark." She stopped. "Everyone who's ever been afraid of a knife. That's who."

Tor's eyes widened slowly. He swallowed.

"That's a lot of copper," he said.

Trice made a small sound.

"It is," said Sara. "It is a lot of copper. Tor—"

But his eyes had gone past her, to the black ring on the sand, the cracks running out of it, the pond below. His lips moved.

"The air," he said. "The air's first. If it burns it all, there's nothing to let in. So it has to keep some. Or make some. And the ground. It held, but the ground didn't, so it has to spread. Like a cake on a plate. And she was quicker than me. She was quicker than me, and that's the worst one, I didn't even have it on. And water." He was looking at the pond. "What would it do with water?"

"Tor," said Sara.

"Sorry," said Tor. "Sorry. One moment."

He wandered off across the sand toward the pond, hands behind his back, muttering.

Sara watched him go. Beside her, Trice had taken her shield out again and was turning it over in her fingers, and her lips were moving, counting. Sara's lips were moving too.

"Not yet," said Trice, under her breath. "Not yet."

At the edge of the pond, Tor crouched and put one finger in the water, and looked at it.
