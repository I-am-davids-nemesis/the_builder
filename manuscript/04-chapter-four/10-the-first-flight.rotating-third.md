Tor woke before the fourth bell, which he hadn't meant to, and lay for a moment in the gray with his heart going, and then remembered why.

The room was dark blue. Rolph was asleep on his back with one arm off the bed and his mouth open, breathing like a slow saw. The plate over Tor's own bed was doing nothing, which was right; the night had been mild. On the desk, where Trice had left them two days ago and where he had very carefully not touched them since, because she'd said *wash, sleep, eat*, and he had, lay the copper and the little flattened thing beside it.

He'd done all three. He'd eaten things with bits in. He'd slept so long on the first night that Rolph had checked his breathing with a spoon. He'd gone to every class, and sat through Doris, and through theory, and through Kolb, who had looked at him across the practice yard and said "Not dead, then," and nothing else. He hadn't touched the desk.

He got up now and dressed without lighting a candle, very quietly, and put on the belt.

It was his old belt, the brown one from home that his father had cut down, with a second hole bored in it by Rolph's knife because he'd got thinner over the four days. The copper rode at the small of his back, flat against him under his tunic, a hand and a half wide, warm already from the room. It didn't hum. He'd half expected it to hum, like the planks. It didn't do anything at all, yet, and he liked it better for that.

The other thing went in his right hand.

It was the size of a hen's egg, flattened, smooth on one side and etched on the other, and it sat in his palm as if it had grown there. He'd made it to fit his own hand, without meaning to; he'd found that out on the second afternoon, when he'd picked it up for the first time since the trance and his fingers had simply closed round it, every one in its place. There was a loop of cord through a hole at one end. He put the loop round his wrist and pulled it snug.

That part he had thought about. He'd thought about it more than anything else. The plate on his back did what the egg in his hand told it, and if the egg went away, the plate went on doing whatever it had last been told, and it would go on doing it until it ran down, which by his reckoning would be some time after next harvest. He'd lain awake on that. He'd pictured it quite clearly: the egg tumbling away below him, very small, and himself going on, up and up, getting colder, and nobody ever knowing where. So the cord went round his wrist, and he tugged it twice to be sure, and then a third time, and then he opened the door.

The chair at the end of the corridor was still there, by the window, empty. Mistress Wensa had left it behind when he came out of the trance. Nobody had moved it. He went past it on his toes.

* * *

The court was gray and wet and silent, and the gate was on the latch, the way it always was before the fifth bell for the bakers' boys who came up from Hollin with the bread. Tor slipped through. The road went down to the left. He went right instead, along the wall and down the path through the long grass, toward the sheep field, with the dew soaking through his boots in the first dozen steps and the mist lying in the low ground ahead like milk in a saucer.

He was halfway down the hill when his hand slapped his chest.

He hadn't told it to. It had done it on its own, the way it had been doing since Trice and the apples: a quick flat slap over the place where the shield sat. Except that his hand landed on tunic. Just tunic. And under the tunic, his shirt, and under that, him.

He stopped dead in the wet grass.

"Oh, *Tor*," he said, aloud.

He'd taken it off to wash. He'd hung it on the bedpost by its cord, so he'd see it, so he'd remember, and he'd seen it, and he hadn't remembered, because he'd been thinking about the egg, and the cord, and how far up was. It was hanging on the bedpost now. He could see it in his mind, swinging slightly.

He stood there in the mist with the dew going through his socks and thought, for a long cold moment, about doing it anyway. It was only a test. He'd go a little way up. A man's height. Two. He'd be careful.

Then he thought about the black rod, and the pell going to splinters, and about a man's height, two, and the ground coming up, and he turned round and went back up the hill.

"Idiot," he told himself, all the way. "Idiot. You *idiot*. The one thing. The *one* thing. Kolb stood on you for twenty minutes, and you'd go up without it." He went through the gate. "You'd go up a *cliff* without it. Into the *sky*." He went up the stair two at a time, and then remembered and went the rest of the way on his toes. "You'd deserve it. You'd absolutely deserve it."

Rolph hadn't moved. The shield was on the bedpost, swinging slightly. Tor put it over his head and tucked it in and laid his hand flat on it, and pressed, and felt it come on: that small tight sense of the air drawing in round him, like a breath being held on his behalf. He stood there a moment with his hand on it, letting his heart slow down.

Then he went out again, on his toes, past the empty chair.

* * *

The sheep field was the one below the wall where he'd run, a month and more ago, with Tovey coming down out of the dark behind him. He hadn't been back to it since. It looked smaller in the daylight. The mist lay on the bottom of it, and the sheep stood about in it up to their knees, and the lambs were awake already, making their small cross noises, and one of the ewes looked at him and went on chewing.

He walked out into the middle, where the grass was short and nothing was near him, and stood with his feet apart, the way Kolb made them stand.

"Right," said Tor.

The egg sat in his hand. On the smooth side, under his thumb, was a little hollow, and in the hollow was where you told it to begin. He'd made it like that so you couldn't do it by accident. You had to mean it. He pressed his thumb into the hollow and held it there a moment, meaning it, and let go.

Nothing happened.

He stood in the wet grass and waited. The ewe chewed. Somewhere up on the wall a rook said something and was answered. The plate on his back sat there being warm, the way it had been warm when he put it on, and no warmer.

"Hm," said Tor.

He looked at the egg. He turned it over, and looked at the etched side, and turned it back. It looked right. It had looked right for two days. He thought, quite calmly, that the trouble with a thing you'd made in a trance was that you weren't entirely sure you'd been there when you made it.

He pressed the hollow again, firmly, and let go.

Nothing happened.

And then, a moment too late, the way it always came, he understood that it had. The first press had been *on*. He'd felt nothing, because there was nothing to feel: he'd been holding his hand still, and a still hand told the plate to stay where it was, and he was already where he was, on the ground, so it had stayed. It had been working perfectly. And he'd just turned it off.

"Oh, for—" said Tor, and pressed it again. On. He knew it was on, now. He stood there with his hand quite still and his heart going and nothing happening, because nothing was meant to happen, and he understood all of that perfectly well, and it didn't help in the least. He'd spent four days on it. He'd spent a month. He'd hung off the ceiling. He'd broken Rolph's window and stood Rolph's boots up the wall and given Trice a whole rota to keep, and here he was in a field, in the wet, at dawn, standing perfectly still so as to go nowhere.

"*Come on*," said Tor, and threw both his hands up in the air.

The ground went away.

It didn't go gradually. One moment there was grass under his boots and the next there wasn't, and his stomach stayed exactly where it had been, down there in the field with the sheep, and the rest of him went up without it. The wind came down on top of his head like a bucket. He saw the field drop away under his feet and get small, and a ring of grass flatten outward from the place he'd stood as though someone had dropped a plate into a pond, and the sheep scattering away from it in every direction, all at once, like spilt peas. He saw the school wall. He saw the top of the school wall. He saw the roofs of the school, and the bell tower, and the top of the bell tower, and the weathercock on it, which was a fish, which he'd never known, *below* him.

He brought his hands down.

He brought them down hard, because he wanted very much to be back on the ground, and the plate believed him. The field came up. It came up *fast*, all of it at once, green and gray and getting bigger, the sheep turning from peas into sheep, and somewhere in it he saw his own flattened ring of grass coming up to meet him like a target.

He stopped his hand.

He stopped all of himself, everything, every muscle, the way you stop when you've stepped on something that moved. And the plate stopped. He hung in the air above the sheep field, perhaps the height of the chapel roof, perfectly still, with his arms out in front of him and his knees drawn up and his mouth open, and the ewe directly underneath him looked up, and went on chewing.

Tor breathed.

He breathed for quite a long while. He didn't move his hand. He didn't move anything. He hung there and felt his heart going in his ears and the cord tight round his wrist and the shield doing nothing, because there was nothing for it to do yet, and the plate at his back, warm and steady, holding him up out of nothing at all.

"Slowly," he whispered. "*Slowly*, Tor."

* * *

He did everything slowly after that.

He did it slower than he'd ever done anything in his life. Slower than etching. Slower than Kolb's twenty minutes. He moved the egg the way you'd move a full cup across a room, a hair at a time, and when he wanted to go down a little he thought about going down a little first, and then moved his hand half as far as he'd thought, and the plate let him down a hand's breadth, gently, like being set down in a chair. He went up the same way. He went sideways, once, by accident, because he'd shifted his weight and his hand had shifted with it; he slid along through the air like a picture being moved along a wall, and stopped himself, and hung there apologizing to nobody.

He'd been afraid that it would be like the planks. That it would want to go one way, and fight him. It didn't. It didn't want anything. It did exactly what he told it, exactly as hard as he told it, and the whole trouble was that his hand had never in its life been asked to be so precise about anything.

He found out about forward last, because he was most afraid of it.

He pushed. Very slightly, as if he were pushing a door that might have someone behind it. The field slid away backward under him, and the sheep went by, and the wall came toward him and went underneath, and he was over the road, and over the hedge on the far side of the road, and going quite gently along in the gray air with the mist below him lying in the folds of the valley like wool.

It was so easy that he pushed a little more.

And a little more. It was like a slope you didn't notice you were running down. The hedges went by faster, and then fast, and then they weren't hedges any more but stripes, and the wind that had come down on his head when he went up was in his face now, all of it, and in his eyes, and in his mouth, and he found he couldn't breathe. The shield let air through. It had to; he'd made it to, in the washing house, with Sara counting. And it was letting this through as well, the whole of it, a great cold river of it straight down his throat so that he couldn't get any in or out, and his eyes were streaming, and his ears roared, and he was going, he thought, quite remarkably fast.

He drew his hand back a little, not much, enough to get a breath.

Then he pushed again.

He couldn't help it. He was ashamed of it afterward, a little, but he couldn't help it. He kept it just at the edge of where the breath went, with his eyes screwed up and his tunic snapping round him like a flag, and the valley went by underneath in long gray-green sweeps, fields and walls and a wood and a stream with the light on it, and a man somewhere very far down with a cart, a tiny man, a tiny cart, and then something came up over the edge of the next hill and he knew it.

It was Hollin.

He'd only ever come to it along the road, at Kolb's orders, at a run, with his chest burning: an hour each way on a good day, more on a bad. It had been perhaps two minutes. He slowed, a hair at a time, until he could breathe and see, and hung there over it in the early light.

It was so *small*. He could see the whole of it at once. The chapel with its stubby tower and the lead on its roof, gone silver. The mill road, and the mill, with its wheel turning very slowly. The old marker stone, a mile out on the road. The well in the square where Kolb's runners slapped the stone and turned for home, and he thought he could see, even from here, the side of it that their hands had polished. A dog trotted across the square and stopped and sat down to scratch. Smoke was going up from four or five chimneys, straight up, because there was no wind down there at all.

"Oh," said Tor, very quietly, to Hollin. "Oh, *look* at you."

* * *

Then he had to turn round, and it took him the better part of a quarter of an hour.

He hadn't thought about turning. He'd thought about up and down and forward, and how slowly, and the cord, and he'd never once thought about what you did when you wanted to go back where you came from. You couldn't just go backward. He tried it; it worked; he slid back across the sky the way he'd come, quite gently, facing the wrong way, with Hollin getting smaller in front of him and no idea at all what was behind. He stopped that very quickly.

So he tried moving his hand sideways, and went sideways, still facing Hollin, like a crab.

So he tried turning his wrist, a little, the way you'd turn a key.

He turned. He turned rather more than a little. He went round once, and then half again, slowly but quite unstoppably, like a top running down, with the world wheeling past him, Hollin and the hills and the sky and Hollin again, and his arms out and his legs out and his stomach somewhere in the next county. When he got it stopped he was facing a hill he'd never seen before, and he hung there feeling sick and waited for it to pass.

Then he did it again, slower.

He did it in pieces in the end. A hair of the wrist, and stop. Breathe. Another hair, and stop. The hills crept round him in little jerks, like the hand of a clock that wanted oiling. He went round in a long, wobbling, lopsided circle over the whole of Hollin, too high and then too low and then too far to the left, so that anybody watching from the square would have seen something dark in the sky going round and round above the chapel like a lost rook that couldn't make up its mind. He didn't think about anybody watching. He was thinking about his wrist.

And then, without his quite noticing when, the school was in front of him again, far off and small on its hill above the valley, with the sun just coming up behind it and catching the bell tower, and he was facing home.

He laughed, once, out loud, at nothing.

Then he pushed his hand forward, and didn't stop it, and went.

He went faster going back. He knew he would. He knew where the breath stopped now, and he went right to the edge of it and held it there with his eyes streaming and his mouth shut, breathing through his nose in short, hard pulls, and the valley went under him in a long gray blur, and the school came up out of it so fast that he was over the wall before he'd properly decided to slow down. He drew back. He drew back hard, too hard, and hung there jolting over the sheep field with his heart going, and the sheep, who had only just come back to the middle of it, all looked up at once.

He looked down at them.

Down was a long way.

He hadn't thought about that, either. Not properly. He'd thought about *up*. Up had been the whole problem, for a month. And now here he was, and the ground was there, and between them was only his right hand, and his right hand had already shown him, this morning, exactly how fast it could put him into the grass if it got excited.

"Slowly," said Tor. "Slowly, slowly, slowly."

He did it slowly.

It took him two minutes. He knew it was two minutes because he counted, the way Kolb made them count on the pell, and the counting helped. He brought his hand down a hair, and the grass came up a hand's breadth, and he stopped, and breathed, and did it again. The sheep watched him the whole way. At sixty, he was as high as the wall. At ninety, as high as a hay cart. At a hundred, as high as Rolph. At a hundred and ten his boots touched the top of the grass and he stopped, from sheer nerves, and hung there with the dew wetting his toes and not his heels. At a hundred and twenty, his heels came down, and his knees bent, and he was standing in the sheep field with his own weight back on his own feet, exactly where he'd started, inside the flattened ring.

He pressed the hollow in the egg. Off. He knew it was off this time. He felt the plate let go of him, the way a hand lets go of your arm.

Then his knees gave way, and he sat down in the wet grass, and then he lay down in it, flat on his back with his arms out, and looked up at the sky where he'd just been.

It was a very ordinary sky. Thin and pale and going blue at the top, with a few rooks in it, going about their business.

Tor started to laugh.

He didn't mean to. It came up out of him from somewhere low down, the way the plate had taken him up, all at once and without asking, and he lay in the sheep field in the dew and laughed out loud with his eyes wet and his hands full of grass. He laughed until his ribs hurt. A lamb came up and looked at him from very close and went away again, offended. Nobody else heard. There was nobody else to hear. There was only Tor, and the sheep, and the sky, which was the same sky it had been yesterday, except that he had been in it.

"It works," he said to it. Not loudly. Just telling it, the way you'd tell someone a piece of news. "I went to *Hollin*."

Up on the hill, the bell began to ring the fifth bell. He lay there and counted the stroke and the echo off the chapel roof, and thought about breakfast, and about Rolph, still asleep with his arm off the bed, and about Trice, who would want to know, and who would be furious, and who would want to know how *fast*. He thought he might not tell anybody quite yet. He thought he might keep it for a day, just one, the way you kept the first apple off a tree in your pocket and kept touching it.

Then he got up, and brushed himself off, and found he'd lost a boot-lace somewhere over the valley, and went up to breakfast.

* * *

In Hollin, at the well, a boy named Sam Carter told his mother he'd seen a rook the size of a man go round and round the chapel tower before the fifth bell, and been sent indoors for lying. By noon the miller had seen it too. By evening it had been a man, then a man with wings, then an Austran, and three people in the Plough swore they'd heard it singing.
