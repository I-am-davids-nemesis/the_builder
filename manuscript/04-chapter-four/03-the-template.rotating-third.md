Tor was dressed before the first bell of the rest day, which was unheard of, and he was standing at the door with his satchel on his shoulder when Rolph opened his eyes.

"I'm going down to the chambers," he said. "It might be two days. I'm sorry."

"What might?" said Rolph, into his pillow.

But Tor had gone, and the bar had dropped behind him, and Rolph lay there in the gray light listening to his footsteps go away down the corridor, quick and light, and then the stair, and then nothing. He lay for a while longer. Then he rolled over and looked at the desk.

He'd asked for the food dryer. That was what he thought, lying there. Ten days ago, sitting on his bed with his mother's box on his knees, he'd asked when the fruit one would be ready, and Tor had sat on the edge of his own bed with one boot off and said he couldn't, he was sorry, he would, just not now. And Rolph had said take your time. He'd said it twice. He'd *meant* it.

And now it was the rest day, and Tor was going down to the chambers before the bell, for two days, for something he hadn't named.

"Oh, *Baker*," said Rolph, to the ceiling.

* * *

He took breakfast down at the fourth bell, on a tray: bread, and the end of a ham Hob had let him have, and a jug of milk, and two apples, because there would be no telling Tor to eat anything that needed a knife.

The undercroft was under the old wing, down a stair worn into a dip in the middle of every step, and it was cool and dry and smelled of stone and lamp oil. There were six chamber doors along the passage, plain oak, each with a slate hung on a nail beside it. Five of the slates were clean. The sixth said, in chalk, in a round careful hand:

*BAKER, T. — In: first bell, rest day.*

There was a bench across from it. Mistress Wensa was sitting at one end of it, with her ledger on her knee.

Rolph stopped at the bottom of the stair with the tray in his hands.

She looked up. She looked at him exactly as she had looked at him across the front of the accounts room for two years, which was to say briefly, as if he were a column of figures she had already added; and then her eyes went past him, up the stair, the way they always went past him, to the door.

He had thought about this. He'd lain awake on it for the better part of a week. He'd thought of a number of things to say to her, and some of them had been very good, and all of them, he'd noticed, came out in his head in a voice that wasn't his. He stood there and felt it coming now, rising in him like the cold off the stone, his father's voice, every consonant in its place.

He put it away.

"Morning," he said. He went past her and set the tray down on the floor by Tor's door, and knocked, once, and nothing happened, and he hadn't expected it to. Then he sat down on the other end of her bench.

They sat. A long way above them, the bell went for the fifth hour.

A woman came along the passage from the far end, small and square and gray, in an apron, with a lamp. She stopped at Tor's door, put her eye to a little shutter set into the oak at head height, looked for the space of three breaths, and closed it. She took the chalk from the ledge under the slate and made a mark. Then she looked down at the tray on the floor, and at Rolph, and at Mistress Wensa, and at Rolph again.

"He won't eat that," she said.

"I know," said Rolph. "It's for when he stops."

"Well, then." She considered the two of them on the bench a moment longer, and her face did nothing at all, which Rolph thought was rather heroic. Then she went on down the passage with her lamp.

The bell went for the sixth hour. Mistress Wensa turned no pages.

"He hasn't done anything, you know," said Rolph, at last, to the opposite wall. "He hasn't done one single thing."

"I'm aware of what he has done," said Mistress Wensa.

He looked at her. She was looking at Tor's door. Her hand lay flat on the ledger, and it did not move, and after a while Rolph found he had nothing to say to that which he was prepared to say in this passage, out loud, to her, and he got up and went back up the stair.

* * *

At supper the whole hall knew where Tor was. Rolph heard it at three tables before he'd got to his own: Baker had gone down to the chambers on the rest day, Baker was building something in the undercroft, Haskins had told the kitchen, Baker was making shields for the whole school, Baker was making a shield for the king. Dorgal Sorvey's table was quiet. Dorgal ate his supper without looking up, and the large fourth-year beside him ate nothing at all, which Rolph had never seen him do.

The tray was still on the floor by the door at the tenth bell, with the bread gone hard and the milk turned and both apples sitting exactly where Rolph had put them. Rolph picked it up and put a fresh one down. The slate by the door had twelve chalk marks on it now, in a neat row, one for every bell.

"Is he all right?" he said, to Bet Haskins, when she came along with the lamp.

She put her eye to the shutter. She looked for longer this time.

"He's all right," she said. "He's working." She closed the shutter, and made her mark, and then she stood a moment with the chalk in her fingers. "Ink's drying on him," she said, more to the chalk than to Rolph. "Drying as he puts it down. I've seen that a dozen times. Thirty-one years." She put the chalk back on its ledge. "Go to bed, young man."

* * *

He came down again before the first bell of the next day, which was a class day, and Mistress Wensa was already on the bench.

Rolph didn't say anything to her and she didn't say anything to him. He sat at his end and she sat at hers, and the lamp burned in its niche, and the slate by the door had twenty-three marks on it. At a quarter to the first bell Bet Haskins came along the passage, and this time she didn't stop at the shutter. She knocked, softly, and opened the door.

Rolph was on his feet before he knew it.

The chamber was small and bare and lit by one lamp on a shelf. There was a bench, and a stool, and Tor on the stool, with his back to the door. In front of him on the bench lay a square of copper the size of a book, and he had both hands flat on it, and he wasn't moving at all. Round him, on the bench and on the floor, there were a great many other things, sheets of paper covered on both sides with close dark marks, a pot of resist, the acid jar stoppered, a heap of plain copper plates, bare. The air in the room was warm, and close, and smelled of a copper kettle boiled dry.

"Mr. Baker," said Bet Haskins. "It's the class day."

Tor didn't turn round. He didn't lift his hands from the copper. But after a moment he spoke, in a soft, faraway, perfectly ordinary voice, as if he were asking someone at breakfast to pass the salt.

"Rolph?"

"Here."

"Would you go and see the building master? Would you ask him to excuse me? For today. I'm sorry. I'd go, only I can't stop just here." A pause. "Tell him I'm sorry."

Rolph stood in the doorway with his mouth open.

Two years he'd lived with Tor. Two years of Tor sick with worry over a missed class, of Tor sure he'd be sent down for an essay a day late, of Tor not asking. Not asking for anything, not from anybody, not even the salt; getting up and fetching it himself. And here he was, with his back to the door and his hands on the copper and two days' growth of beard, asking.

"Yes," said Rolph. "Yes, of course. Of course I will."

"Thank you," said Tor, to the copper, and Bet Haskins shut the door.

Rolph stood looking at it.

"Well, then," said Bet Haskins.

* * *

The building master's office was on the first floor of the north side, and smelled of linseed and cold ashes, and the building master was in it, standing at the window with a board tipped up to the light. He didn't put it down when Rolph knocked.

"Yes?"

"Sir. I've come about Tor. About Baker, sir. He's—" Rolph had got it ready on the stairs, and it came out in a rush. "He's in the chambers, sir, he's been there since the first bell yesterday, he's building, he hasn't stopped once, Mistress Haskins says the ink's drying on him, and he asked me, sir, he *asked*, he said would I come and see you and ask if he could be excused today, and to say he's sorry, and he is, sir, he really is, he'd come himself only he can't stop just where he is, and I know it's a class day, sir, but if you'd seen him, honestly, it's not that he doesn't want to come, he's just, he's busy being—"

The building master turned round. He looked at Rolph over the top of the board.

"He asked?" he said.

"Yes, sir."

The building master looked at him for some time longer. Then he put the board down, and took a slip of paper from the desk, and wrote on it, two lines, and signed it, and held it out.

"Haskins told the staff table at breakfast," he said. "Yesterday's breakfast. And today's." He looked back at his board. "Give that to Doris. She'll pass it round. And tell Baker that Master Kolb's test is still the twelfth, and that Master Kolb will not have heard about any of this."

"Yes, sir. Thank you, sir."

"Young man."

Rolph turned in the doorway.

"Busy being what?" said the building master, to the board.

Rolph thought about it.

"I don't know, sir," he said. "I don't think there's a word for it."

The building master made a sound that might have been a cough. He tipped the board to the window again. Rolph went.

* * *

Tor came back at the eleventh bell of the second night.

Rolph heard him on the stair. He'd been lying on his bed with a book open on his chest, not reading it, rehearsing. He had the apology quite ready by then. He'd had two days to work on it. So when the bar lifted and the door opened, he was off the bed and across the room before Tor was properly through it.

"Tor. Listen. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. I told you to take your time. I *said* take your time, I said it twice, and you went and did it anyway, two days, in the chambers, for a *fruit dryer*, Tor, for *fruit*, because I went on about it, didn't I, I went on and on, and you can't even sit down without—"

He stopped.

Tor was standing just inside the door, swaying a little, and smiling. He had two days of beard and a smear of something black across one cheekbone, and his hands were full. He was holding them out in front of him, cupped together, the way you hold out a bird's nest. Things hung from his fingers on cords. Copper things. They clinked.

"Ten," said Tor. "It's done, Rolph. They're done. It's the shield."

Rolph looked at the copper.

"The shield," he said.

"For Master Kolb. And for— Well. Ten." Tor looked at him, and then the words he'd just walked in on seemed to arrive. He frowned. "What fruit dryer?"

"The—" Rolph waved a hand. "The one I asked for. The food one. For the crops. You said you'd do it, and I said take your time, and you went down there before the bell, and I thought—"

Tor began to laugh.

It was not much of a laugh. He was too tired for much of one. But it shook him from head to foot, and he had to lean on the door frame, and the shields clinked on their cords, and he lifted one hand, with three of them dangling from it, and pointed past Rolph, at the desk.

Rolph turned round.

There was a board on the desk. It was a plain square of pale wood, unpainted, with a sigil drawn on it in close dark lines, and it was lying under a sheet of Rolph's own theory notes and a cup. Rolph's cup. He'd put his milk down on it last night. He'd put his milk down on it, he thought, every night since fourth day. There was a ring.

"That's the food dryer," said Tor. "The template. I did it on fourth day. Before the shield. I thought you'd seen it."

Rolph looked at the board for a long time.

He picked up the cup. He looked at the ring. He put the cup down again, very carefully, somewhere else.

"Fourth day," he said.

"I was going to say. But you were writing to your mother. And then there was the wax."

"It's been on the desk," said Rolph, "for three days."

"Two and a half," said Tor helpfully, and laughed again, and slid down the door frame until he was sitting on the floor with the shields in his lap.

* * *

Rolph went to the kitchens. He went himself, at the eleventh bell, through the dark yard, and knocked up Hob, who came to the door in his shirt with a candle, and looked at him, and listened, and went away and came back with half a loaf and a lump of cheese and a jug of water and a bowl of something that had been stew and was now cold and set, and wouldn't hear of anything for it.

When he got back to the room, Tor was where he'd left him, on the floor, against the door, with the shields in his lap. He had gone to sleep sitting up.

Rolph got him to the bed. It took some doing. Tor was small, but he was all corners, and he kept waking up halfway and apologizing and helping, which made it harder. Rolph got him sitting on the edge of the bed, and held the cup, and Tor drank the whole of it, and then another, and then Rolph tore the bread into pieces and handed them to him one at a time, the way you'd feed a horse, and Tor ate each one and said thank you for each one.

"Who are they for?" said Rolph. "Ten."

"People," said Tor, chewing. "Master Kolb's. And people who might need one." He looked at the shields, lying in a heap on the end of the bed, catching the lamp. "I'll say. When I've— I'll say."

Rolph knelt down and began on his boots.

"You asked me," he said, to the laces. "This morning. To go to the building master. You know that? You asked."

"Did he mind?"

"No. He didn't mind."

"Good," said Tor. "I didn't know if you were allowed. To ask. If somebody'd—" He yawned, hugely. "I thought, Rolph won't mind. Rolph knows how. And you did, didn't you. You went. Thank you."

The second boot came off. Rolph set them side by side by the bed and sat back on his heels.

"Tor," he said. "Of course I went."

But Tor was asleep, sitting up, with a piece of bread in his hand.

Rolph took the bread away and laid him down, and put the blanket over him. Then he gathered up the ten shields from the end of the bed, one by one, by their cords. They were warm. He stood holding them for a while, in the middle of the room, all ten. Then he went to the lockbox at the foot of his bed, and laid his hand flat on the lid until it clicked, and put them in, on top of the gold, and closed it.

He went to the desk last. He took his theory notes off the food dryer, and wiped the milk ring off it with his sleeve, and stood it up on its edge against the wall, where it could be seen.
