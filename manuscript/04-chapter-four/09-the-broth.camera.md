At supper on fourth day, Tor came back from combat with his sleeves wet from the water butt, sat down at the desk, took the bread Rolph handed him, put it down, and picked up the pen.

At the tenth bell Rolph said goodnight. Tor didn't answer.

At the third bell of the night the candle was burning down and Tor's hand was moving.

In the morning the candle had gone out on its own. The hand was still moving. The sheet under it had gone gray with lines, and the ink in the pot had a skin on it like a pond in a frost.

* * *

"He's gone under," said Rolph at breakfast, sitting down by Trice.

"How far?"

"I said his name. I said it quite loud. Then I dropped a boot." He took her toast. "He said 'mm.'"

* * *

On the second afternoon a woman in a gray shawl came up the dormitory stair with an unlit lamp in her hand and asked which door was Baker's.

Bet Haskins stood in the doorway a long while. Trice stood beside her.

At the desk, with his back to them, Tor had a square of copper in front of him, bigger than a shield, and beside it a second thing no bigger than a hen's egg, flattened. His right hand went from one to the other and back without hurrying: stylus, brush, stylus. His left hand lay flat beside the egg-shaped thing, as if to keep it from rolling. Where the brush touched the copper, the ink went dull almost before it lifted.

"Well, then," said Bet Haskins. "Drying on him. Look at that."

"Is he all right?" said Trice.

"He's under. Don't pull him. He'll come up when he's done, or when he can't go on. Water in his hand every bell. He'll hold a cup if you put one in his fingers; they mostly do. And not bread. He won't chew. Broth, warm, not hot, salt in it. Put the spoon to his lip and he'll take it."

"And if he doesn't?"

Bet Haskins looked at her for the first time.

"Then you come and get me," she said, "and don't run on the stair."

* * *

That evening a sheet of Tor's gray paper hung on the inside of the door from the bent nail where Rolph's coat usually went. On it, in a firm hand:

*Rolph: nights. Trice: afternoons, and after supper until Rolph. Mornings: whoever has no class first, and if nobody, Rolph.*

At the bottom, underlined twice: *Water every bell.*

Sara read it over Trice's shoulder. "You haven't put me on."

"You've got the crate," said Trice. "And your father's letter. And the washing-house account."

"I could do an afternoon."

"You could." Trice pressed the corner flat. "You've got the crate."

Sara looked at her a moment. "I'll bring his notes from theory," she said.

* * *

At the far end of the corridor, by the window, stood a plain hall chair with a rush seat. Mistress Wensa sat on it with a ledger on her knee, marking first-year accounts in pencil. She was there in the afternoon and after supper. She didn't look up when Trice went past with the water jug, or when she came back.

"Is she marking," said Trice to Rolph in the doorway, "or is she counting the doors?"

"She's marking," said Rolph. "She's terribly behind. It's very sad."

Trice looked at him. He looked back. Neither of them said anything else.

* * *

On the fourth afternoon, Trice opened the window and wedged it with a book. Outside it was soft and gray; a lamb was complaining somewhere below the wall. The cold-snap plate still hung over Tor's bed. The plank lay under it with the dead ones.

Tor sat at the desk, leaning to the left, his left shoulder up near his ear. His lips were cracked at the corners. A smear of dried brown resist ran up the side of his jaw. His collar had gone dark with sweat, and dried, and gone dark again.

At the fifth bell a lidded crock came up from the kitchens. Trice set it at his elbow, away from the copper, and lifted the lid: chicken and barley, strained, the fat skimmed off. She dipped a finger, tested it on the inside of her wrist, and sat down on the end of his bed with the bowl in her lap to let it cool.

"You are," she told the back of his neck, "a very great deal of trouble."

The brush went into the pot, came out, touched the copper.

"I had to put Rolph on nights. *Rolph.* Do you know how he sleeps? Like a dropped sack. I came up on the second morning and he was asleep in the chair with his mouth open, and your cup was empty and you were holding it out. Just holding it out, Tor. Over the floor. Like a beggar at a gate." She blew on the broth. "I filled it. Obviously. That's not the point."

The brush went into the pot.

"And the building master came up yesterday and stood exactly where I'm sitting and looked at you for ten minutes and went away without a word. And Kolb sent a boy to ask whether you'd died, and if not, whether you'd be at practice." She tested the broth again. "I said not. To both."

She pulled the stool round beside his chair, sat close, and put the spoon to his lip.

"Open."

His mouth opened. He took it. His throat moved. The brush in his other hand never stopped.

Trice sat back slightly. Then she leaned in again, and did it again. A spoon, and a wait, and a spoon. Her voice stayed low and very even.

"Four days. *Four.* You can't do this in a chamber, oh no, with a slate and a shutter and a woman who's done it thirty-one years. You do it at a desk. With a door anyone can walk through. Open." Spoon. "I've worked out what it costs, keeping you alive. Four afternoons. Four nights. That's on your account, Master Baker, and I'm charging interest. Open." Spoon. "And nobody *asked* you to. Nobody needs a man flying by the end of the month. Kolb said *make one*, he didn't say *make one before you've had your supper*—"

On the twelfth spoon the brush stopped, a finger's width above the copper. A bead of ink gathered on the tip and didn't fall.

Tor blinked, slowly.

He swallowed. He looked at the brush in his hand, and put it down. He turned his head, carefully, and saw the spoon, and the bowl, and Trice.

"Oh," said Tor. His voice was cracked and small. "I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. Was I— were we talking?"

"No," said Trice. "You weren't. Open."

He opened his mouth. She put the spoon in it.

He swallowed, looked down at his collar and his hands, then at the window. "What bell is it?"

"Just past the fifth."

"Oh, that's all right, then." He tried to straighten. His shoulder didn't come down. "Oh. Ow. Trice, what *day* is it?"

"The wrong one," she said.

He looked at her.

"You sat down at supper on fourth day," said Trice, "and you've been sitting there since. That's four days, Tor. Four. Rolph's done every night. I've done every afternoon. Bet Haskins came up from the undercroft. Kolb thinks you're dead. Your ink's gone solid, and you've got resist in your hair, and if you'd gone one more day I'd have had to get Davie to help me lift you onto the bed, and *Davie* would have cried. Open."

He opened. He swallowed. He was staring at her.

"And you look," said Trice, "*dreadful*."

"I'm sorry," said Tor faintly.

"Don't be sorry. Be *fed*." She scraped the bowl. "Nobody was waiting. You could have done a bit and gone to bed. You could have done a bit and *eaten something*. You could have—" Her voice had gone up. She stopped, and put the spoon to his lip, harder than before. "Open."

He took it. He didn't turn back to the desk. He sat looking at her, and his eyes had gone bright, and he blinked twice, quickly.

"You stayed," he said.

"It was on the rota."

"You made a *rota*?"

"Somebody had to."

"And you're— You're angry. You're angry because I didn't eat."

"I am angry," said Trice, "because you are a great deal of trouble."

"No, but you're—" His face opened, and then, very quickly, closed. "It's almost," said Tor slowly, "as if you—"

He stopped.

Trice held the spoon still.

Tor's face settled. It softened, and went gentle and grateful.

"You're a very good friend, Trice," he said. "Thank you. Really. That's very kind."

"I am not kind," said Trice. "Ask anyone. Ask Tovey."

"Tovey says you're kind."

"Tovey's my cousin. He's obliged to." She held out the last spoonful. "Open."

He ate it, and said thank you. Then he looked down at the desk, at the copper and the egg-shaped thing beside it, and his face went still and far away, toward the open window.

"Oh," he said softly. "It's *done*. Trice. I think it's done."

"Good," said Trice. "It can wait."

"But I ought to—"

"It's waited four days. It can wait for you to wash." She set the bowl on the bare corner of the desk and pushed the egg-shaped thing to the far side with one finger, out of his reach. His eyes followed it all the way. "Wash. Sleep. Eat something with *bits* in. Then you can fly into a tree with my blessing."

He laughed, a cracked, surprised laugh, and winced at his shoulder.

* * *

At the sixth bell Rolph came in with bread, cheese and a jug of milk, and stopped in the doorway.

Tor lay asleep on top of the blanket under the copper plate, in his stiff clothes, boots on, face to the wall. On the desk: the copper, the flattened thing, the crock with its lid off, and the bowl, scraped clean, with the spoon in it.

Trice sat on the stool with her hands in her lap. She looked up.

Rolph looked at the bowl. Then at her face, a moment longer than he needed to. Then he came in, put the bread and cheese and milk down beside the bowl, knelt at the foot of the bed, and began quietly working Tor's boots off.

He said nothing.

Trice stood, took the rota down off the door, folded it small and put it in her pocket. At the end of the corridor Wensa turned a page in her ledger. Trice walked past her, down the stair and out across the court, where the light was going and the rooks were coming home. Her lips were moving slightly, as if she were counting something. Halfway across the court she stopped counting, and walked the rest of the way with her hands in her pockets, looking at nothing.
