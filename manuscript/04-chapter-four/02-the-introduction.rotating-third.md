By the ninth day, the whole school had a name for it, and Dorgal hadn't even had to start it.

"Baker's sweetheart," said Marco, with his mouth full, "has had the fish."

Dorgal didn't look round. He didn't need to. He could see the end of the long table by the kitchen door without turning his head, and had been seeing it for nine days: Baker, small and brown and bent over his plate, with the merchant boy across from him; and at the end of the next table, back to the wall, upright as a hat stand, Mistress Wensa of the accounts room, eating her fish one exact forkful at a time and looking at nothing in particular, which was to say at Baker.

She had been at breakfast. She had been at lunch. She had been outside meditation on the bench, which Dorgal had seen with his own eyes, and on the bench by the water butt while Kolb took the top off a pell, which half the yard had seen. She had been in the doorway of a workroom on the east stair at the eleventh bell, which Dorgal had also seen, because he'd gone up there on the third night to use the room with the stove and found a slate hung on the door next to it with BAKER chalked on it in a round, careful hand. Every night that week. Ninth bell to twelfth.

He had gone back down again. He hadn't been up since.

"She's had the fish," said Marco again, as if this were significant, and laughed.

Marco laughed at most things. He was a fourth-year, large and pleasant and slow, from a family that grew barley somewhere and wanted him to marry someone who didn't, and he had attached himself to Dorgal in the autumn for no reason Dorgal had ever examined. He was useful. He laughed at the right places, and quite often at the wrong ones too, which made the right ones sound more numerous.

"Do you suppose," said Dorgal, cutting his own fish into very even pieces, "that he's told his mother?"

Marco thought this was the funniest thing he'd ever heard.

On Dorgal's other side, the third of them said nothing. He hadn't eaten. He never seemed to eat much. He sat with his hands folded on the table in front of his plate, very still, and he was looking at the same thing Dorgal was looking at, and it occurred to Dorgal, not for the first time, that he couldn't remember who had introduced them.

He had been there since the autumn. He was too old to be a student, though only by a little: a man of twenty-five, perhaps, or a very worn twenty-two, in the school brown like anybody else, with a long, quiet face that nobody would have noticed in a crowd. Except for the eyes. Dorgal was used to the eyes now. He still didn't like to meet them. Where the whites should have been, they were black: not dark, not shadowed, but black, an even wet black like the ink in a well, and the irises sat in the middle of it gray and ordinary and perfectly calm, like two coins dropped into a pond.

Dorgal had never needed to call him anything. When Dorgal turned round, he was there.

"He's getting up," said Marco.

Baker was getting up. He was stacking his plate on the merchant boy's plate, and the cups on the plates, and taking them to the hatch, because Baker was the kind of person who took his plate to the hatch. He said something to the merchant boy, who nodded and stayed where he was, with half his supper in front of him. Baker went to the door.

Dorgal waited for the accounts mistress to get up.

She didn't. For the first time in nine days, she stayed where she was. She'd put down her fork, and her eyes had gone, not to the door where Baker was, but to the far end of the hall, to something Dorgal couldn't see; and she sat on, very straight, with her hand flat beside her plate.

Dorgal looked at her for a moment. Then he wiped his mouth and stood up.

"Gentlemen," he said. "I think we ought to give the bridegroom our congratulations."

* * *

The passage outside the dining hall ran down to the kitchen yard, and in the evenings it was full of wet coats. It had been raining all afternoon, the first warm rain of the spring, and the coats hung steaming on their pegs the whole length of the wall, and the lamps at either end made the middle of it dim and close and smelling of wool. Baker had nearly reached the door to the yard.

"My dear Baker," said Dorgal.

Baker stopped. He turned round. Dorgal watched him take it in: Dorgal in the middle of the passage, Marco a step behind on the left, filling it, and the third man on the right, just at the edge of the lamplight, where his eyes didn't show.

Baker's face went very quiet. He put his hands together in front of him.

"Good evening," he said. "Master Sorvey."

"We've been admiring," said Dorgal, coming a step nearer, "your romance."

Baker looked at him.

"I'm sorry?"

"Oh, don't be modest. The whole school's talking about it. Breakfast, lunch and supper. The yard. The library. The workroom at the eleventh bell." Dorgal laid a hand on his heart. "It's touching, Baker, honestly. We're all touched. A lady of her years, and on the faculty, too, walking out with a third-year. Following him about like a governess with a very slow child. Does she hold your hand on the stairs?"

Marco laughed.

"I don't," said Baker carefully, "have a— I'm not walking out with anybody. Master Sorvey. I think there's been a mistake."

"No mistake. We've all seen her. The accounts mistress." Dorgal smiled. "I'll admit, when I heard you'd found yourself a sweetheart, she wasn't who I pictured. But then I thought, well. Be fair. He's a baker's boy. And she's a schoolmistress. She's practically marrying up."

Marco liked that one very much.

Baker didn't laugh, or flush, or say anything clever, or do any of the things Dorgal had arranged in his head for him to do. He stood among the wet coats with his hands together and looked at Dorgal with that curious, uncritical attention, as if Dorgal were a sigil he hadn't seen before and he was trying to find the hook in it. It was the way he'd looked at Dorgal across the bench in the workroom, that night, while he built fifty boards with his finger wandering off the line and Dorgal's perfect board lay under its cloth doing nothing.

Dorgal found he didn't like being looked at like that.

He glanced to his right without quite knowing why. The third man hadn't moved. He was watching Baker, and the gray eyes in their black were quite steady, and quite interested.

"Still," Dorgal heard himself say, "I suppose a man in your position takes what he's offered. Isn't that right? After the last one." He let it hang. "What was her name? Maria, wasn't it. Is it Maria? I heard she screamed the place down. I heard you hid in your room for a month."

It came out lightly, in his own voice, with the little lift at the end that he used for punchlines, and it was not a thing he had meant to say. He'd known about Maria for two years, the way everyone knew. He'd never thought it was funny. He stood there with it in the air between them and found, distantly, that his face was still smiling.

Marco had stopped laughing.

Baker had gone white. It was not much; he was pale anyway. But it went over him from his hair to his collar, and for a moment his hands came apart in front of him, and then he put them together again, carefully, one inside the other.

Then he looked past Dorgal. Past Marco's shoulder, down the passage, to the open door of the dining hall, with the light and the noise coming out of it, and the clatter of plates.

"Oh," said Baker, quietly.

Dorgal had no idea what that meant.

"Would you excuse me a moment?" said Baker. "I won't be long. I'm sorry."

He came forward. Dorgal, with no clear idea why, stepped aside to let him pass, and so did Marco, and Baker walked between them, politely, not hurrying, keeping exactly to the middle of the passage, and went back through the door into the dining hall.

Marco looked at Dorgal.

"Is he coming back?"

"Interesting," said Dorgal.

* * *

He was coming back. He came back down the passage among the coats with Mistress Wensa beside him, and she had her ledger under her arm.

Dorgal straightened his tunic before he knew he was doing it.

She walked the way she sat, upright and exact, and she didn't look at Baker as she came, or at Marco, or at Dorgal. She looked at the third man. She looked at him once, the whole length of him, from his boots to his face, and her eyes stopped on his face, and stayed there. Something in her shoulders settled, the way a cat settles before it moves.

The third man looked back at her with mild interest.

Baker stopped in the middle of the passage, halfway between them, and turned so that he could see both parties at once. He was still pale. He put his hands together in front of him, and he spoke clearly, and slowly, and correctly, the way a boy at a village school recites in front of the parson.

"Mistress Wensa," he said, "this is Master Dorgal Sorvey, who would like to frighten me. And these are his friends. I don't know their names, I'm afraid. They're helping him."

Marco opened his mouth and shut it.

"Master Sorvey," said Baker, turning, "this is Mistress Wensa, of the royal guard, who is following me." He hesitated. "You wanted to know why. I don't really know, I'm afraid. I think it's better if she tells you herself. She'll be able to explain it much better than I could."

There was a silence in the passage. Somewhere a coat dripped onto the flags.

Dorgal looked at the accounts mistress. Her face had not changed. That was the thing he would remember about it afterward, lying awake: that her face had not changed at all, and yet it had gone, in the space of one breath, from the face of a schoolmistress to something else entirely, something that made him want to be standing a good deal further back. Her hand was not quite at her belt. It was near it.

*Royal guard*, he thought. He looked at her belt. There was a hilt there, a plain one, cord-wrapped, a hand and a half long.

He had seen exactly one hilt like that in his life. It hung on Kolb.

"Well," said Baker. "Good night."

He bowed, to all of them equally, and turned and walked away down the passage toward the kitchen yard.

Nobody stopped him. Dorgal watched him go, because it was easier than watching anything else. Baker walked steadily, not hurrying, down the long dim aisle between the coats, and reached the yard door, and put his hand on the latch.

Then he stopped. He stood quite still with his hand on the latch. Dorgal saw his shoulders come up, slowly, almost to his ears.

"Oh, *Tor*," said Baker, very quietly, to the door.

He opened it and went out into the rain, a good deal faster than he'd been walking.

The door banged behind him. Dorgal turned back.

Mistress Wensa was looking at him now. She had not moved, except to take the ledger from under her arm and hold it, closed, in her left hand, the way someone holds a thing they don't want to be holding when the time comes to need both hands.

On Dorgal's right, very softly, the third man laughed.

"Mr. Sorvey," said the royal guard.
