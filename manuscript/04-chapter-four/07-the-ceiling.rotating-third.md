For two days after Kolb's challenge, Tor didn't build anything. He drew.

Rolph had seen him like this before, and knew better than to talk to him. He sat at the desk with a stack of the cheap gray paper from the store and drew on it, very fast, and then sat looking at what he'd drawn, and then crossed it out, and then drew the same thing again a little differently, and crossed that out too. He went to his classes. He came back and drew. He ate what was put in front of him, if it could be eaten with one hand. The pile of crossed-out sheets on the floor by his chair grew to the height of a boot, and then a shin, and Rolph, coming in from accounts on the second evening, kicked it over by accident and spent a quarter of an hour on his knees putting it back in an order he hoped was right.

"Sorry," he said.

"Mm," said Tor.

"Any good?"

Tor held one up without looking round. It was covered in little arrows. Some of them went up. Some of them went round in circles. One of them, near the bottom, went up, and then had a very small drawing next to it of a man with his arms out, looking surprised.

"Three," said Tor. "Three that might. The rest are pushing. It can't be pushing. He wasn't pushing. He was just—" He made a motion with his hand, a flat lift, like a man raising a tray. "Up."

"Right," said Rolph, who had not understood a word of it, and was perfectly happy.

It was the warmest week of the spring so far. The window stood open all day, and the room smelled of the wet grass down on the sheep field, and in the evenings you could hear the lambs. Half the dormitory had packed away their winter things. Rolph had packed his. He'd put his heavy coat into the chest at the foot of his bed on top of the lockbox, and lain on his bed with his boots off, listening to the lambs and to Tor's pen scratching, and thought that if this was what having a genius for a roommate meant, it was really very restful.

* * *

On the third day he came back from the Barrow at the ninth bell, pleasantly full of somebody else's ale, and opened the door, and stopped.

There was a plank on the ceiling.

It was a short plank, the length of his arm, pale pine, one of the offcuts Tor kept under the bed. It was pressed flat against the plaster directly above the middle of the room, as if someone had nailed it there. Nobody had nailed it there. It was humming, very faintly, like a bee in a jar, and a fine white dust of plaster was sifting down from around its edges and settling on everything below: the desk, the floor, and Tor.

Tor was standing on his bed. He had both arms stretched straight up over his head, and he was hanging from the plank by his fingertips, with his heels just off the blanket, and he was trying to pull it down.

It wasn't coming down.

Rolph stood in the doorway. He found he had nothing to say. He stood there and looked, for what was probably a long time, at his roommate dangling from the ceiling by a plank of wood, with plaster dust in his hair, swaying slightly.

"Hello," said Tor, from up there. "Sorry. I'll just be a moment."

"Take your time," said Rolph.

"It's not very strong," said Tor, a little breathlessly. "I only spent a few minutes on it. So I *should* be able to—" He hauled. The plank stayed exactly where it was. His heels came back down onto the blanket. "There. It's coming. It's definitely coming."

It was not coming.

Rolph came in and shut the door behind him, because there were some things the corridor did not need to see. He looked at the bed. Then he looked at the desk. Then he went and stood under the plank, next to the bed, and braced his feet.

"Get on my shoulders."

"Are you sure? I don't want to—"

"Tor. Get on."

Tor got on. He did it carefully and apologetically, with a knee in Rolph's ear and then a boot on his collarbone, and Rolph staggered and steadied and took hold of his ankles, and Tor reached up and got both hands round the plank and pulled.

The plank came down. It came slowly, an inch at a time, unwilling, humming louder as it came, the way a dog on a lead comes when it has seen a cat. Tor brought it down hand over hand to his chest. Rolph lowered himself, carefully, to his knees, and then Tor slid off him onto the floor, and the two of them sat there side by side on the boards in the plaster dust, with the plank across Tor's lap, held down with both his hands, straining gently upward like something that wanted to be let out.

Rolph looked at it.

"Is it meant to do that?" he said.

"Yes," said Tor. "Well. Partly." He looked at the plank. His hands were white on it. "It works. Rolph, it *works*, it goes up, it just goes up by itself, there's nothing under it, it's not pushing on anything at all. Look at it."

"I'm looking at it."

"It only goes *up*," said Tor.

There was a short silence.

"Well," said Rolph, reasonably, "what did you want it to do?"

"Everything *else*."

* * *

He spent the next four days trying to make it do everything else, and Rolph, who had never thought about it, learned more than he'd ever wanted to know about all the ways a plank of wood can fail to fly.

The first idea was a weight on one end, so it would lean, and go where it leaned. Rolph came back from theory to find a plank with a half-brick tied to it wedged into the far corner of the ceiling, above the window, tilted at a sad angle, where it had skidded along the plaster and stuck. It stayed there for a whole day and a night. They slept under it. Rolph lay awake in the dark, looking up at the pale shape of it in the corner, humming, with the half-brick hanging off it on its string, and directly underneath it, at the foot of his bed, the chest with his coat in it and the lockbox under the coat.

He'd slept under some strange ceilings in his time. He'd slept under ceilings with whole skies painted on them, gods and clouds and gilt stars, and once, very small, under one with a dragon that had frightened him for a year. He'd never slept under a plank. He found he didn't mind it.

"It's weakening," said Tor, in the dark, from the other bed. "By morning, I should think. I'm sorry."

"It's fine."

"If the string goes, the brick'll come down on your chest."

Rolph moved the chest.

The second idea was two fields, one on each end, to push and pull against each other. That was the one that cracked the window. Rolph wasn't there for it; he heard it from the stair, a crack and a bang and Tor saying "*Sorry!*" to nobody, and came in to find a long silver crack from corner to corner across the lower pane and two planks on the floor, both quite dead, lying side by side like a pair of boots, and Tor sitting between them with his head in his hands.

The third was a tail. A card, a thin strip of copper, a bird's feather, each tied to the back of the plank in turn, to steer it the way a rudder steers a boat. None of them did anything except hang off the plank and twirl. The feather was the worst, because it was so obviously, hopefully, a feather.

The fourth was a field that pulled instead of pushing. Rolph never fully understood what that one was meant to do. What it did was pull his boots, which were by the door, three feet up the wall and hold them there, side by side, toes pointing at the ceiling, as if an invisible man were standing on his hands. They stayed for an hour. Tor kept apologizing to them.

By the end of the fourth day there was a scorch mark on the corner of the desk the shape of a hand, and a broken pane in the window with a sheet of paper pasted over it, and a ring of fine white plaster dust on the floor under the middle of the ceiling that no amount of sweeping got all of. There were three dead planks under Tor's bed and a fourth in the corner. And Tor had stopped apologizing, which Rolph had learned was a bad sign, and had begun instead to sit very still at the desk for long periods, with his hands flat on the wood, looking at nothing.

"Supper," said Rolph, on the fourth night, and put it in front of him.

* * *

It was bread and soup and a pot of tea, because Hob had taken pity. Tor ate the bread without noticing. Then he poured himself tea, and put in sugar, and stirred it, and Rolph, who was eating his own soup and watching him over the spoon, saw him stop.

He'd stopped with the spoon in the cup. He was looking at the tea. The tea was going round. It went round after the spoon, in a slow brown swirl, the way tea does, carrying a few leaves with it, and as Rolph watched, Tor moved the spoon, very slightly, the other way. The swirl slowed. It turned. It went the other way, after the spoon.

Tor lifted the spoon out of the cup and held it up and looked at it.

He turned it over in his fingers. Rolph had seen him do that with a sigil, with a square of copper, once with a cabbage stalk. He turned the spoon over, and back, and then he went perfectly still, with the spoon held up in front of his face, and a drop of tea gathered on the end of it and fell back into the cup.

"Rolph," he said.

"Mm."

"What if it wasn't on the board?"

"What wasn't?"

"The telling. Which way to go." He was looking at the spoon. "I keep trying to put the telling on the plank. The weight, the tail, the second field, all of it. All of it's on the plank. But the tea doesn't know which way to go. The spoon knows. And you hold the spoon." He put it down on the saucer, very carefully, as if it were made of glass. "What if you *held* it? Something small. In your hand. And it tells the big one. And you just—" He moved his hand, flat, slowly, up, and forward, and round. "You just *tell* it."

Rolph looked at the hand.

Then he looked at Tor's face, which had gone the way it went: still, and far off, and lit from somewhere behind, like a window with a lamp in it a long way down a road.

"Tor," he said.

"Mm?"

"Eat your soup first."

Tor looked down at the soup as though he'd never seen soup. Then he picked up the spoon again, the same spoon, the tea spoon, and began to eat it, absently, with his left hand, while his right hand went to the stack of gray paper and the pen and began, very fast, to draw.

Rolph watched him for a while. Then he got up and went to the door, where his boots had finally come down off the wall that afternoon, and set them straight, side by side. He looked up at the corner of the ceiling above the window, where the half-brick plank had stuck for a day and a night and left a long pale scrape in the plaster, which would probably stay there until someone came with a ladder.

He found he didn't mind that either.
