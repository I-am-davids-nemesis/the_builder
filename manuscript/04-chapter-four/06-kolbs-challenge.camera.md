On the wet sand of the practice yard, Tor sparred with Trice and Sara, both in black. Trice opened with a spinning kick. Sara came in low on the other side. Tor was half a step to the left of both, apologizing.

Each time Trice moved fast, his right hand flew up and slapped flat against his chest, over the copper plate under his tunic, and stayed there while the rest of him dodged.

Kolb stood at the edge of the sand with his arms folded. His lips moved slightly as he watched, counting.

"Hold," he said.

They held. Trice was breathing hard. Sara wasn't. Tor's hand was on his chest again. He saw Kolb looking at it, took it away, and put it behind his back.

"Sorry, sir. I keep doing that."

"I see you do."

"It's for the shield, sir. I'm practising. I'm trying to be quicker." His eyes went across the yard to the bench by the water butt, where Mistress Wensa sat with nothing on her knee and the hilt plain on her belt. He lowered his voice. "Trice has been throwing things."

"Patricia," said Kolb, "throws things at everyone."

"Not breakfast, sir. Not usually."

Trice looked at the sky.

"Black," said Kolb. "Pell. Both of you. Two hundred."

Sara went without a word. Trice went with a look back at Tor. Kolb watched her go and said nothing.

* * *

He walked Tor over to the water butt. As they came, Wensa got up from the bench and went to stand by the gate, a little way off. Kolb nodded to her. She nodded back.

Kolb scooped a dipper of water, drank, and looked at Tor. Tor stood very straight, hands behind his back, face polite and braced.

"Shield's done," said Kolb.

"Yes, sir. Well. Mostly, sir. There's still the—" Tor's hand started toward his chest and stopped halfway. He put it back. "There's the one that isn't."

"You'll get it."

"I hope so, sir."

"You're bored," said Kolb.

Tor blinked. "No, sir."

"You're bored. You keep looking at the pond." Kolb jerked his chin down the slope. "Third time today. You walked along the bottom of it. Half the school saw you. And now you've done it, and there's nothing down there, and you keep looking at it anyway." He wiped his mouth. "That's a man who wants the next thing."

Tor opened his mouth, and shut it.

"So here's the next thing," said Kolb. "Make one that flies."

"Sir?"

"A man. Make a man fly." Kolb hung the dipper back on its hook. "Something he wears. Like the shield. He puts it on, up he goes."

Tor's face didn't move for a long moment. Then he shook his head, slowly.

"It isn't possible, sir."

"No?"

"No, sir. I'm sorry. A field pushes. That's all it does, really, it pushes things, or it pulls them, but it has to have something to do it against. The shield pushes everything away from me, but I'm standing on the ground, and the ground pushes back, so I stay where I am. If I wanted it to push *me*—" He held one hand flat in the air and pushed at it with the other. "It'd have to push against something. The ground, I suppose. And the higher you went, the less ground there'd be to push on. It'd be like trying to lift yourself up by your own boots, sir. You can pull as hard as you like. You don't go anywhere."

"Austrans do it."

Tor stopped.

"The Austrans," he said.

"Whole wings of them." Kolb looked past him at the hills beyond the valley, going blue in the afternoon. "Come over the ridge at first light, like geese. High up. Too high for a bow. And you stand in the valley with your shield and your cutter and your fine kit, and they're up there, and you're down here, and they choose. Where, and when, and who." He was quiet a moment. "Why do you think we watch them the way we do? Why do you think every border fort we've got keeps a man on the roof, day and night, looking up? It's not that they've got more men. They haven't. It's that. It's the one thing they've got and we haven't."

Tor followed his look to the hills.

"I thought that was a story, sir," he said faintly. "The flying. The second-years tell it. I thought it was one of the ones they tell."

"I've seen it. Last time I was away."

Tor turned and looked at him, at the pale scar from eye to jaw. Kolb looked back and said nothing more about it.

"And you've seen it, Baker," he said. "Haven't you."

"Sir?"

"Three weeks back. Over the sheep field. In the middle of the night. Thompson."

Tor's face changed.

"That was the rage, sir," he said. "That was— he wasn't himself. It's his family's, I think, it's in the blood, it's not—" He stopped. "It's not a *device*."

"Did the rage have something to push on?"

Tor's mouth opened. Nothing came out.

His eyes went up, to the sky over the yard: thin high cloud, a single rook flapping toward the wood. They stayed there. Then they came down, slowly, all the way to his own boots on the wet sand, and stayed there.

Kolb waited, arms folded, and didn't speak.

"He went *up*," said Tor, to his boots. "He didn't jump. He just went up. There was nothing under him. He— Oh."

"Baker."

"Sorry, sir." He didn't look up. "Sorry. It's not pushing, then. Or it is, but not on the— It'd have to be— Oh, that's *stupid*, I'm so stupid, it was right there, he was right *there*—"

He turned and walked away across the yard, still talking.

He didn't wait to be dismissed. He walked past the racks and past the pell, where Trice and Sara had stopped to stare, and past the gate, where Wensa stepped aside for him, and up the steps to the long hall with his hands clasped behind his back and his head down, muttering. The door closed behind him.

Wensa looked at Kolb across the yard.

Kolb shrugged.

"Nice and loose," he said, to nobody. Then, louder, without turning: "Black. I said two hundred. That's sixty."

Behind him, the cutting started again.
