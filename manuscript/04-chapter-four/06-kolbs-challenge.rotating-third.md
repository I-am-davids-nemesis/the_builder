Kolb had Baker on the sand with the two in black, and he wasn't watching the fight.

He didn't need to. He knew how it would go; he'd been watching it go that way for three weeks. Patricia would open with something showy, because she was cross, and she was always cross with Baker now, in a way that had nothing to do with fighting. Debri would come in low on the other side while Baker was busy with the showy thing. And Baker would be somewhere else, a half-step left of wherever either of them had aimed, apologizing.

What Kolb was watching was the hand.

Baker had the shield on. They all wore them now, the ones he'd given them to. Kolb had the rest of the school's in the armory chest under the long hall, thirty of them, in a sack, and he'd given out four, and he knew who'd get the next six. The rule was it stayed off in class. A man who fights behind a field learns to fight like a man behind a field, and the day the field isn't there, he dies of it. Baker knew the rule. Baker kept it, the way he kept every rule. And still, every time Patricia moved quickly, his right hand flew up and slapped flat against his chest, over the copper, and stayed there, while the rest of him went on dodging.

Twelve times in four minutes. Kolb counted.

"Hold," he said.

They held. Patricia was breathing hard. Debri wasn't. Baker had his hand on his chest again, and when he saw Kolb looking at it, he took it away and put it behind his back, like a boy caught with a biscuit.

"Sorry, sir. I keep doing that."

"I see you do."

"It's for the shield, sir. I'm practising. I'm trying to be quicker." He glanced across the yard, to the bench by the water butt, where Mistress Wensa was sitting with nothing on her knee and the hilt at her belt in plain sight. He lowered his voice. "Trice has been throwing things."

"Patricia," said Kolb, "throws things at everyone."

"Not breakfast, sir. Not usually."

Kolb considered that. Patricia was looking at the sky, innocent as a cat.

"Black," he said. "Pell. Both of you. Two hundred."

They went. Debri went without a word. Patricia went with a look at Baker that Kolb filed away and didn't open; he'd been teaching in this yard since before the racks were built, and he'd learned the hard way which looks were his business.

* * *

He walked Baker over to the water butt, away from the racks. Mistress Wensa got up from the bench as they came and went and stood by the gate instead, a little way off, where she could still see. Kolb nodded to her. She nodded back. Two professionals, passing on a stair.

He scooped water from the butt and drank it, and looked at Baker, and Baker looked back with that polite, slightly braced face he always wore when he thought he was about to be told he'd done something wrong.

"Shield's done," said Kolb.

"Yes, sir. Well. Mostly, sir. There's still the—" The hand went up toward his chest, and stopped halfway, and he put it back behind him. "There's the one that isn't."

"You'll get it."

"I hope so, sir."

"You're bored," said Kolb.

Baker blinked. "No, sir."

"You're bored. You keep looking at the pond." Kolb jerked his chin down the slope. "Third time today. You walked along the bottom of it. Half the school saw you. And now you've done it, and there's nothing down there, and you keep looking at it anyway." He wiped his mouth. "That's a man who wants the next thing."

Baker opened his mouth. He shut it.

The head of the black school had sat in Kolb's office, the week he set the shield, with his boots on Kolb's desk, and asked what the boy could do. *Anything you give him,* Kolb had said. He hadn't known then if it was true. He'd said it the way you say a horse will jump, because you've seen how it looks at the fence.

"So here's the next thing," said Kolb. "Make one that flies."

Baker looked at him.

"Sir?"

"A man. Make a man fly." Kolb put the dipper back on its hook. "Something he wears. Like the shield. He puts it on, up he goes."

He watched Baker hear it. He watched the polite face stay exactly where it was while everything behind it stopped. Then Baker shook his head, slowly, the way a man shakes his head at a peddler who has offered him a cure for death.

"It isn't possible, sir."

"No?"

"No, sir. I'm sorry." Baker was quite certain. He wasn't apologizing for the idea; he was apologizing for the world. "A field pushes. That's all it does, really, it pushes things, or it pulls them, but it has to have something to do it against. The shield pushes everything away from me, but I'm standing on the ground, and the ground pushes back, so I stay where I am. If I wanted it to push *me*—" He held one hand flat in the air and pushed at it with the other. "It'd have to push against something. The ground, I suppose. And the higher you went, the less ground there'd be to push on. It'd be like trying to lift yourself up by your own boots, sir. You can pull as hard as you like. You don't go anywhere."

"Austrans do it."

Baker stopped.

"The Austrans," he said.

"Whole wings of them." Kolb looked past him, at the long line of the hills beyond the valley, going blue with the afternoon. "Come over the ridge at first light, like geese. High up. Too high for a bow. And you stand in the valley with your shield and your cutter and your fine kit, and they're up there, and you're down here, and they choose. Where, and when, and who." He was quiet for a moment. "Why do you think we watch them the way we do? Why do you think every border fort we've got keeps a man on the roof, day and night, looking up? It's not that they've got more men. They haven't. It's that. It's the one thing they've got and we haven't."

He hadn't meant to say so much. He didn't, as a rule. He looked back at Baker.

Baker was looking at the hills too.

"I thought that was a story, sir," he said, rather faintly. "The flying. The second-years tell it. I thought it was one of the ones they tell."

"I've seen it. Last time I was away."

Baker turned and looked at him. Kolb let him look. He didn't explain the scar, or the year, or where, or how long he'd been gone, or why he always came back; there are things you say to a boy and things you don't, and he'd said enough.

"And you've seen it, Baker," he said. "Haven't you."

"Sir?"

"Three weeks back. Over the sheep field. In the middle of the night. Thompson."

Baker's face changed.

"That was the rage, sir," he said. "That was— he wasn't himself. It's his family's, I think, it's in the blood, it's not—" He stopped. "It's not a *device*."

"Did the rage have something to push on?"

Baker opened his mouth.

Nothing came out of it. Kolb watched. He'd seen this face before, in the yard weeks back with the sand hissing away in a ring, and once in the cafeteria over a bowl of soup, and he'd learned to keep quiet for it. Baker's mouth stayed a little open. His eyes went up, to the sky over the yard, the thin high cloud, a single rook going over toward the wood. They stayed there for a while. Then they came down, slowly, all the way down, to his own feet on the wet sand, and stayed there.

"He went *up*," said Baker, to his boots. "He didn't jump. He just went up. There was nothing under him. He— Oh."

"Baker."

"Sorry, sir." He didn't look up. "Sorry. It's not pushing, then. Or it is, but not on the— It'd have to be— Oh, that's *stupid*, I'm so stupid, it was right there, he was right *there*—"

He turned round, still talking, and walked away across the yard.

He didn't ask to be dismissed. He didn't look at the racks, or the pell, where Patricia and Debri had stopped cutting to stare at him, or the bench, or Mistress Wensa at the gate, who stepped aside for him without being asked. He went straight up the steps to the long hall with his hands clasped behind his back and his head down, muttering, and went in, and was gone.

Mistress Wensa looked at Kolb across the yard.

Kolb shrugged.

"Nice and loose," he said, to nobody. Then, louder, without turning round: "Black. I said two hundred. That's sixty."

Behind him, the cutting started again.
