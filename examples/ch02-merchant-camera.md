# Chapter 2, the merchant: external, camera-like narrator

**Experiment.** Mode: external, camera-like narrator (option 3). Nobody's thoughts; only what can be seen and heard. Beats follow `outline/chapters.md`, "The merchant, beat by beat." The dialogue stays close to `ch02-merchant-rotating-third.md` so the modes can be compared.

---

The building room ran along the north side of the hall, and the morning light came in flat and even across two rows of scarred benches. Twenty students sat at them, copying lamp-squares: a circle, a cross, a little hooked line, forty times over.

Dorgal had three pens laid out in a row and was using the middle one. Across the aisle and one bench down, Tor held his pen carefully. There was a bruise on the back of his hand the size and color of a plum. When he reached for the ink, he did it slowly, like an old man.

The door opened. A porter in gray crossed to the building master's desk and gave him a card. The building master held it up to the window and tipped it so the light ran across it. It was heavy and cream-colored, with a small mark pressed into one corner: a ring of knotted rope with a letter inside it.

Dorgal looked down at his square.

The building master put the card in his apron and looked out of the window, down into the court, for a moment. "Baker. With me."

Tor looked up. He put down his pen, wiped it, put it down again a little farther from the edge, and stood.

"Off to be dunked again, Baker?" said Dorgal, without raising his head. "Do try to float this time."

Someone laughed. "I'll try," said Tor, seriously, and followed the building master out.

Dorgal finished his circle and began the cross. On the second stroke the nib split with a small, clean sound and left a blot in the middle of the square. He looked at it. Then he laid the pen at the end of the row and picked up the left-hand one.

Through the window, between the frame and the corner of the dormitory, a black carriage stood in the court with a pair of grays in the traces. On its door was the same knotted ring. A broad man in a brown coat stood at the horses' heads, talking to one of them. He had a new-looking beard.

Dorgal looked at the carriage for some time. Then he started a new square.

---

The building master's office smelled of linseed and cold ashes. A man sat in the master's chair, behind the desk, facing the door. He wasn't large. He wore a dark coat, very well cut, and there were two rings on his right hand and one on his left. The hands under the rings were broad, the knuckles flattened. A painted square of pine lay on the desk in front of him beside a dry handkerchief, folded.

Tor came in behind the building master and stopped two steps inside the door. The door was still close at his back.

The man smiled. "Sit down, Scholar Baker."

There was one other chair. Tor looked at it, then at the building master, who nodded, and went to the window. Tor sat on the front third of the chair with his hands on his knees.

"Sir," he said. "Good morning, sir."

"Good morning. My name's Sorvey. I buy things. I'm told you've made a thing that dries laundry."

"Yes, sir. It's not really anything. It's only a field for cloth, it lifts the water out, it's on pine, the pine's not very good—"

"I've seen it." Sorvey touched the folded handkerchief with one finger. "It's on pine. It works. I'd like as many as you can make." He took a small purse from inside his coat and set it on the desk, closed. "Two gold each, Scholar Baker. I'll send the boards and the paint. I'll send a cart for them when they're done. You'll never have to carry one anywhere or speak to anyone about it. You build them, I take them, you're paid."

Tor's eyes went to the purse and away again, quickly. His hands tightened on his knees. His shoulders came forward a little.

"That's very generous, sir," he said.

"It is."

"Only, I'm afraid I—" He stopped. He looked at the building master, whose back was to the room. He looked at Sorvey's hands, resting on the desk. Sorvey didn't move them. "I've already got someone," said Tor. "To do that. Represent me. Miss Sara is. Representing me. In this." A pause. "I think."

"You think," said Sorvey.

"She is. Sir." Tor's lips moved, as if he were adding something up. "She priced it, at the laundry, and Miss Trice said—and then they—yes. Miss Sara Debri. She is."

Sorvey looked at him. Tor looked back for as long as he could, and then looked at the desk.

"Debri," said Sorvey.

At the window, the building master shifted his weight. Sorvey didn't look around.

"Then I won't tread on the Debris' toes," he said. "Not over laundry." He put the purse back inside his coat. "But I'll tell you what I'll do, Scholar Baker. Anything new you make, anything at all, whether it dries a shirt or lights a lamp or does something nobody's thought of, I'll pay for the making of it. Before it's made, if you like. You tell me what you want to try, and I'll pay for the trying." He smiled. "Failures included."

"Thank you, sir," said Tor.

Sorvey took a card from his waistcoat, cream, with the knotted ring in the corner, and held it out across the desk. Tor took it by the very edges and didn't look at it.

"Keep it somewhere you'll find it." Sorvey stood. Tor was on his feet a beat later. "Master. Thank you for the use of your chair."

The building master turned from the window. He didn't speak. Sorvey shook his hand.

At the door, Sorvey stopped beside Tor, close. Tor stood very still.

"You've a good eye for a room, Scholar Baker," said Sorvey pleasantly. "Who taught you that?"

"Nobody, sir," said Tor. "Sorry, sir."

Sorvey nodded and went out, pulling on a pair of gray gloves, a finger at a time.

---

In the corridor, the first-year sat on the bench with a cloth bundle of lamp-squares on his knees. Sorvey stopped in front of him, still working the second glove onto his hand, and looked down.

"Good morning," Sorvey said.

The first-year's mouth opened. Nothing came out.

Sorvey smiled and walked away down the corridor. His boots made no sound on the boards. The first-year watched him all the way to the corner.

Behind him, the office door stood ajar.

"That was Sorvey," the building master said.

"Yes, sir. He seemed very—" Tor stopped. "He seemed very sure, sir."

"He is a buyer, Baker. One of the biggest in the north. He came here because I asked him to." The master's voice was entirely flat. "And you've sent him away."

"I'm sorry, sir. I didn't mean to be ungrateful. Only, Miss Sara—"

"Yes. Debri." A pause. Something was picked up from the desk and put down. "You've a great deal to learn about the trade, Baker. Let me tell you one piece of it for nothing. When a builder's piece goes through testing, it is not unusual for the builder to remember the testing. Testing is work. Whoever tests a piece puts his name to it. It wouldn't be a bad idea, when your money comes in, to see that he's not forgotten."

On the bench, the first-year put both hands over his mouth.

"Oh," said Tor. "Oh, of course, sir. That's only fair. I hadn't thought. I'm sorry, I should have thought of it myself, testing's ever so much work. Who does the testing, sir? I'll go and find him. I'll ask Miss Sara how much is usual."

The office was silent. The first-year leaned, very slightly, toward the gap in the door. Through it he could see the end of the desk, and the building master's face, long and gray, and on it an expression that held for a long moment and gave nothing away except that it was holding.

"Go back to class, Baker."

"Yes, sir. Thank you, sir. Sorry, sir."

Tor came out and nearly walked into the bench. "Oh, hello! It's you. Are you late again?"

"He was asking you for a—" the first-year began, and stopped.

"For a what?"

"Nothing."

"He's very fair, I think," said Tor. "Don't you? He thinks of things."

He looked down at the card in his fingers: cream, heavy, the knotted ring in the corner. He held it a moment longer. Then he slid it into the front of his tunic, slowly, holding it well away from himself until the last moment.

"Good luck with your squares," he said, and went off up the corridor toward the north room, walking stiffly.

The first-year sat on the bench. After a while, the building master appeared in the doorway and held out his hand. The first-year gave him the lamp-squares. The master looked at the top one, tipped it to the light, and handed the bundle back.

"Again," he said. "The cross is crooked."

He shut the door.

## How this draft differs from the rotating third

- **What's lost:**
  - Dorgal's private hints: that he could draw the mark in the dark, that he knows the coachman's name (Abel), that he was "trained" not to get bored, and that he managed not to look at the window for a quarter of a bell. Here only the split nib and his long look at the carriage remain, so the father-son tie is fainter.
  - Sorvey's reading of Tor: the stop at the door as something he's seen in men who've met him before, "described at length, over two dinners," and his rare, unpleasant liking for a boy who refuses money for the wrong reason.
  - The first-year's terror of Sorvey as a rabbit's fear of a shadow, and his attempt to describe the master's face to three people.
- **What's gained:**
  - Sorvey is more frightening without an inner voice: rings on broad, flattened knuckles, the chair facing the door, silent boots.
  - The folded, dry handkerchief on the desk shows he tested the field himself without anyone saying so.
  - The master's face "holding" is more ambiguous with no guesses offered.
  - The office scene is quicker, and Tor's "I think" lands harder in plain dialogue.
