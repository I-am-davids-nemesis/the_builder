## Dorgal

The building room was the long one on the north side, where the light came in flat and even all morning and the benches were scarred by forty years of other people's mistakes. The morning after the sand, twenty of them sat at it in two rows, copying lamp-squares. It was the second week of lamp-squares. There would be a third.

Dorgal had laid out his three pens in a row and was using the middle one. Across the aisle and one bench down, Baker was using whatever pen had come to hand, and was holding it, Dorgal noticed, rather carefully, because there was a bruise on the back of that hand the size and color of a plum. He moved like an old man when he reached for the ink. He had moved like an old man coming in, too, and two of the second-years at the back had made a little show of flinching away from him, and Baker had apologized to them.

The whole school had heard about the sand by now. Dorgal had heard it four times before breakfast, each version larger. In the last one Trice had been thrown over the wall.

"Interesting," he'd said, each time.

He bent over his square. The sigil for a lamp-square was simple, a circle, a cross, a little hooked line for the light to come out of, and the trick of it was doing the simple thing perfectly forty times without once getting bored. Dorgal was very good at not getting bored. He'd been trained to it.

The door opened. A porter in the school's gray came in, went to the building master's desk, and gave him a card.

The building master read it. He was a long gray folded sort of man, and he read the way he inspected work, holding the card up to the window and tipping it so the light ran across it. Dorgal, who had very good eyes, saw the card catch the light. It was heavy and cream-colored, and pressed into one corner was a mark he could have drawn in the dark: a ring of knotted rope with a letter inside it.

He looked down at his square.

The building master put the card in his apron. He looked out of the window for a moment, down at the court. Then he said, "Baker. With me."

Baker's head came up. He had the face of a boy who has been called to the front of a room many times and never once for anything good. He put his pen down, wiped it, put it down again in a slightly better place, and stood, carefully.

"Off to be dunked again, Baker?" said Dorgal, without looking up. "Do try to float this time."

Somebody laughed. Baker said, "I'll try," quite seriously, and followed the building master out.

Dorgal finished his circle. He started on the cross. On the second stroke the nib of the middle pen split with a small, clean sound and put a blot the size of a fingernail in the middle of the square.

He looked at it for a while. Then he set the pen down at the end of the row and took up the left-hand one.

From where he sat he could see a slice of the court through the window, between the frame and the corner of the dormitory. There was a carriage in it. It was black and plain and very well kept, with a pair of grays in the traces, and on the door, small, the same knotted ring. The coachman was standing at the horses' heads, a broad man in a brown coat, talking to one of them.

Abel had grown a beard, Dorgal noticed. It didn't suit him.

He went back to his square. He started a new one. He didn't look at the window again for almost a quarter of a bell, which was, he felt, a creditable effort.

## Sorvey

The boy stopped two steps inside the door.

That was the first thing Douglas Sorvey noticed about him, and it was the most interesting thing he'd see all morning. Most people came all the way into a room. They came in until something stopped them: a chair, a desk, a person. The boy had stopped where nothing stopped him, with the door still close behind him and the building master's shoulder half in the way, and he'd stopped there as if he'd walked up to the edge of something. Sorvey had seen men stop like that. Men who'd been in rooms with him before. He'd never seen a boy do it on the first try.

He was smaller than he'd been described. He'd been described at length, over two dinners.

"Sit down, Scholar Baker," Sorvey said, and smiled.

He was in the building master's chair, because the building master had offered it to him, and because it faced the door. There was one other chair. The boy looked at it, then at the master, who nodded. The boy sat on the front third of it with his hands on his knees. There was a bruise on one hand like a plum.

"Sir," the boy said. "Good morning, sir."

"Good morning. My name's Sorvey. I buy things." He let that sit. "I'm told you've made a thing that dries laundry."

"Yes, sir. It's not really anything. It's only a field for cloth, it lifts the water out, it's on pine, the pine's not very good—"

"I've seen it," said Sorvey. He had; the master had shown it to him that morning, on the desk, in the clean light. He had held it in his hand and dried his own handkerchief with it, twice, and then he had put it down and said nothing for some time. "It's on pine. It works. I'd like as many as you can make." He took out a small purse and set it on the desk, not open, just present. "Two gold each, Scholar Baker. I'll send the boards and the paint. I'll send a cart for them when they're done. You'll never have to carry one anywhere or speak to anyone about it. You build them, I take them, you're paid."

He watched the boy hear *two gold*.

It went into him like water into dry ground. His eyes went to the purse and away again, fast, as though looking at it were rude. Sorvey had expected that, more or less. A baker's son on a king's charity, the master had said. Two gold was a month of bread in the woods. What he hadn't expected was the next thing: the boy's hands tightening on his knees, and his shoulders coming a quarter-inch forward, the way a man's do when he's decided to get up and leave and hasn't told his legs yet.

He wasn't going to take it. He thought it was a fortune, and he wasn't going to take it.

"That's very generous, sir," the boy said.

"It is."

"Only, I'm afraid I—" The boy stopped. He looked at the building master, who looked at the window. He looked at Sorvey's hands, which were resting on the desk, and stayed on them a moment. Sorvey didn't move them. "I've already got someone," the boy said. "To do that. Represent me. Miss Sara is. Representing me. In this." A pause, short and dreadful. "I think."

"You think," said Sorvey.

"She is. Sir." Then, as if he were checking the sum after he'd written it down: "She priced it, at the laundry, and Miss Trice said—and then they—yes. Miss Sara Debri. She is."

Sorvey looked at him for a while. The boy looked back, very politely, for as long as he could, which was longer than most grown men managed, and then looked at the desk.

"Debri," Sorvey said.

The building master shifted his weight by the window. Sorvey didn't look around at him.

Debri's girl would have told him a figure, at that laundry. Sorvey could guess the figure, give or take. And the boy had heard it, and heard two, and called two *generous*, and still sat on the front of his chair with his hands tight on his knees, saying no. Not for the money. Sorvey turned that over and found that he liked it, which was a rare and slightly unpleasant feeling.

"Then I won't tread on the Debris' toes," he said. "Not over laundry." He picked up the purse and put it back inside his coat. "But I'll tell you what I'll do, Scholar Baker. Anything new you make, anything at all, whether it dries a shirt or lights a lamp or does something nobody's thought of, I'll pay for the making of it. Before it's made, if you like. You tell me what you want to try, and I'll pay for the trying." He smiled again. "Failures included."

"Thank you, sir," the boy said. He said it the way you'd say it to a large dog that had decided, for now, not to bite.

Sorvey took a card from his waistcoat and held it out across the desk. Heavy, cream, the knotted ring in the corner. The boy took it by the very edges, the way you'd hold a plate that had come out of the oven, and didn't look at it.

"Keep it somewhere you'll find it," Sorvey said. He stood. He was not especially tall, but he took up the room when he stood, and the boy was on his feet a beat later without seeming to have decided to be. "Master. Thank you for the use of your chair."

The building master said nothing at all. Sorvey shook his hand anyway, and found it stiff.

At the door he stopped beside the boy, close, and the boy stood very still.

"You've a good eye for a room, Scholar Baker," he said pleasantly. "Who taught you that?"

"Nobody, sir," said the boy. "Sorry, sir."

Sorvey nodded, as if that were the answer he'd expected, and went out, pulling on his gloves.

## The first-year

He was four days late with the lamp-squares now, and he'd thought, wrongly, that the building master's door would be the quietest place in the school to wait.

The door opened and a man came out. He was pulling on gloves, gray ones, a finger at a time, and he wasn't tall, and there was nothing whatever about him to be frightened of. The first-year was frightened of him at once and completely, the way a rabbit is frightened of a shadow. The man looked down at him on the bench.

"Good morning," the man said.

The first-year's mouth opened. Nothing came out of it.

The man smiled, as if that were a perfectly good answer, and went off down the corridor at an easy walk. His boots made no noise on the boards, which the first-year noticed, and then wished he hadn't.

He had left the door ajar. Through it, the first-year could see a slice of the office: the end of the desk, the window, the building master's back, and the scholarship boy, Baker, standing with something white held flat between his fingertips.

"That was Sorvey," the building master said.

"Yes, sir. He seemed very—" Baker stopped. "He seemed very sure, sir."

"He is a buyer, Baker. One of the biggest in the north. He came here because I asked him to." There was nothing in the master's voice at all, which, the first-year had learned in the washing house, was where the master kept the most. "And you've sent him away."

"I'm sorry, sir. I didn't mean to be ungrateful. Only, Miss Sara—"

"Yes. Debri." A pause. The master turned from the window. The first-year could see his face now, long and gray and closed. "You've a great deal to learn about the trade, Baker. Let me tell you one piece of it for nothing." He picked up something from the desk, put it down again. "When a builder's piece goes through testing, it is not unusual for the builder to remember the testing. Testing is work. Whoever tests a piece puts his name to it. It wouldn't be a bad idea, when your money comes in, to see that he's not forgotten."

On the bench, the first-year put both hands over his mouth.

"Oh," said Baker. He sounded, of all things, relieved. "Oh, of course, sir. That's only fair. I hadn't thought. I'm sorry, I should have thought of it myself, testing's ever so much work." He nodded several times. "Who does the testing, sir? I'll go and find him. I'll ask Miss Sara how much is usual."

There was a silence in the office.

It went on for some time. The first-year, who could see the building master's face, watched something happen in it that he would never be able to describe afterward, though he tried, that night, to three people. It wasn't anger. It might have been something like pain. It might, very possibly, have been the beginning of a laugh, strangled at birth.

"Go back to class, Baker," the building master said.

"Yes, sir. Thank you, sir. Sorry, sir."

Baker came out. He nearly walked into the bench, and stopped, and his face lit with recognition. "Oh, hello! It's you. Are you late again?"

"He was asking you for a—" the first-year began, and stopped, because the boy was looking at him with such open, interested courtesy that the rest of the sentence simply died.

"For a what?"

"Nothing."

"He's very fair, I think," Baker said. "Don't you? He thinks of things." He looked down at the card between his fingertips, and for the first time seemed to see it: the cream, the weight, the little knotted ring in the corner. His face did something complicated. He put the card into his tunic, carefully, at arm's length, the way you'd put away something that might go off. "Good luck with your squares."

He went off up the corridor toward the north room, walking stiffly, like an old man.

The first-year sat on the bench. After a while, the building master appeared in the doorway and held out his hand without a word, and the first-year gave him the lamp-squares. The master looked at the top one, tipped it to the light, and handed it back.

"Again," he said. "The cross is crooked."

He shut the door.

## Notes on the mode

- **The father-son tie is shown, never said.** Dorgal knows the card's mark in the dark, splits a nib, knows the coachman by name. Sorvey has had Tor "described over two dinners." A reader can put it together; Tor never does.
- **The danger comes through Sorvey's own eye.** We never hear Tor's alarm. Sorvey notices the stop at the door, the hands on the knees, the glance at his own hands, and the reader reads Tor's fear through what a dangerous man notices about it.
- **The instructor stays opaque.** The first-year sees the silence but can't read it, so the reader can't either: mercenary, embarrassed, or amused.
- **The laugh line is Tor's question** ("Who does the testing, sir?"), and it works because the first-year's hands are already over his mouth.
