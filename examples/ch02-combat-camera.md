# Chapter 2, combat class: external, camera-like narrator

**Experiment.** Mode: external, camera-like narrator (option 3). Nobody's thoughts; only what can be seen and heard. Beats follow `outline/chapters.md`, "Combat class, beat by beat." The dialogue stays close to `ch02-combat-rotating-third.md` so the modes can be compared.

---

The yard lay behind the long hall: a square of packed sand the color of old bread, weapon racks along three sides, and along the fourth a wall that had plainly belonged to something else before the school was built against it. In the middle stood the pell, an oak trunk as thick as a man, sunk to half its height. At chest height the wood was beaten pale and furred.

Kolb was hitting it with a wooden waster about as slender as a fence post. He set his feet, turned from the hips, and struck. The sound was flat and enormous. The racks rattled. Dust jumped off the top of the wall and hung there.

Tor came through the gate. Rolph came two steps behind him.

"Sir," Tor said.

The waster came round again. The pell took it. A chip of mortar fell off the wall.

"Baker." Kolb lowered the waster and turned. He was smiling. Rolph, seeing it, stopped walking. "Good. You're early. I like early." He looked past Tor. "Rolph. Go away."

"Sir, I said I'd partner him, I thought—"

"And I've said go away. That's two things said. Mine's the one that counts." A pause. "You can stand by the rack. Stand by the rack and be quiet, and you can watch. Most people pay to watch me."

Rolph went to the rack and stopped there.

"Know what that is, Baker?"

"A pell, sir."

"What does it do?"

Tor looked at the pell for some time.

"It stands there, sir," he said. "Mostly."

Kolb laughed, short and pleased. "It does. It's very good at it. Watch."

He hit it again. The trunk shuddered, all the way down into the ground. At the rack, Rolph shifted his feet.

"That," Kolb said, in the same pleasant voice, "would kill you. Not hurt you. Kill you. Your ribs would go in and your heart would stop and you'd lie down on my sand and be dead, and I'd have to write to your mother. Unless you had armor on, or a shield field. Have you got a shield on you, Baker?"

"No, sir."

"No. Most people haven't."

He leaned the waster against the pell, walked to the end of the rack where Rolph stood, and took down a long case of oiled leather without looking at him. He opened it on the sand. Inside lay a blade about four feet long, gray, plain, unpolished. As Kolb lifted the case, the blade turned edge-on toward the gate and vanished: a hilt, and nothing, and then, as the case tipped back, a blade again. A line of small regular marks ran down the flat near the hilt.

"Cutter," said Kolb. "You'll have seen pictures."

Tor nodded. His face had lost most of its color.

Kolb took the cutter in both hands, walked to the pell, and swung once, level, with no visible effort.

There was no sound. The blade went through the oak without slowing and came out the other side, and Kolb let it finish its swing. For a moment nothing else happened. Then the top half of the pell, taller than Kolb, leaned a little to one side, slid off the bottom half, and fell onto the sand with a thump. It rolled a short way and stopped. The cut face was smooth as a tabletop.

"Armor doesn't do much about that," Kolb said. "A shield does, if it's a good one. Most aren't." He wiped the clean blade on a rag from his belt. "You build things, Baker."

"Sir, I—a little—"

"I hear you build them very well. I hear it at breakfast whether I want to or not." He laid the cutter back in its case. "So build me a shield. Stops that." The waster. "And stops that." The cutter. "Two weeks."

"Yes, sir." Then: "For how long should it stop them, sir?"

"Twenty minutes."

"Twenty minutes, sir?"

"Of me," said Kolb. "Hitting it. With you inside."

Nobody in the yard said anything.

"Thank you, sir," said Tor.

At the rack, Rolph shut his eyes.

"Sir," he said, "sir, with respect, he's a builder, he isn't—"

"Which is why I'm not asking him to fight me," Kolb said, without turning. "I'm asking him to build. Different thing. Easier. I've seen him fight." He looked Tor up and down: boots, tunic, hands. "Now. Warm up. Run to town and back."

"Town's ten miles!" said Rolph.

"So it's twenty, there and back. Your arithmetic's coming along." Kolb tilted his head. "What do I always tell you, Baker? About a real fight?"

"That I should run away from it, sir."

"Good. Practice."

Tor's shoulders came down. He bent and retied both bootlaces, quickly, and straightened, and went out through the gate at an easy lope.

Kolb watched him all the way to the bend in the road.

"Nice and loose," he said.

Then he turned his head. "Rolph. You're still here. Why are you still here?"

---

A mile short of Hollin, where the road leveled out at the bottom of the long slope, there was a gray marker stone at the verge, smoother than any stone ought to be, with letters cut in it that nobody could read. Tor was standing beside it with his hands on his hips.

He wasn't running. He was looking back up the slope to the ridge, where the school's bell tower stood up against the sky. He looked at it for a long while. Then he turned his head and looked the other way, down a narrower road that went off east into the trees.

A cart came along the town road behind him, pulled by a mule, loaded with cabbages. Its near wheel squealed at every turn. The woman driving it pulled up beside him.

"You want a ride back, love? Nobody'll know. I've had worse than you sat on my cabbages."

Tor jumped and turned round. "Oh! No, ma'am. Thank you, ma'am. I'm meant to be running."

"Looked like you were meant to be standing."

"Yes, ma'am. Sorry." He looked down the east road again. "Ma'am, which way is Two Rivers? From here?"

She pointed down the east road. "That way. Four days walking, if you're quick. Five if you're sensible." She looked at him. "Why? You thinking of going?"

He didn't answer. He went on looking down the road.

"Kolb's lot, are you," she said. "What's he got you running for?"

"To warm up, ma'am."

"Warm up for what?"

He considered it. "I'm not certain, ma'am. I think possibly to be killed."

She laughed. He didn't. She stopped.

She took up a short knife from the board beside her and cut a cabbage free of its stalk in one stroke. The cabbage rolled over against the side of the cart.

Tor was watching the knife.

"It's a line," he said.

"It's a knife, love."

"No, ma'am, sorry, I mean, a blow's a weight. It comes in all over, you spread it out and it's—but an edge is a line, it all comes in at one place, so you can't spread it, you'd have to—" He stopped talking aloud. His lips went on moving. "Unless you didn't stop it. Unless you turned it."

"Turned what?"

He was already running. He went past the cart toward Hollin, not back up the slope and not down the east road, talking to himself, his hands shaping something in the air in front of him.

In Hollin's square, there was a well with a worn stone lip, polished on one side where many hands had slapped it. Tor ran past it without touching it, past the chandler's, out the far side of the square and up the mill road. A woman at the pump called after him that the school was the other way. He didn't look round.

The bell in Hollin's little chapel struck the half hour. The cabbage cart stood unloading by the pump.

When Tor came back down the mill road, he was running faster than he had been going out. He was still talking. As he passed the well he reached out and slapped the stone without breaking stride, and went on across the square and out onto the school road, and up the long slope toward the tower.

The woman with the cart watched him go. She had one cabbage left on the board, set apart from the others. She looked at it, and then up the road, and then put it back in the cart.

---

By the time Tor came back through the gate, the yard was full: thirty or so students in brown, pairing off along the fence, and two in black, sitting on the bench by the water butt. There was space on the bench on either side of them.

Tor was dust to the knees and sweat-dark down his back, and he was talking under his breath. He nearly ran into Kolb.

"Baker. You're late."

"Sorry, sir. I went a bit past the town."

"How far past?"

"There was a mill, sir."

Kolb nodded once. Then he turned his head toward the bench. "Black. Over here."

The two in black stood and crossed the sand together. Tor watched them come with a polite, blank face. When they were two strides away, his face changed.

"Oh," he said. "Miss Sara. Miss—Trice. I didn't—you're in *black*."

"We are, Master Baker," said Trice. "Do keep up."

"Unarmed," said Kolb. "Baker, you've got black, both of them, by turns. Trice first. Go."

Sara stepped back to a line scratched in the sand and folded her arms. Trice opened with a kick straight up between Tor's legs.

Tor made a noise like a startled hen and was a foot to the left when it arrived. Then he turned round and started to run away.

"Baker," said Kolb. "Spar."

Tor stopped. He turned back. "Sorry, sir," he said, and to Trice, with a little bob of the head, "sorry," and put his hands up.

Trice's mouth went thin.

Along the fence, one pair after another stopped practicing and watched. Trice's kicks came low to the knee, high to the head, a front kick that changed halfway and came round from the side. Tor wasn't there for any of them. He moved before she did, small and quick. Twice, when she missed him, he said "sorry."

Trice took an extra breath before her next attack. She spun into a wheel kick, her whole body turning, her heel coming round at head height fast enough to whistle.

Tor stepped forward, inside the spin, caught her round the waist as she came round, turned with her, and took them both down onto the sand with Trice underneath.

Someone along the fence said, "*Oh*," out loud.

On the ground, Trice twisted, and Tor was already there. She twisted the other way, and he was there too. "Sorry," he said. "Sorry." Her arm was nearly pinned.

Then there was a knife at his throat: small, black-handled, its flat lying along the side of his neck under the jaw.

Tor went still. Nobody in the yard moved.

Then the sand moved. It slid away from Tor all at once, in a ring, with a hiss, grit and pebbles skittering outward across the yard, and Trice went with it. She was lifted off him and pushed back along the ground a full arm's length, her heels scoring two lines in the sand. Her knife hand was still stretched out toward him. The point had stopped against nothing, a hand's breadth from his throat. She leaned on it. It went no farther.

Sara's eyes went to Tor's wrists, then his neck, his belt, the hem of his tunic.

"Trice," she said. "Off. It's a shield. The knife won't help."

Trice got to her feet. The knife was no longer in her hand. She looked at Tor, sitting in his clean ring of sand with his hands up in front of his face. Then she took a handful of something out of a pouch at her belt, and threw it.

White powder burst around Tor and stopped in the air. It clung to nothing, a white shell in the shape of a large egg, with Tor inside it. Inside, his hands went to his face. His head turned one way, then the other. Then he went still in a different way. His shoulders had stopped moving.

"Tor," said Sara sharply.

The shell collapsed. The powder came down all at once in a soft white heap, and Tor came out of the bottom of it on his hands and knees, coughing, white from his hair to his elbows, like a loaf rolled in flour.

He coughed a long time. Between coughs, he talked to the sand.

"—air. It has to let the air—*hkk*—air in. Air but not—not *that*. Not dust. Sorry." He looked up at Trice. "Sorry, did I hurt you? I didn't mean to push."

Trice looked at him with her head a little on one side. Then she turned to Kolb.

"Sir," she said, "he has a shield."

"A good one," said Sara. "Sir, that's not a fair bout. Whatever it is, it held a knife off at—"

"And a perfectly good handful of—" said Trice.

Kolb put his head back and laughed. It carried right across the yard. Along the fence, students looked at one another.

"He hasn't got a shield," said Kolb. "That's my homework you're complaining about."

"Your—"

"He's building me one. Two weeks. Looks like it'll work, too." He was still smiling. "Which means I'm going to have to hit it properly."

Tor stopped coughing long enough to look up at him.

"Air, Baker," said Kolb.

"Yes, sir. Sorry, sir. I noticed."

Kolb laughed again and walked off down the fence, and the pairs along it went back to work.

Sara stood looking at Tor. He was sitting back on his heels, trying to beat the powder out of his hair and only moving it about. She looked at his wrists again. Then at the whole of him: the smallest boy in the yard, by a head.

"Right," Kolb called from the far end. "Again. Sara, your turn."

---

The bouts went on all afternoon, black and black by turns.

Trice put her knife to his throat once more, from behind, and held it there. Tor froze, said, "Oh, sorry," and tapped the sand. The sand didn't move. Trice took the knife away and looked at the back of his neck for a while before she stepped back.

Sara threw him twice. Both times she held out a hand and pulled him up, and both times he said, "Thank you."

"You don't thank people for that," said Trice.

"Sorry. It was a very good throw."

Once, Trice left her shoulder open. Tor put a palm into it, hard enough that she took a step back, and then spent the whole of the next exchange saying, "I'm so sorry, are you—I didn't—" until she hit him in the stomach. He folded over, straightened, and said, "Sorry."

The tower bell struck. The yard emptied. Tor stood in the middle of the sand, still white at the hairline, and bowed to each of them in turn, very correctly.

"Thank you, Miss Sara. Thank you, Miss—" a hesitation— "Trice. I'm sorry about your shoulder."

"I left it open, Master Baker."

"Yes," he said, "but I hit it."

He walked off toward the dormitory arch, a little stiffly, talking under his breath. "Air," he said, and "a line," and "turn it, not stop it," and then he was through the arch.

Trice sat down on the bench by the water butt. Sara sat down beside her.

"Who benefits," said Trice, "from making a boy like that run twenty miles?"

"He ran more than twenty," said Sara. "Rolph said there was a mill."

Neither of them said anything else. They sat on the bench and looked at the ring in the sand, which nobody had raked.

---

In their room, the lamp was lit and Tor was at the desk. There was still white in his hair and eyebrows, and a white tidemark on the collar of his tunic. Two sheets of paper lay in front of him. The left one was covered edge to edge in cramped lines, sigils drawn and redrawn, a column of small numbers down the margin. The right one was nearly empty. At the top it said AIR, underlined three times, so hard the pen had gone through.

Rolph came in with a brown bottle.

"Shirt off," he said. "I promised."

"You don't have to—"

"I said I'd rub liniment on your back and you'd apologize the whole time, and I keep my promises. Off."

Tor pulled his tunic over his head without getting up. Across his shoulder blades ran a long red stripe. There was a yellowing patch on his ribs the size of a heel, and a small round bruise on his upper arm. Rolph uncorked the bottle. The room filled with the smell of camphor.

"So," said Rolph. "You lived."

"For now." Tor dipped his pen. "Two weeks."

"He's not actually going to kill you."

"He said twenty minutes, Rolph."

"He says lots of things."

"He cut the pell in half."

Rolph opened his mouth, and closed it, and put liniment on the stripe.

"Sorry," said Tor. "Ow. Sorry."

"There it is."

"Did you see the—at the end, with Trice—"

"I saw the cloud from the far end of the yard. I thought you'd exploded." Rolph worked the liniment in. "Half the second-years are saying you've got a shield sewn in your smalls. The other half are saying you don't, and they're the ones who are worried." His hands slowed. "Did you mean to do that? The push. With the sand."

The pen hovered. "I think so," said Tor. "It seemed like the thing to do. Is that—was it wrong?"

Rolph's hands stopped on Tor's back. They stayed there.

"No," he said. "Not wrong." He picked up the bottle again. "Do you know how many people in this school have been beaten by both of those two in one afternoon? It's a very short list. You should be proud."

"I'm not sure it's the sort of list one's proud of."

"It's a list with you at the top of it. That's a start."

Tor didn't answer. He wrote the last line on the left-hand sheet, blotted it, and set it to one side, squared to the edge of the desk. He pulled the right-hand sheet toward him and, under AIR, wrote *Dust*. He drew a line between the two words, then a small gate in the line, then crossed the gate out.

"Air but not dust," he said, to the paper. "A knife's a line. Turn it, don't stop it."

Rolph put liniment on the bruise on his ribs.

"Ow, sorry," said Tor, and kept writing.

## How this draft differs from the rotating third

- **What's lost:**
  - Rolph's reading of Tor (he takes questions seriously, "too many forks") and his inability to read Kolb.
  - The carter's nineteen years on the road, her sense of Kolb's runners as entertainment, the rule that they slap the well because they can't lie to Kolb, and her urge to give the boy a cabbage.
  - Sara's knowledge of Trice's habits ("because it saved time"; "'unarmed' was a word Trice treated as advice"), her search for the device explained as a merchant's daughter's reflex, and her father's rule about not pricing some things aloud.
  - The ring-as-brass comparison, which carried the noble-field hint. Here it's only Sara looking at his wrists, then at his size.
  - Trice's list and her "account."
- **What's gained:**
  - The road plays as a short silent film: the stone, the two roads, the knife, the unslapped well, the slapped well. The extra half hour is told by the chapel bell, not by anyone's estimate.
  - Rolph's hands stopping on Tor's back reads more starkly with no thought attached.
  - The unraked ring in the sand closes the yard more quietly than Trice's account does.
  - The carter's kept cabbage, put back in the cart, is a small sadness without comment.
