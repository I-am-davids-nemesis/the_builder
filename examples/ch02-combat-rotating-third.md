# Chapter 2, combat class: rotating close third

**Experiment.** Mode: rotating close third, never Tor (option 1). Viewpoints in order: Rolph, a carter on the town road (invented minor character), Sara, Trice, Rolph. Sara and Trice are keeping a secret (the special school), so their sections stay on explicit action and only word choice hints. Kolb never holds the viewpoint, so whether this is revenge or training stays outside the page. Beats follow `outline/chapters.md`, "Combat class, beat by beat." Paired with `ch02-combat-camera.md`.

## Rolph

The yard lay behind the long hall, a square of packed sand the color of old bread, fenced on three sides by weapon racks and on the fourth by a wall that had been part of something else before the school was built against it. In the middle stood the pell. It was an oak trunk as thick as a man, sunk to half its height in the ground, with the bark long gone and the wood at chest height beaten into a pale, furred mess like a chewed pencil.

Kolb was hitting it.

He was using a practice waster, a wooden blade about as slender as a fence post, and he wasn't hurrying. He set his feet, turned from the hips, and struck. The sound was flat and enormous. The racks rattled. A film of dust jumped off the top of the wall and hung there. Rolph, coming through the gate two steps behind Tor, felt it in his back teeth.

Kolb set his feet again.

"Sir," Tor said.

The waster came round. The pell took it. Something small fell off the wall.

"Baker." Kolb lowered the waster and turned, and he was smiling, which Rolph had seen perhaps twice in three years and had not enjoyed either time. "Good. You're early. I like early." His eyes moved. "Rolph. Go away."

"Sir, I said I'd partner him, I thought—"

"And I've said go away. That's two things said. Mine's the one that counts." He considered. "You can stand by the rack. Stand by the rack and be quiet, and you can watch. Most people pay to watch me."

Rolph went to the rack. He did not go any farther than the rack.

"Know what that is, Baker?"

"A pell, sir."

"What does it do?"

Tor looked at it. Rolph could see him taking the question seriously, which was the trouble with Tor; he took everything seriously, including things that had been put to him only so that he could be wrong.

"It stands there, sir," Tor said. "Mostly."

Kolb laughed. It was short and sounded genuinely pleased, and it made Rolph like this even less. "It does. It's very good at it. Watch."

He hit the pell again. This time Rolph saw the trunk move, a shudder that went down into the ground and came back up through the soles of his boots.

"That," Kolb said, in the same pleasant voice, "would kill you. Not hurt you. Kill you. Your ribs would go in and your heart would stop and you'd lie down on my sand and be dead, and I'd have to write to your mother. Unless you had armor on, or a shield field. Have you got a shield on you, Baker?"

"No, sir."

"No. Most people haven't." He leaned the waster against the pell and unhooked something from his belt. Rolph had seen it hanging there for three years and taken it for a tool: a plain hilt a hand and a half long, wrapped in cord, with a line of small marks running round the guard, too regular to be nicks.

Kolb held it out to one side, and there was a blade on it.

It didn't slide out or unfold. It was simply there, the way a thing is there when you turn round and look at it: four feet of dull gray, the length of a boy's arm and a half again. It had no edge that Rolph could see. When Kolb turned his wrist, the blade went edge-on to him and wasn't there at all: a hilt, and a long gray nothing, and then, as the wrist turned back, a blade again.

"Cutter," Kolb said. "You'll have seen pictures."

Tor nodded. He had gone the particular shade of pale that Rolph associated with dinners where there were too many forks.

Kolb walked to the pell with the cutter held loosely in one hand, as if it were a switch for cows, and swung it once, level, the way a man waves off a fly.

There was no sound at all. The blade didn't slow; it went through the oak the way a hand goes through smoke, and came out the other side still traveling, and Kolb let it finish its swing. Nothing else happened, for a moment. Then the top half of the pell, which was taller than Kolb, leaned very slightly to one side, as if it had remembered an appointment, and slid off the bottom half and fell over onto the sand with a thump that Rolph felt in his knees. It rolled a little way and stopped. The cut face was smooth as a tabletop. You could have eaten off it.

"Armor doesn't do much about that," Kolb said. "A shield does, if it's a good one. Most aren't." "You build things, Baker."

"Sir, I—a little—"

"I hear you build them very well. I hear it at breakfast whether I want to or not." The blade was gone; Rolph didn't see it go. Kolb hooked the hilt back on his belt. "So build me a shield. Stops that." He nodded at the waster. "And stops that." He tapped the hilt. "Two weeks."

"Yes, sir," Tor said. Then, because he was Tor: "For how long should it stop them, sir?"

"Twenty minutes."

"Twenty minutes, sir?"

"Of me," Kolb said. "Hitting it. With you inside."

There was a short silence in the yard.

"Thank you, sir," said Tor.

Rolph shut his eyes.

"Sir," he said, from the rack, "sir, with respect, he's a builder, he isn't—"

"Which is why I'm not asking him to fight me," Kolb said, without turning round. "I'm asking him to build. Different thing. Easier. I've seen him fight." He looked Tor over, the dust on his boots, the tunic, the hands, the way a man looks at a horse he's been told is sound. Rolph could not tell what he saw. "Now. Warm up. Run to town and back."

"Town's five miles!" said Rolph.

"So it's ten, there and back. Your arithmetic's coming along." Kolb tilted his head at Tor. "What do I always tell you, Baker? About a real fight?"

"That I should run away from it, sir."

"Good. Practice."

And the strange thing, the thing Rolph would keep turning over all afternoon while he did two hundred cuts at the remains of the pell for having spoken twice, was that Tor looked relieved. His shoulders came down. He bent and tightened his bootlaces with something close to cheer, and then he set off through the gate at an easy lope, as if someone had finally asked him to do a thing he knew how to do.

Kolb watched him go. He watched the whole way to the bend in the road, and his face, the scar and all, did nothing that Rolph could read.

"Nice and loose," Kolb said, to nobody. Then: "Rolph. You're still here. Why are you still here?"

## The carter

She'd been taking cabbages into Hollin on this road for nineteen years, and in nineteen years she had seen a great many of Kolb's lot go by, because the town well was where he sent them to and from. They came past in ones and twos, purple in the face and blowing like bellows, and they slapped the stone of the well before they turned for home, because otherwise they'd be asked whether they had, and they couldn't lie to Kolb. Everybody knew that. Their boots were good and their faces were miserable, and they were the best entertainment the road had.

This one was small. He was the smallest she'd ever seen in the school's brown, and when she first saw him, a mile short of Hollin, he wasn't running at all. He was standing by the old marker stone, the gray one that was smoother than any stone had a right to be, with letters on it nobody could read. He had his hands on his hips and his back to the town, and he was looking back the way he'd come, up the long slope to where the school's bell tower stood on the ridge like a finger.

He looked at it for a long while. Then he turned his head and looked down the other road, the east one, that went off into the trees.

She pulled up the mule.

"You want a ride back, love? Nobody'll know. I've had worse than you sat on my cabbages."

He jumped. He hadn't heard the cart, which took some doing; the near wheel squealed like a pig. "Oh! No, ma'am. Thank you, ma'am. I'm meant to be running."

"Looked like you were meant to be standing."

"Yes, ma'am. Sorry." He looked down the east road again. "Ma'am, which way is Two Rivers? From here?"

She pointed down the east road, into the trees. "That way. Four days walking, if you're quick. Five if you're sensible." She looked at him harder. "Why? You thinking of going?"

He didn't answer. He kept looking down the road. He had a face that showed everything and seemed to have no idea it did, and what it showed just then made her want, absurdly, to give him a cabbage.

"Kolb's lot, are you," she said, to change the subject. "What's he got you running for?"

"To warm up, ma'am."

"Warm up for what?"

He thought about it, honestly, like a child asked a sum. "I'm not certain, ma'am. I think possibly to be killed."

She laughed. He didn't, which made her stop.

She picked up the knife she kept on the board for cutting stalks and cut a cabbage free of its neighbor, because she was going to give him one after all and he could carry it or not. The blade went through the stalk in one stroke, and the cabbage rolled over against the side of the cart, heavy as a head.

He was watching the knife.

"It's a line," he said.

"It's a knife, love."

"No, ma'am, sorry, I mean, a blow's a weight. It comes in all over, you spread it out and it's—but an edge is a line, it all comes in at one place, so you can't spread it, you'd have to—" He stopped. His lips kept moving. "Unless you didn't stop it. Unless you turned it."

"Turned what?"

But he'd gone. Not back up the slope to the school, and not down the east road either. He went past her cart toward Hollin at a run, talking to himself, his hands making shapes in the air in front of him like a man describing a fish.

She followed him in at the mule's pace. By the time she reached the square he was across it, past the well without slapping the stone, past the chandler's, out the far side and away up the mill road, still talking. A woman at the pump called after him that the school was the other way. He didn't hear her.

She sold her cabbages. It took most of half an hour. She'd loaded the empties and was buying a twist of salt when he came back down the mill road, and she wouldn't have believed it was the same boy if not for his size. He was running hard and easy at once, the way a dog runs when it's forgotten it's tired, and he was still talking. As he passed the well he reached out and slapped the stone, the way they all did, as though he'd only just remembered he was supposed to have been there.

Then he went off up the school road at a pace none of Kolb's lot had ever gone up it, and she lost sight of him at the marker stone.

She'd kept the cabbage for him. She ate it herself, in the end, in a soup, and thought about him more than she meant to.

## Sara

The yard was full by the time he came back: thirty or so in brown, pairing off along the fence, and the two of them in black, on the bench by the water butt where nobody else sat because nobody else was ever quite invited to.

Sara saw him first. He came through the gate at a run, and he was coming in faster than he'd gone out, which she knew because Rolph had told the whole yard how he'd gone out, at length. He was dust to the knees and sweat-dark down the back of his tunic, and he was talking under his breath. He nearly ran into Kolb.

"Baker. You're late."

"Sorry, sir. I went a bit past the town."

"How far past?"

He thought. "There was a mill, sir."

Kolb's expression didn't change, but he nodded once, as if somebody had told him the weather would hold. Then he turned his head. "Black. Over here."

Sara stood up. Trice stood up beside her, unhurried, and they crossed the sand together. Tor watched them come with the polite, blank face of someone being introduced to strangers, and then, when they were two strides away, his face changed completely.

"Oh," he said. "Miss Sara. Miss—Trice. I didn't—you're in *black*."

"We are, Master Baker," said Trice. "Do keep up."

"Unarmed," Kolb said. "Baker, you've got black, both of them, by turns. Trice first. Go."

Sara stepped back to the line scratched in the sand and folded her arms, and watched Trice open the way she always opened against boys, which was with a kick straight up between the legs, because it saved time.

Tor wasn't there when it arrived. He made a noise like a startled hen and was simply somewhere else, a foot to the left, and then he did what Sara would not have guessed in a hundred tries: he turned round and started to run away.

"Baker," said Kolb. "Spar."

Tor stopped. He turned back. "Sorry, sir," he said, and then to Trice, with a little bob of the head, "sorry," and put his hands up.

Trice's mouth went thin.

What followed, Sara thought, was not what she'd been told about him, and it was not what anybody in the yard had expected either, because along the fence the pairs had stopped pretending to practice. Trice was very good. Sara had known that for years, and had the bruises to prove it. Her kicks came from everywhere: low to the knee, high to the head, a front kick that changed its mind halfway and came round from the side. And Tor wasn't there for any of them. He wasn't fast so much as early. He moved before she did, small and quick and apologetic, and twice he said "sorry" when she missed him, as if her missing were his fault.

Trice did not like that. Sara saw her not liking it, the set of the jaw, the little extra breath before each attack, and she knew what was coming a beat before it came. Trice always got showy when she was cross.

The wheel kick was beautiful. Trice spun into it, her whole body turning like a door on its hinge, her heel coming round at head height fast enough to whistle.

Tor stepped inside it. He didn't go back from it, the way anybody sensible would. He went forward, into the middle of the spin where the heel couldn't reach, and caught her round the waist as she came round, and turned with her, and they both went down on the sand with Trice underneath.

Somebody along the fence said, "*Oh*," out loud.

On the ground it went on going wrong for Trice. He was smaller than she was and he didn't seem to care. He was all elbows and patience, and every time she twisted he was already where she was twisting to, saying sorry, and she was going to be pinned in a moment. Sara could see it, and so could Trice.

Then there was a knife at his throat.

Sara didn't see where it came from. She never did, with Trice. It was simply there, small and black-handled, the flat of it lying along the side of his neck under the jaw, and Tor went absolutely still.

"Unarmed" was a word Trice had always treated as advice.

For one breath nothing moved in the whole yard.

Then the sand moved.

It slid away from him all at once, in a ring, the way water jumps away from a drop of oil. It hissed. Grit and small stones skittered outward across the yard, and Trice went with them. She didn't let go so much as get put somewhere else: lifted off him, knife hand and all, and pushed back along the ground a full arm's length, her heels scoring two lines in the sand. The knife was still in her hand, held out toward him, and the point of it had stopped against nothing a hand's breadth from his throat. She leaned on it. It didn't go any farther.

Sara looked for the device. Her eyes did it before she'd decided to: wrist, neck, belt, the hem of the tunic, the places people kept shield fields when they could afford them. Nothing on his wrists. Nothing at his throat but the ties of his tunic. He might have something sewn in somewhere. A personal shield that would hold off a blade at that distance, full round, and push a grown girl along the ground while it did it—her father had a rule about things like that. You didn't put a price on them out loud, because whoever was listening would know you'd never been near one.

"Trice," she said. "Off. It's a shield. The knife won't help."

Trice got up. She put the knife away, somewhere, the way it had come. She looked at Tor sitting in his clean ring of sand with his hands up in front of his face, and her expression was one Sara knew. It meant *all right, then*.

She took her hand out of her pouch and threw.

The powder went out in a white burst and stopped in the air around him. It didn't fall. It clung, all over, to nothing, a fine white shell the shape of a large egg with Tor somewhere in the middle of it. For a moment it was almost pretty. Then Sara saw the shape inside it put its hands to its face, and turn its head one way and the other, blind. And then go still in a different way. Its shoulders weren't moving.

He wasn't breathing. He couldn't. Whatever kept the knife out was keeping the air out too.

"Tor," Sara said sharply.

The egg collapsed. All the powder came down at once in a soft white slump, and Tor came out of the bottom of it on his hands and knees, coughing so hard he couldn't stop, white from his hair to his elbows, like a loaf that had been rolled in flour and left on the board.

He coughed for a long time. In between coughs he was talking. Sara went close enough to hear it.

"—air," he was saying, to the sand. "It has to let the air—*hkk*—air in. Air but not—not *that*. Not dust. Sorry." This last was to Trice. "Sorry, did I hurt you? I didn't mean to push."

Trice was looking at him with her head a little on one side. She didn't answer him. She turned to Kolb.

"Sir," she said, "he has a shield."

"A good one," Sara said. "Sir, that's not a fair bout. Whatever it is, it held a knife off at—"

"And a perfectly good handful of—" said Trice.

Kolb laughed.

It was a real laugh, a big one, with his head back, and it went right across the yard and made the pairs along the fence look at each other. Sara had never heard it before. She didn't think anyone had.

"He hasn't got a shield," Kolb said. "That's my homework you're complaining about."

"Your—"

"He's building me one. Two weeks. Looks like it'll work, too." He was still smiling, which was frightening. "Which means I'm going to have to hit it properly."

On the sand, Tor stopped coughing long enough to look up at him with an expression of the most profound courtesy and dismay.

"Air, Baker," said Kolb.

"Yes, sir. Sorry, sir. I noticed."

Kolb laughed again and walked away down the fence, and the pairs along it hurriedly remembered they were practicing.

Sara looked at Tor. He was sitting back on his heels now, trying to beat the powder out of his hair and only moving it about. Not a device. Nothing sewn in. He was the smallest boy in the yard, by a long way, and he was saying sorry to Trice again.

She looked at him the way she'd once seen her father look at a ring a man had sold him as brass.

"Right," said Kolb, from the far end of the yard. "Again. Sara, your turn."

## Trice

By the fourth bout, Trice had a list.

He went left when he was frightened. He watched hands, not faces, which was sensible and meant faces were worth using. He couldn't be feinted, not really, because he didn't commit to anything he hadn't seen, and you couldn't sell a boy a lie when he wasn't buying. He was early, always, a half beat before you'd decided. And he did not, at any point in the whole long afternoon, do the thing with the sand again.

She watched for it every time. She put Sara's knife to his throat herself once, borrowed, from behind, as an experiment. He froze, and said, "Oh, sorry," as if he'd stepped on her foot, and tapped the sand to concede. No ring. No push. Either he couldn't do it twice, or he'd decided not to, and she found she didn't care for either answer.

Sara put him down twice. Sara was plainer than Trice and harder to read, and he kept trying to read her anyway, which cost him. Both times, she helped him up. Both times, he thanked her for throwing him.

"You don't thank people for that," Trice told him.

"Sorry," he said. "It was a very good throw."

He landed exactly one real blow all afternoon, a palm against her shoulder that she'd left open on purpose to see what he'd do with it, and he spent the next exchange apologizing so sincerely that she hit him in the stomach out of sheer exasperation, and then he apologized for that.

The bell went on the tower. The yard emptied the way yards do, everybody suddenly tired. Tor stood in the middle of it, still faintly white at the hairline, and bowed to each of them in turn, very correctly, as if they'd all three been at a dance.

"Thank you, Miss Sara. Thank you, Miss—" a hesitation, while he decided what he was allowed to call her— "Trice. I'm sorry about your shoulder."

"I left it open, Master Baker."

"Yes," he said, "but I hit it."

He went off toward the dormitory, walking a little stiffly and talking to himself again. Trice heard "air" and "line" and "turn it, not stop it," and then he was through the arch and gone.

She sat down on the bench by the water butt. Sara sat beside her.

The account, as far as Trice could total it, came out roughly even, which was itself a loss. She had come into the yard expecting a scholarship boy who'd be grateful for a lesson. She was leaving with a list, a sore shoulder, a question she couldn't put a price on, and a strong sense of owing something to somebody, which she disliked very much.

"Who benefits," she said, half to herself, "from making a boy like that run ten miles?"

"He ran more than ten," said Sara. "Rolph said there was a mill."

They sat with that for a while. Neither of them said anything about the sand.

## Rolph

He came back with the liniment and found Tor at the desk, which was the least surprising thing that had happened all day.

There was still white in Tor's hair. There was white in his eyebrows and in the creases of his neck, and a fine white tidemark on the collar of his tunic where it had been wet and dried. He had two sheets of paper in front of him. The left-hand one was the food-drying field, nearly finished: the familiar cramped lines, the sigil drawn and redrawn, a column of small numbers down the side that Rolph understood were about apples. The right-hand one was new, and nearly empty. At the top of it Tor had written AIR and underlined it three times, so hard the pen had gone through.

"Shirt off," Rolph said. "I promised."

"You don't have to—"

"I said I'd rub liniment on your back and you'd apologize the whole time, and I keep my promises. Off."

Tor took off his tunic without getting up. His back was a map of the afternoon: a long red stripe across the shoulder blades, a yellowing patch on his ribs the size of a heel, a neat round bruise on the upper arm that Rolph thought was probably Sara's thumb. Rolph uncorked the bottle. The smell of camphor and something green filled the room.

"So," he said. "You lived."

"For now." Tor dipped his pen. "Two weeks."

"He's not actually going to kill you."

"He said twenty minutes, Rolph."

"He says lots of things."

"He cut the pell in half."

Rolph opened his mouth, and found that he had nothing to put in it, and shut it again. He put liniment on the stripe across Tor's shoulders instead.

"Sorry," Tor said. "Ow. Sorry."

"There it is."

"Did you see the—at the end, with Trice—"

"I saw the cloud from the far end of the yard. I thought you'd exploded." Rolph worked the liniment in. "Half the second-years are saying you've got a shield sewn in your smalls. The other half are saying you don't, and they're the ones who are worried." He paused. "Did you mean to do that? The push. With the sand."

Tor thought about it, the pen hovering. "I think so," he said. "It seemed like the thing to do. Is that—was it wrong?"

Rolph's hands were still on Tor's back. They stayed there a moment longer than they needed to.

"No," Rolph said. "Not wrong." He picked the bottle up again. "Do you know how many people in this school have been beaten by both of those two in one afternoon? It's a very short list. You should be proud."

"I'm not sure it's the sort of list one's proud of."

"It's a list with you at the top of it. That's a start."

Tor didn't answer. He'd finished the last line on the left-hand sheet. He blotted it, looked at it for a moment, and set it carefully to one side, squared to the edge of the desk, done. Then he drew the right-hand sheet toward him, under AIR, and wrote a single word: *Dust*. He drew a line between the two, and then a little gate in the line, and then crossed the gate out.

"Air but not dust," he said, to the paper. "A knife's a line. Turn it, don't stop it."

Rolph put liniment on the heel-print on Tor's ribs. Tor said, "Ow, sorry," without looking up, and kept writing.

## Details this draft invents

To add to the codex if kept.

- **The practice yard:** a square of packed sand behind the long hall, weapon racks on three sides, an old wall on the fourth that belonged to something older than the school. A bench by the water butt where the students in black sit.
- **The pell:** an oak trunk as thick as a man, sunk to half its height; beaten pale and furred at chest height.
- **Kolb's practice weapon:** a wooden waster about as thick as a fence post.
- **The cutter:** a plain cord-wrapped hilt a hand and a half long, which Kolb wears on his belt every day (Rolph took it for a tool for three years); a line of small regular marks round the guard (sigils; unexplained on the page). The four-foot blade of dull gray is simply there when he wants it and gone when he doesn't; no sheath. Turned edge-on, the blade vanishes. Cuts in silence, without slowing. That a cutter is magic (an infinitely thin blade that passes through any physical thing without resistance) is canon (user); the page shows it rather than explains it.
- **Kolb's run:** students run to the town well and slap the stone before turning back; they "can't lie to Kolb." Kolb smiles rarely and has never been heard to laugh aloud (Sara).
- **Kolb punishes Rolph** with two hundred cuts at the pell's remains, for speaking twice.
- **The town is Hollin**, five miles from the school (ten there and back) down a long slope; it has a square, a well, a pump, a chandler's, and a mill road out the far side. From the road, the school's bell tower stands on the ridge "like a finger."
- **The old marker stone** a mile short of Hollin: gray, smoother than stone should be, with letters nobody can read (post-collapse texture).
- **The east road** into the trees goes toward Two Rivers: four days on foot.
- **The carter:** a woman who has carted cabbages into Hollin for nineteen years; her near wheel squeals. Unnamed.
- **Tor's shield idea, first form:** a blow is a weight that can be spread; an edge is a line that can't, so the field should **turn** a cut rather than stop it. Then: **air but not dust**.
- **Trice opens against boys with a groin kick** "because it saved time"; gets showy when cross; carries a small black-handled knife and a pouch of white powder. Treats "unarmed" as advice.
- **Tor's fighting:** early rather than fast; watches hands, not faces; can't be feinted; goes left when frightened; apologizes when he dodges, when he lands a blow, and when he's thrown.

She looks at Tor afterward "the way she'd once seen her father look at a ring a man had sold him as brass" (the noble-field hint, unspoken).
- **Tor calls them** "Miss Sara" and "Miss—Trice," hesitating over what he's allowed to call her.
- **Rolph's hesitation** when Tor asks whether the push was wrong: his hands stay on Tor's back a moment too long (a royal's knowledge of noble fields, hinted by action only).
- **The liniment** smells of camphor and something green; Rolph's chapter-one promise kept.
- **The new sheet:** AIR underlined three times; *Dust*; a line with a gate in it, crossed out.

## Notes on the mode

- **Kolb stays outside.** Rolph can't read him at the gate ("nice and loose," to nobody); Sara has never heard him laugh. Whether this is revenge or training is left in the reader's hands.
- **The despair is visible, not narrated.** The carter sees the long look back at the tower, the look down the east road, and the question about Two Rivers. Tor never says he's thinking of leaving. The cabbage knife turns him around.
- **Sara and Trice keep their secret by not thinking about it.** Neither section names the special school or explains the black; Trice's "account" and "list" and Sara's search for the device are the only professional tells.
