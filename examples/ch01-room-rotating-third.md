## Rolph

They came up the dormitory stair with half the washing house behind them. Rolph could hear them on the turns below, the scuff and patter of a crowd trying to look as if it just happened to be going the same way.

"Don't look back," he said. "It only encourages them."

Tor didn't look back. He was holding the board against his chest with both hands, the way he might have carried a hot loaf out of an oven, and taking the stairs one at a time, like an old man. Thirty hours on a stool in an isolation chamber did that to a body. Rolph had seen it before. He'd see it again; it was only a question of when.

Their door was the fourth along the top corridor, plain oak with a plain iron latch. Nothing about it said anything. Rolph liked that about it. As they came within a stride, something on the inside gave a soft wooden knock, the sound of a bar lifting out of its brackets, and the door eased an inch off the jamb by itself.

Behind them on the landing, somebody said, "Did you see—"

Rolph put Tor through the gap, followed him, and shut the door with his heel. On the inside the bar was already coming down again: a length of plain ash, dark with handling, lowering itself into its brackets as if somebody's hand were on it. It dropped home with a *clunk*. A moment later someone knocked, politely, and then less politely, and then voices conferred on the far side, and then feet went away down the corridor, not all of them.

"Well," Rolph said. "That's the door."

The room was a narrow one, two beds and two desks and one window, with the light coming in long and gray over the dormitory roofs. Tor's half was all paper. Paper on the desk, paper under the desk, scraps of pine and offcuts of board on the sill with half a pattern painted on each, a jar of brushes, a saucer of dried blue. Rolph's half had a bed, made, a shelf of books, and at the foot of the bed the chest.

The chest was the size of a bread bin, dark wood and darker iron, with a lock plate on the front that had no keyhole. It had come with him from home in his first year, which was the arrangement. Rolph went to it now and laid his palm on the lid, and the lock plate gave the same soft knock as the door had, and the lid came loose under his hand.

He took out a few coins for later. The rest of it gleamed up at him, yellow and heaped, the way it always did. He shut it again.

Tor had sat down on his own bed with the board on his knees. He was looking across at the door bar.

"It held," he said. "I was worried it wouldn't hold, with that many people. It's only meant to know two."

"It held," Rolph agreed.

Tor had made the door out of the chest, the winter before last. He'd asked first. He'd asked very carefully, the way he asked for everything, as if he expected the answer to be no and wanted it on record that he'd understood. Then he'd sat on the floor in front of the chest for an evening with his chin on his fists, and the next morning there had been a pattern painted on the inside of the door in that same cheap blue, and the bar lifted itself when they came home.

Rolph had sat on that same floor for most of an afternoon afterward, watching the bar. Up, down. Up, down. He had walked out into the corridor and come back in nine or ten times. He'd looked at the blue lines until they swam. He had understood exactly as much at the end of the afternoon as at the start, which was that it worked.

He looked at the board on Tor's knees now. The same blue lines, a different shape. They meant nothing to him at all.

"Thirty gold," he said. "Sara said thirty?"

"She was being kind."

"Sara's never been kind about a price in her life. It's not in her." He dropped onto his own bed opposite, boots and all, and leaned back against the wall. "Mother would want one."

Tor looked up.

"Mother would want one *first*," Rolph said. "That's the trouble. Before anyone else has one. You don't know what it's like, Little Tor. It's not about the laundry. Among the—" he waved a hand, a gesture that took in Tovey and the girls and the whole loud washing house, "—among the nobility, if you've got a new thing before anyone else has it, that's worth more than the thing. That's what they were all doing down there. Tovey wants it, so they want it, so their mothers will want it, and the one who gets it first gets to have people round to look at it. Trust me."

"For laundry," Tor said.

"For anything. A lamp. A lock." He nodded at the door. "If anyone knew about that, you'd have a queue down the stairs."

"There's a queue down the stairs now."

"There is a queue down the stairs now," Rolph allowed. "Well. You'll have to make one for her, then."

"Of course." Tor said it at once, before Rolph had finished. Then, under his breath, looking at nothing: "Twenty-four."

"Nobody asked you for twenty-four."

"Sara said that."

"Sara's right about most things." Rolph put his hands behind his head. "If it did fruit, now. That'd be something. Apples. Plums. Beans. You'd not believe how much rots at home in a wet year. It's all anyone talks about at dinner some autumns, the accounts come in and it's so many bushels lost in the low barns, and so many in the carts on the road, and everyone looks very grave and passes the salt—"

He stopped, because Tor had stopped listening.

It was always easy to tell. Tor's face didn't change, exactly. It just stopped being pointed at you. He was looking at the board on his knees, and then through it, and his lips were moving.

"Cloth wants to let go," Tor said, not to Rolph. "Cloth's all little channels, it's easy, the water's only sitting in it. An apple's got a skin. The water's inside. You'd have to go slower. If you go fast the outside would seal, like a crust on a loaf before the middle's done, and then it would rot from the inside, worse than before—"

He got up. He didn't seem to notice he'd done it. He went to his desk, sat, pushed a stack of paper onto the floor to clear a space, found a pencil, and began to draw.

Rolph didn't move. He knew this, too.

"—and it's alive, isn't it? An apple. A bit. After it's picked. Does that matter? It might matter. Beans are drier to start with. Grain's nearly dry already, that's half the job done, but then you'd want to stop it partway, you don't want dust, you want—" Pencil scratching. A sheet pulled across. "—you want it to *stop*. Cloth doesn't care when you stop."

Outside, the tower bell struck the hour. Tor didn't look up. Rolph counted the strokes, out of habit, and then just watched him: the small hunched back in the shirt with the elbows gone thin, the pencil going, a hand coming up now and then to rub at his eyes and leaving a gray smudge across one of them. He was smiling a little, Rolph realized. He didn't think Tor knew.

It was funny, in its way. It was the funniest thing about Tor, how he could be lifted clean out of a room by a sentence. You could mention apples and lose him for a bell.

Rolph looked at the pattern growing under the pencil and, for a moment, wanted very badly to be lost like that. Then the moment passed, and he went on watching.

Some time later, the pencil stopped.

Tor sat very still. Then he turned round on the stool, and his face was pointed at Rolph again, and it was stricken.

"Sorry," he said. "Oh, I'm sorry. You were talking. You were telling me about your mother, and I just—"

"I was telling you about beans."

"I'm sorry."

"I'll live." Rolph swung his boots off the bed. "You haven't eaten. You're the color of that wall. Stay there and don't build anything while I'm gone." He went to the door. The bar lifted for him. "I mean it. Nothing."

The corridor outside was empty except for two first-years sitting on the top step, who stood up very quickly when they saw him and pretended to be going down.

---

He came back with a tray: a heap of eggs, half a loaf, a wedge of cheese, a pot of the cafeteria's dreadful tea, and two apples, because he was not above a joke. Tor ate everything except one apple, which he turned in his fingers for some time and then set on the desk beside his drawings, as if it were a reference.

The afternoon went slowly after that, which was what Rolph had wanted. Tor ate, and dozed sitting up against the wall, and woke, and ate the end of the loaf, and moved stiffly to the desk to write something down, and moved stiffly back again. He winced when he stretched. Every so often he said, "Sorry," about nothing in particular, and Rolph said, "Stop that," and Tor said, "Sorry."

Rolph lay on his own bed with a book open on his chest. It was *Principles of Field Theory, Third Year*, and he was, in a general way, studying it. He had been on the same page for some time. Mostly he watched Tor, over the top of it, and kept the room quiet.

Nobody knocked again. Once someone tried the door, gently, and the bar didn't lift, and they went away.

By the sixth bell Tor was asleep on top of his blankets with his boots still on and one hand trailing toward the floor. Rolph got up and took the boots off him. He looked at the drawings on the desk on his way back: arrows, half-patterns, a little picture of what might have been a plum, a word underlined three times. He couldn't read the word. He put out the lamp.

---

He woke in the dark to the sound of a pencil.

There was a candle lit on Tor's desk, the stub of one, and Tor was bent over it in his shirt, writing fast. Not drawing. Writing, line after line, the pencil going so hard Rolph could hear it bite. Tor was muttering under his breath, too low to make out, and once he laughed, a short surprised sound, and wrote faster.

"Tor," Rolph said.

Nothing.

Rolph lay and listened to the pencil for a while. Then he turned over and went back to sleep.

---

When he woke again it was barely gray at the window, and Tor's bed was empty and made, and so was Tor's corner of the desk, which was a thing Rolph had never seen. The board was gone. The door bar was down. Tor had gone out and the lock had shut itself behind him.

On the desk, squared neatly at the edge, were the night's pages.

Rolph picked up the top one. It was covered edge to edge in Tor's small cramped hand, the lines so close they nearly touched, words running into one another without spaces, broken every few inches by a scrap of pattern or an arrow looping back to something earlier. In the margin, in capitals, underlined twice: CRUST. Below that: *slower, ask about skins, NOT dust*. The rest of it might have been in another language. It might have been in several.

He turned it the other way up, in case that helped. It didn't.

Rolph put the page back exactly where it had been, squared it with the others, and went to see about breakfast.
