# Chapter 2, Rolph's order and the night build: external, camera-like narrator

**Experiment.** Mode: external, camera-like narrator (option 3). Nobody's thoughts; only what can be seen and heard. Beats follow `outline/chapters.md`, "Rolph's order" and "The night build." The dialogue stays close to `ch02-night-build-rotating-third.md` so the modes can be compared.

---

The bar lifted before Rolph touched the door. He came in loudly and shut it with his heel. Tor was at the desk under a low lamp, in a drift of paper, his hands lying on top of it.

"Little Tor! You're famous."

"I'm not."

"You're famous in the Barrow, which is the only place that counts. Do you know what I heard tonight? I heard a second-year from House Calder tell a girl from the town that your laundry board can dry a cloak in the rain while you're wearing it. I heard a fifth-year say his father would pay forty for one. Forty! I heard Tovey's lot at the end table arguing about whether you'd sell to them first, on account of Tovey asking first." Rolph dropped onto his bed. It creaked. "Everybody wants one. Everybody."

Tor said nothing.

"What's the matter with your hands?"

"Kolb. The pell. With an iron bar. Until I couldn't." Tor flexed his fingers slowly. "It's coming back."

"Good. Because here's the thing." Rolph sat up. "Mother. Mother has to have them first. Not one: ten. Ten boards, so she can give them out to her friends, and her friends have friends, and inside a month every great house in the—everyone who matters will have seen one in Mother's hands first, and the rest of them will be breaking down Sara's door. You understand? It's the best thing that could happen to you. It's practically a favor."

"I haven't got anything to make them on," said Tor.

"You've got pine."

Tor put his chin down. The corners of his mouth turned in. "Sara says pine warps. Sara says pine's firewood in five years. Sara says it has to be metal. I haven't got any metal, Rolph. I haven't got any acid. I've got a cup of cold tea that I can't lift."

Rolph looked at him for a moment, his head tilted.

"Then buy metal."

"With what?"

Rolph went to the chest at the foot of his bed and laid his hand flat on the lid. It clicked. He took out a purse and counted gold into his palm. Tor's eyes went wider with every coin.

"Fifty-five." Rolph stacked them on the desk beside Tor's hands. "Half up front. That's how it's done."

"Half of *what*?"

"Of a hundred and ten. Eleven a board. I heard Debri's paying you twelve, and I'm your friend, so I get the friend price."

Tor looked at the stack. "That's one gold off for friendship."

"Exactly."

"That seems backward."

"It's how friendship works among merchants," said Rolph. "You wouldn't understand."

The corner of Tor's mouth twitched. He pulled it straight again.

"I'd need them soon," said Rolph. "That's the only thing. It's Mother's birthday on fifth day next week, and the express to the capital takes three days, and it goes when it goes, and I want them on it."

Tor looked up. "Her birthday's fifth day? Like the queen's?"

"Just like the queen's," said Rolph, and laughed.

"Do we get that day off? For the queen's?"

"Not a chance. You'd have to ask Kolb." Rolph laughed again, a little longer, and lay back on the bed with his hands behind his head. "Anyway. No hurry. Any time before the express. Tomorrow. The day after."

The chair scraped. Tor was on his feet with the stack of gold held against his chest in both hands, and he was looking at the door.

"The store closes at the ninth bell," said Rolph.

"I know," said Tor. "I'll knock."

---

The student store was dark. Three soft knocks sounded on its door, then a pause, then three more.

After a while a candle moved inside, and the door opened on an old man in a nightcap and slippers.

Tor stood on the step. "I'm terribly sorry," he said. "I know you're closed. I'm so sorry. Only, I need some copper squares. And some acid."

"It's past the ninth bell."

"Yes. I'm sorry."

"Copper's dear. Five-inch squares, three copper a square, and it's not cheap acid either, the good stuff, for etching. Come back in the morning with your—"

"How many have you got?"

"Squares? A crate. A hundred, near enough."

"I'll take them all," said Tor. "And enough acid for a hundred. And a basin, if you've got one that won't mind. Sorry." He held out his hands and opened them. They were full of gold.

The storekeeper looked at the gold for some time. He looked up the court, toward the gate, where a lamp burned by the porters' lodge. Then he looked back at Tor's face.

"Come in," he said. "Watch the step."

Inside, he set out a crate of copper squares on the counter, two stone jars stoppered with wax, a lead-lined basin, a pair of tongs, and a bundle of rags.

"Will the acid hurt my hands?" Tor asked.

The storekeeper reached under the counter and brought out a pair of oiled gloves, a little dusty. He blew on them and laid them on the pile.

He counted change out of the till in silver and copper, twice. Tor counted it after him, twice.

Tor got the crate onto his shoulder and the jars across his back in a sling, and went off across the dark court, slowly, listing to one side.

The storekeeper stood in his doorway with the candle until Tor reached the dormitory. Then he shut the door, went to the till, and opened it, and looked inside for a while before he closed it again.

---

The window of the room on the top floor stood open to the cold. On the sill sat a basin, and beside it the lamp, and Tor, in gloves much too big for him, lowered a copper square into the basin with tongs. The bare metal fizzed. Where he'd drawn the pattern on in something waxy, the lines stayed bright.

Rolph sat up in bed with his eyes watering. "What *is* that?"

"Acid. Sorry. I've got the window open."

"I can see that. I can *taste* that."

"It's only for the lines." Tor lifted the square out, tipped it to the lamp, rinsed it in the jug, and set it on a rag on the floor. The floor between the beds was covered in squares, laid out in rows of ten, each one with the drying field bitten into it in fine dark lines.

"How many have you done?"

"Seventy-two. Seventy-three."

Rolph got out of bed and stepped carefully between the rows to the window. He looked down at the squares. Most of the lines were crisp. On one, a line had gone wide and furry; on another, a corner of the pattern was missing. These lay in a small separate pile: five or six.

"What about these?"

"Those are crooked," said Tor, without looking around. "They'll work. They're just crooked. I'll give those away."

"Give them—Tor, those are *eleven gold*—"

"They're crooked." Tor lowered another square into the basin. "Davie can have one. He can't keep his shirts dry, he's got no idea, it's awful. And the washing house should have one, for the people who do the real washing, the sheets. And Hob, in the kitchens, his mother takes in laundry in Hollin. And the other scholarship boys in the east wing. They can share. Seventy-four."

Rolph opened his mouth. He shut it. He sat down on the end of his bed in the cold and watched.

Later, the lamp had moved to the floor by the door, and Rolph was lying down with his eyes closed. Tor crouched by the door, putting squares into a sack, ten at a time, counting under his breath. Fifty went into the sack. Fifty stayed on the desk in a squared-off stack.

Rolph opened his eyes. "Where are you going?"

"Workroom." Tor stood. The sack clanked. "You need to sleep. And the fumes. I'll do half there and half here tomorrow. Yours'll be in the first ten. I'll do yours first."

"Tor, it's the middle of the night."

"Yes. Sorry." At the door, with the sack on his shoulder, he put his chin down again. "Fifty-five gold. Honestly, Rolph."

The bar dropped behind him.

Rolph lay looking at the ceiling. The window was still open. After a long time he got up and went to the desk and looked at the small pile of crooked squares. He picked up the top one, turned it to the faint light from the window, and put it back exactly where it had been.

---

The workroom on the east stair had a stove that ticked. One lamp burned on the long bench. Dorgal sat beside it with a painted pine board in front of him and a sheaf of notes, a wet rag laid across the board. He lifted the rag. It was still wet. He put it down.

The door opened. In one movement, Dorgal pulled a cloth over the board.

"Oh!" Tor stood in the doorway with a clanking sack on his shoulder. "Sorry. I didn't think anyone would—I'm so sorry. Do you mind if I—"

"My dear Baker," said Dorgal. "It's a workroom. Work."

Tor set the sack down by the stove, gently, and took the stool at the far end of the bench. He laid out ten copper squares in a row. The pattern on each was the same: a hook at the lower left, a doubled crossing near the center, something like a fishbone along the top edge.

Dorgal looked at the squares. Then he looked at the cloth over his own board.

"I heard you had a visitor this morning," he said.

"Yes! Mr. Sorvey." Tor looked up, smiling a little. "Do you know him?"

Dorgal didn't answer at once.

"Everyone knows him," he said.

"He frightened me a bit, honestly. Sorry. I expect he's very nice."

"He isn't," said Dorgal. "Particularly."

Tor looked at him for a moment, then went back to his squares.

He held his hand flat above the first one, not touching it, and half closed his eyes. His lips moved. He laid a finger on the line at the lower left and traced it, slowly, around the hook and over the crossing and along the fishbone. Halfway along the fishbone, his finger slid off the line onto bare copper, and stayed there, and his lips kept moving.

After a while, he opened his eyes, set the square aside, and reached for the next.

Dorgal watched until Tor's eyes were closed again. Then he wet a corner of his rag at the jug and laid it over the finished square. Water rose out of the cloth in tiny beads and ran off onto the bench.

Dorgal folded the rag and put it on his knee.

"Baker," he said. "Your finger came off the line. On the first one."

"Did it?" Tor opened his eyes. "Oh. Probably. I'm tired. It doesn't matter."

"It doesn't *matter*?"

"Not really. The sigil's only there to keep my place. And so people know what it's for, after. You could do it on a blank square if you wanted to, it's only harder, you lose track of where you are." Tor thought. "It's like a recipe. You don't need it to make the bread. It just helps if you're doing forty loaves."

The stove ticked.

"Interesting," said Dorgal.

He looked at the cloth over his board. He looked at his notes, eleven pages, each with the same sigil drawn on it in careful ink. Tor had gone back to work.

The tower bell struck. Dorgal took out a lamp-square and a pen and began to copy it. The tower bell struck again.

By the third batch, Tor was yawning. He yawned in the middle of a square, stopped, looked at the square, said, "Sorry. Again," and began at the hook. He spoke to the squares, quietly. "You're the same as the last one, aren't you. Yes. You are. Just like him." Once he stopped, laid the batch out, counted nine, searched under the bench, and found the tenth in his lap.

The bell struck again. Dorgal's lamp-square lay unfinished in front of him.

At last Tor stood and stretched, and his back cracked. He put the squares into the sack by tens, slowly, stopping to flex his hands. One square stayed on the bench, a little apart, with a furry line along the fishbone.

"Would you like that one?" said Tor. "It's crooked. It works, it's just crooked. I'm giving the crooked ones away."

Dorgal looked at the square.

"I couldn't possibly," he said.

"It's only crooked."

"Then give it to someone who needs it."

Tor nodded, and put the square in the sack.

"Goodnight," he said. "Thank you for the company. It's much nicer, doing copies with someone there."

He heaved the sack onto his shoulder and went out. On the stair, his steps went down unevenly: one step, a pause, the next.

The outer door banged. Below the window, uneven footsteps crossed the stones of the court, and faded.

Dorgal lifted the cloth from his board. He laid one finger on the painted line at the lower left and began to trace it. Halfway around the hook, he lifted the finger and let it wander off the line.

## How this draft differs from the rotating third

- **What's lost:**
  - Rolph's ale-and-triumph mood on the hill, and his recognition that Tor is performing a sulk. Here the sulk is shown (chin down, mouth turned in) and Rolph's tilted head is the only sign he sees through it.
  - Rolph's unspoken thoughts about people who've never counted coppers on a counter, and about his mother opening the boards. In exchange he gets a silent beat with a crooked square.
  - The storekeeper's thirty-one years, his sleepless night, and his brief suspicion that the boy stole the gold. Here it's a look toward the porters' lodge.
  - Dorgal's eleven drawings from memory, the two bells, and his teaching ("press it through the sigil like a seal into wax"). Here only the notes and the wet rag show what he was trying.
  - Dorgal's unrecognized pride.
- **What's gained:**
  - Rolph returning the crooked square "exactly where it had been" carries his respect without a word.
  - The storekeeper's look toward the porters' lodge is the joke, unexplained.
  - Dorgal's test of the square with his own wet rag is sharper as pure action: he checks it before he asks.
  - The bell striking over Dorgal's unfinished lamp-square marks the night and his distraction at once.
