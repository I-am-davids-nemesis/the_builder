## Petra

He'd gone out at the first bell after lunch and he came back with the sun in the gate.

Petra was the only one still in the yard, because Kolb had kept her back to work on her guard, and because, if she was honest, she'd wanted to see whether he came back at all. Thirty miles. Kolb had said it the way he said everything, as if it were the weather, and the whole yard had gone quiet, and Baker had said, "Yes, sir," and gone. Three times to Hollin and back. Somebody had laid a bet he'd be found asleep in a ditch. Somebody else had laid a bet he'd keep going and never stop, all the way home to wherever it was he came from, and Petra had thought that was unkind, and also not impossible.

He came through the gate at a jog that was barely a jog. His face was gray with dust, except where sweat had cut lines down it, and his tunic had gone dark and then dry and then dark again, in rings, like a tree. He stopped in front of Kolb and stood there swaying slightly.

"Sir."

"Baker." Kolb looked at him for a moment. Then he picked up something that had been leaning against the rack and held it out.

It was an iron bar, the length of a waster and the thickness of a broom handle. Petra had lifted it once, in her first year, for a dare. She'd put it down again quickly.

"The pell," Kolb said. "Until you can't."

The pell was new, a fresh trunk sunk in yesterday's hole, still pale and smelling of sap. Baker took the bar. His arms dropped a few inches when its weight came on them, then came back up. He walked to the pell and set his feet, and hit it.

It was a terrible blow. It didn't shake anything. The bar bounced off the wood with a sound like a dropped pot, and Baker's whole body rang with it, she could see it go through him, and he set his feet again and hit it again.

She lost count somewhere past sixty. The blows got slower, and lower. Somewhere in there Baker started talking, under his breath, and she drifted near enough to hear it, pretending to fix her bootlace. It wasn't complaining. It was about weight, and lines, and something being spread, and air. He'd hit the pell and say a word, and hit it and say another word, like a man reciting a lesson with a hammer.

The bar came down on the sand. He bent for it. He got it as high as his knees, and his arms stopped. They didn't drop it; they simply stopped, and stayed there, trembling, and he looked down at them with an expression of polite surprise, as though they belonged to somebody he'd only just met.

"Enough," said Kolb, from the gate. He'd been watching. Petra hadn't seen him come back. "Go and eat, Baker."

He went. Petra caught up with him at the arch, and took the iron bar out of his hands, because he was still holding it and didn't seem to know how to let go.

"Oh," he said. "Thank you. That's very kind. Thank you, um—"

He looked at her face with enormous, apologetic effort, the way you'd look at a word on the tip of your tongue.

"Petra," said Petra.

"Petra. Yes. Sorry. I knew that."

He hadn't. He never did. She'd beaten him in the yard eleven times since his first year and he'd thanked her by name for none of them. She found, as she always did, that she didn't mind; it was hard to mind, when he was so sorry about it. She smiled at him, and he blinked, as if that had surprised him too, and went off toward the dormitory with his arms hanging at his sides like a pair of wet sleeves.

## Trice

They'd changed for it, which in hindsight had been a waste of an hour.

Trice had taken her hair down, which she did perhaps twice a term and never without a reason. Sara was in green, with sleeves too thin for the dormitory corridors, and had spent ten minutes deciding which of three combs. A second-year coming up the stairs had looked at them twice and missed a step. It was, Trice had judged at the time, a reasonable return on the investment.

Baker opened the door with his elbow.

He couldn't use his hands, it turned out. He held them up to show them, a little sheepishly, both of them trembling like leaves. "Sorry," he said. "Kolb. The pell. I can't quite— Please come in. Sorry. It's a mess."

It was. The desk was a drift of paper. The bed was a drift of paper. On the floor by the desk, a sheet with AIR at the top had been weighted down with a boot. There was a tray with a teapot on it, and a cup, and Baker had evidently been trying to drink from the cup by bending down to it, because there was a ring of tea on the desk and a damp patch on his collar.

He looked at them standing in his doorway. Trice watched him take them in: the hair, the green, the combs. His face cleared with understanding.

"Oh," he said. "Are you going somewhere nice? I'm sorry, I won't keep you."

"We came here," said Trice.

"Oh! Before, you mean." He smiled, pleased to have worked it out. "That's very good of you. Please, sit. Anywhere. Not there, that's the food field. There. Sorry."

Trice sat. She did not look at Sara, because if she looked at Sara one of them would laugh, and it would not be Sara.

Sara poured the tea. She did it without asking, the way she did things, and held the cup for him while he drank, and he said "thank you" into it twice, and she said, "Tor. When did you last eat?" and he said, "Lunch, I think," and Trice could see Sara deciding not to ask which day's.

"You had a visitor this morning," Trice said.

He looked surprised. "How did you—"

"Everybody knows. There's a first-year who's told it to half the school." She folded her hands. "Douglas Sorvey offered you two gold a board for the laundry field, and you told him Sara represents you."

He went red, from the collar up. "I'm sorry. I shouldn't have said it without asking. Only, at the laundry, when Miss Sara said thirty, and then you said— I thought it was sort of— and he was very—" He stopped. "I'm sorry," he said again, to Sara this time.

"Don't be," said Sara. "You did exactly right."

"Did I?"

"Who benefits, Master Baker," said Trice, "from a man paying two gold for a thing worth thirty?"

He thought about it, honestly, the way he thought about everything. "Him," he said.

"Him. And from paying for your *failures*?" She'd heard that part too. The first-year had been very thorough. "He'll pay for every failure you ever make, and then he'll own every failure you ever make, and the successes that come after them, and you'll find one morning that there's a carriage in the court for you, and it's not taking you anywhere you'd have chosen. That's how the Sorveys buy. They buy early, and then they own the stable."

"He did seem," said Baker slowly, "very sure."

"He's always very sure," said Trice. "It's his best quality."

Sara put the cup down. She'd let Trice have the politics; now it was hers. "Twelve gold," she said. "Each. That's what the Debri house will pay you, for as many as you can make. And twenty if you can get it onto a metal plate."

Baker made a sound that wasn't a word.

"Twelve," he said. "He said two, and I thought two was—"

"I know what he said." Sara's voice was quite gentle. "Pine warps, Tor. Paint flakes. It's a beautiful field and it's on a board that'll be firewood in five years, and the people who buy fields like this know it, and that's why it's thirty and not three hundred. Put it on copper, or brass, anything that won't warp, and it lasts. Lasting is what people pay for."

He wasn't looking at her anymore. He was looking past her, at the lamp on the desk, at its brass foot, and his lips were moving. Trice had seen him do that in the yard yesterday with a knife against his neck. She'd seen it on the sand, coughing up powder. She was beginning to know what it meant.

"You'd have to etch it," he said, to the lamp. "Paint won't sit on metal, not properly. You'd etch it, with acid. The store has— I'd need to think about the depth of the line—" He stopped. He looked at Sara. "Sorry. Yes. Twenty. That's— yes."

"Good," said Sara.

Then something crossed his face, and stayed.

"Only," he said, "I might not be able to. To make very many. Not past two weeks." He looked at his hands, still trembling on his knees. "Kolb's set me a shield. It has to stop his blows and a cutter, for twenty minutes, with me inside it. And if it doesn't—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. "I'd hate to promise the house a lot of boards and then be— not able to deliver them."

Trice looked at him for a long moment. Of all the things she'd come prepared for tonight, a scholarship boy apologizing in advance for the inconvenience of his own death had not been on the list.

"Master Baker," she said. "We have every confidence in you. As it happens, we heard Kolb discussing you with the head of our department yesterday, after the bell. He said you'd manage it. He said he wouldn't have set it otherwise."

"Your department?"

"Mm," said Trice. "And besides, the school doesn't kill students. Hardly ever. They're paid by the head."

He looked at her with wide, grateful eyes.

"Oh," he said. "That's— thank you. That's very reassuring." He meant it. She could see that he meant it. He thought about it a moment longer, and his face cleared further. "So he only said it to make me work harder. So that I'd take it seriously."

"Yes," said Sara.

"That's very clever of him." A pause. "He did cut the pell in half, though."

Nobody had anything to say to that.

"There's one more thing," Sara said, after a moment, in a slightly different voice, the voice her father used, Trice suspected, when a contract was about to be real. "If I'm to represent you. I'd do the selling, and the carting, and the arguing, and you'd do the building and nothing else. And the split would be ten in the hundred."

"Ten," said Baker.

"Ten in the hundred. On everything sold."

He thought about it. His lips moved, briefly. "Is that usual?"

"For a first contract it's usual."

"Then yes," he said, and smiled at her, a real smile, wide and relieved. "Yes. Thank you. That's very kind."

Trice looked at Sara. Sara was looking at Baker, and didn't look back.

"Good," Sara said. "Then that's done." She stood, and so did Trice, and Baker got up so fast he knocked the boot off the AIR sheet. "I'd give you a card," Sara said. She made a small gesture down at the green, which had no pockets, and nowhere about it that would hold so much as a pin. "But. If you need us, ask Rolph. Or Tovey. Either of them can find us."

Baker laughed politely. It was a very small laugh, of the kind people give when they've been told a joke in a language they only half speak.

"Goodnight, Master Baker," said Trice.

"Goodnight. Thank you. Thank you for coming. Before your— before you go wherever you're going. I hope it's nice."

The door closed behind them. The bar dropped into place on the other side with a soft wooden knock.

## Sara

She was at the top of the stairs before she realized she'd left her comb on his bed.

"Go on," she told Trice. "I'll catch you."

The corridor was dark between the lamps, and cold, and her sleeves were, as Trice had told her they would be, too thin for it. She went back along it quietly, because it was late and because she always went quietly, and she had her hand up to knock when she heard him through the door.

He was talking to himself. He did that; she'd noticed it in the yard. It wasn't loud.

"Ask Tovey," he was saying. "Ask Count Thompson. To go and *fetch* them. For me." A small breath of a laugh. "Very good. That's very good." Then, more quietly, and not as if it were funny at all: "I suppose I deserved that."

Sara stood with her hand raised.

The pen started, on the other side of the door. Scratch, and scratch. "Copper," he said. "Not pine. Copper, acid, the depth of the line. Air but not dust. Twelve, twenty." A pause. "Ten in the hundred." Another pause. "Very kind."

She lowered her hand. She didn't knock. She went back down the corridor, and down the stairs, and found Trice waiting at the bottom with her arms folded against the cold.

"Where's your comb?"

"I'll get it tomorrow." Sara put her arm through Trice's, for the warmth. "He thinks we were joking. About Tovey."

"Of course he does."

"He thinks Tovey would be insulted to be asked."

"Tovey would be delighted to be asked. He'd go and get us himself. He'd bring a lantern." Trice walked a few steps. "And Rolph?"

Sara thought about it.

"He didn't even hear Rolph," she said.

They went out into the court. The bell tower stood up black against the stars. Behind them, on the top floor of the dormitory, one window was still lit, and stayed lit, long after they'd stopped looking back at it.

## Notes on the mode

- **The non-sexual staging works through Trice's ledger.** She treats the dressing up as an investment that didn't pay. The second-year's missed step is the only outside reaction. Tor's misreading (they must be going somewhere nice) is the joke.
- **The 10% stays balanced.** Sara's "ten in the hundred, on everything sold" and Tor's "That's very kind" read either way. Trice looks at Sara; Sara doesn't look back. Neither viewpoint thinks about which way it runs.
- **The flabbergast beat moves outside Tor.** Since he never holds the viewpoint, Sara overhears it. That turns his private reaction into a small sadness for her ("I suppose I deserved that"), and lets the coda carry the Rolph hint ("He didn't even hear Rolph").
