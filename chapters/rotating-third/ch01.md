* * *

The tower bell at Lairdgren struck the first hour of the rest day, and Bet Haskins got up off her stool to look in on the scholarship boy again.

She had been keeper of the isolation chambers for thirty-one years, and in that time she had learned the bell the way a miller learns his wheel. She could tell you which stroke was the clapper's and which was the echo off the chapel roof. She could tell a student by how they came in under it: the ones who'd be out again in an hour, sulking, having built nothing; the ones who'd sit a day and a night and come out with a lamp-square that worked about half the time. The bell went on whether anyone listened or not. Up in the tower it had been striking since before the school was a school, since before the tower itself, some said. The bottom three courses were not stone at all but something older and grayer and smoother, poured rather than cut, that no frost in living memory had managed to crack. Bet had put her hand on it once as a girl. It had been warmer than it ought to be.

The chambers ran along the undercroft of the east range, six doors in a row, each with a shutter at eye height and a slate on a nail beside it. The slate on the fourth door said, in her own chalk:

*BAKER, T. — In: late, fourth day.*

Under that, a tally. One stroke for every bell she'd looked in on him since. She counted them with her thumb. Thirty, near enough.

She slid the shutter back.

The chamber was a stone box, whitewashed once, with a bench, a stool, a high window no bigger than a loaf, and a lamp that had burned down to a blue bead. On the bench lay a square of pale pine and a saucer of blue paint. On the stool sat the boy, exactly as he had been sitting at the last bell, and the bell before that, and every bell since late on fourth day: hands flat on his thighs, back straight, eyes open and fixed on the pine.

He was a small thing. That had been the first thing anybody said about him, three years ago, when he'd come up from the forests round Two Rivers on a scholarship with the king's own seal on it, though nobody had ever been able to say why the king should have bothered. The kitchens had called him the king's boy for a term or two, then got used to him. He was twenty now, by his records, and could have passed for fifteen in a poor light. He rarely smiled. He talked to himself. He thanked her every time he signed the slate, which no other student had ever done.

He didn't look as though he were breathing. She had seen that before and didn't worry about it. She looked instead at the board.

The paint on it was dry.

She'd watched him lay the last of it down at the turn of the night, the brush going so slowly she'd thought at first he'd fallen asleep with it in his hand. That was perhaps four bells ago. Cheap blue house paint on unsealed pine should still have been tacky; it should have been tacky till the end of the rest day. It wasn't. Even from the door she could see the dull, settled skin it had, like a puddle frozen over. In thirty-one years she had seen paint do that perhaps a dozen times. She had stopped telling anyone when she saw it. Nobody ever believed a keeper about anything.

"Well, then," she said, to nobody.

The boy blinked.

It was a very ordinary blink. It was the first one in some hours. Then he took a breath, a long one, that went down further than breaths usually go, and let it out, and his hands came up off his thighs as if he'd only just noticed they were there. He looked at them.

Bet unbolted the door.

"What bell?" he said. His voice came out cracked and small. He tried again. "I'm sorry. What bell is it, ma'am?"

"First. Rest day."

He looked up at her. She watched him do the sum. She watched it come out wrong, and then come out right, which was worse.

"Rest day," he said. "Then I've missed—fifth day. All of fifth day." His face went white under the gray. "Oh, no. Oh, they'll send me home."

Bet had nothing to say to that, so she didn't say anything.

He tried to stand, and his knees didn't like it. He caught himself on the bench with both hands and stood bent over it for a moment, breathing, the way old men stand when they've got out of a cart. Then he straightened, slowly, a piece at a time, and she heard something in his back go off like a knuckle.

"Sorry," he said, to his back.

"You'll want feeding."

"Yes, ma'am. Thank you, ma'am. In a moment." He wasn't looking at her. He'd picked up the board and was holding it close to his face, tilting it to the bead of the lamp. He touched one corner with the tip of his finger, very lightly, and looked at the finger. Clean. He let out a breath that was almost a laugh.

"It's dry," he said.

"So I see."

"It's not meant to be dry yet." He turned it over, looked at the back, turned it face up again. "I don't know if it works. I don't know if it does anything at all. I have to see." He was already moving to the door, the board held against his chest, his steps short and careful. "I need something wet. Laundry. I need laundry. Thank you, ma'am, I'm sorry, thank you."

"Sign the slate."

He came back and signed the slate. His hand shook a little. Under her chalk he wrote *Out: first bell, rest day*, and then, after a moment's hesitation, *Thank you*.

Then he was gone up the undercroft steps into the gray morning, and she stood in the chamber doorway and listened to his feet cross the court, uneven, hurrying, going toward the dormitories and not toward the kitchens at all.

Bet Haskins looked at the empty stool for a while. Then she wiped the slate clean with the heel of her hand and went up to her breakfast, where, though she had stopped telling anyone anything years ago, she found she had something to say.

* * *

The washing house smelled of wet stone and other people's stockings, and Davie had been in it for most of a bell without getting anything clean.

He had done it properly. He had worked the pump handle himself, which nobody at home would have believed, and filled a tub, one of the old white ones that stood along the wall on iron feet cast like an animal's paws. Nobody knew what animal. Nobody knew why the tubs were glazed inside like a dinner plate, either, or why there had once been so many of them that the school could fill a room. He had put his shirts in it, and his second-best hose, and pushed them under, and swirled them, and hauled them up and wrung them until his wrists burned, and every time they came out exactly as gray as they'd gone in, only colder.

Three tubs down, the scholarship boy was making foam.

Davie watched him sideways. The boy was small, smaller than Davie's youngest sister, in a brown tunic gone shiny at the elbows. He rubbed something pale against a collar, folded the cloth over on itself, and worked it with his knuckles in short hard strokes, and the water in his tub rose up white and crackling around his wrists. When he pushed the foam aside it clung to his forearms like lace.

Davie looked down into his own tub. The water lay flat and gray and unrepentant.

It was the pump, obviously. The boy had come in later and had the better water, the way the first pour from a jug was always clearer than the last. Davie considered changing tubs. He considered how that would look. If anyone from the third-year table walked in and found him chasing the good water from tub to tub like a dog after a dropped bun, he would hear about it until midsummer.

He scrubbed harder. He splashed himself in the eye and said something that at home would have got him sent from the table.

The boy looked up.

"Your water," Davie said. He said it to the wall above the boy's head, as if he were remarking on the weather. "Is that from the near pump? Only mine's come out rather poor."

"It's the same pump, sir." The boy looked at Davie's tub. Then he looked at Davie's hands, which were red and empty, and at the bench beside Davie, which was empty too. Something in his face went careful. Davie knew the look. Footmen wore it when someone at table had said something stupid and it would be their job not to have heard.

"Would you like some soap, sir?" The boy held up the pale lump. "I've plenty. I bought too much."

Davie stared at it. It was gray and cracked and smelled faintly of ash.

"Is that what that is," he said.

"Yes, sir." The boy was already coming over, drying his hands on his tunic, which did nothing. "You wet it, and rub it into the dirty parts. Collars and cuffs, mostly, and under the arms. Then you work the cloth against itself, like this. Like kneading, a bit. You don't have to be gentle with linen. You do with wool, or it shrinks to fit a cat." He put the soap down on the edge of Davie's tub. "Rinse after. Otherwise it itches."

"I knew that," Davie said.

"Yes, sir." The boy went back to his own tub.

That was all. He didn't wait to be thanked. He didn't look back to see whether Davie was doing it right. Davie picked up the soap, which was slippery and unpleasant, and rubbed it into a cuff, and folded the cuff over on itself and worked it with his knuckles. A thin froth rose around his wrists. He watched it for longer than he meant to.

The boy, meanwhile, had wrung out his own things and laid them along the bench in a row: two shirts, a pair of trousers, a towel, all still dark with water. Then he took a square of wood from inside his tunic and set it down beside them. It was cheap wood, a hand across, pale and unsealed, with a pattern painted on it in blue. The paint had cracked where the grain drank it.

The boy put both hands flat on the bench on either side of the square and looked at it for a long moment.

"Right," he muttered. "Corners first. Don't rush it."

"My dear Baker."

Davie knew the voice before he turned. Everyone in the school knew it. Dorgal Sorvey stood in the doorway with one shoulder on the frame, as if he'd been passing and the washing house had caught his eye the way a shop window might.

"They've found you a trade at last," Sorvey said. "I always said you had a washerwoman's hands. Do you take in mending? I've a cuff that wants turning, and you'd be doing a kindness to a man of limited means."

"I'm sorry," Baker said. "I'm only doing my own."

Sorvey's gaze drifted down the row of tubs. It stopped at Davie's.

Davie bent over his washing and scrubbed at a cuff that was already clean. He did not look up. He scrubbed until the soap was gone and his knuckles stung, and his face was hot, and he told himself that it was the water.

"And what's this?" Sorvey came in, unhurried, and leaned over the bench to look at the square. "Quaint. Very quaint. Does it do anything?"

"I don't know yet," Baker said. "That is, I think so. I hope so."

Then the door opened again, and a good many people came through it.

* * *

The word at the rest-day breakfast had been that Baker had come out of the isolation chambers after thirty hours, gray as a tallow candle, carrying a board, and gone straight to the washing house. Tovey had stood up to see why. A dozen others had stood up because he had.

He hadn't asked them to. He never did. He had stopped looking behind him some time in his first year, because they were always there.

The washing house was low and damp and full of steam from somebody's copper. Sorvey was in it, which was no surprise, and the fifth son of a county, which was, and Baker, in shirtsleeves, with his hands flat on a bench.

"Sorvey," Tovey said. It was a greeting, and also the end of whatever Sorvey had been saying. Sorvey straightened from the bench and smiled and said nothing more.

"Scholar Baker."

Baker took his hands off the bench as if it had burned him. "Sir. My lord. I'm sorry, I'll be out of your way in just a moment, I'm nearly done—"

Tovey waited for the apology to finish. It was a long one. Baker had better manners than half the third year, he thought, for all he'd been raised in a forest. Better than Sorvey's, certainly.

"You built something," Tovey said. "I'd like to see it work."

"It's for drying, sir. Laundry." Baker looked at the square as though it might have changed while he wasn't watching. "It's probably nothing. I've missed a whole day of classes for it, so if it's nothing, they'll likely send me home."

Tovey waited for the rest of the joke.

There wasn't any. Baker said it with a perfectly straight face, which was perhaps the joke. Somewhere behind Tovey, someone laughed politely.

"Proceed," Tovey said.

Baker touched the middle of the painted square with two fingers.

Nothing happened, for about the space of a breath. Then the shirts on the bench darkened, all at once, as if someone had poured more water over them, and Tovey realized it was the water coming *out*: welling from the weave in beads, running together, sliding off the cloth and over the edge of the bench in a thin clear sheet. It pattered on the flagstones. It ran in a widening fan toward the drain and toward several pairs of good shoes, which stepped back. The shirts lay where they were, pale and flat and dry. The towel had stiffened slightly, like a towel left in the sun.

The fifth son of a county yelped. He was holding up his arms. His sleeves had been soaked to the elbow, and they were dry.

Tovey thought of his mother's house in the wet months: the linen hung in the long gallery for days and still smelling of the river, the maids carrying it room to room after the fires, his mother saying nothing about it because there was nothing to be done. She would like this. She would like it very much.

"Can it be bought?" he asked.

"I— it has to go to my building master, sir. For testing. It isn't mine to sell. I'm sorry."

"I asked whether it could be bought," Tovey said. "Not whether you would be in trouble." He frowned. "Why would you be in trouble?"

Baker opened his mouth and nothing came out of it.

Behind Tovey the room had found its voice. *Can you make another? I'll take one. Two, my sister's at the Ridge house and it's damp as a well. How long does it last? Does it do wool?* Baker turned toward each voice as it came, the way a sparrow turns toward each crumb, and his face went from pale to paler.

Tovey looked back at them. They wanted it because he had. By supper there would be twenty of them asking, and by the next rest day half the school would have heard that Tovey of House A had wanted a laundry board, and something meant for his mother would have become a thing people were seen to own.

He had wanted a gift. He seemed to have started a market.

* * *

"Well," said Trice in Sara's ear, from the doorway. "That's going to be a problem for somebody."

The washing house floor was wet in a long fan from one bench, and the bench had a little square of wood on it, and everyone in the room was looking at the square, except Tor, who was looking at the floor as if he meant to mop it.

Trice went in the way she went into every room, without hurrying and without stopping. Sara went with her. Tovey's crowd made space for Trice without seeming to decide to.

"Master Baker," Trice said. "What have you done?"

"It dries clothes, ma'am. That's all."

"That's all," Trice repeated, as if setting it down somewhere to look at later. She turned her head. "Sara. What would it fetch?"

Sara crouched by the bench. She didn't touch the square; you didn't, with someone else's work, not until they offered. Pine, unsealed. You could see the saw marks. The pattern was in blue house paint, the cheap kind dyers sold by the pot for doors and shutters, and it had soaked into the soft grain unevenly and cracked as it dried. In a damp room the wood would cup within the year. The paint would flake before that.

In the glass halls on the river in the capital, she had seen fields set into ironwood with the lines inlaid in silver wire, on velvet, under lamps, with a clerk at each table writing down who'd looked at what. Her father had walked her past them when she was nine and said, *Price the board first, then the work, then the years. The years are where they cheat you.*

"It depends what it's on and how long it'll hold," she said. "This is pine and paint. You're buying a season, maybe two, before the wood moves and the lines break. On ironwood, with the lines set properly, you'd be having a very different conversation." She stood. "As it is, thirty gold."

Someone whistled.

"Thirty," Trice said. "Against what?"

"Against anyone in the capital having one, which they don't." Sara looked at Tor. He hadn't moved. "Tor. Thirty gold. Did you hear me?"

"It's only paint," Tor said.

"I know it's only paint. That's why it's thirty and not three hundred."

He didn't seem to hear that. His lips were moving. Sara watched them. He was looking past her at the crowd, and his eyes went from face to face along the wall, and he was counting. *Eleven. Twelve.* He reached the end of the wall and started on the doorway. Then he looked down at the little square of pine, and Sara knew that look: her father's clerks wore it when a cargo came in that they'd have to unload alone, in the rain, before morning.

"Tor," she said. "Nobody's asked you for twenty of them."

"Twenty-three," Tor said, under his breath, and then looked up, startled, as if she'd caught him at something. "Sorry. What?"

Trice was watching him too. She had the expression she wore over a game board when the other player had just done something she hadn't predicted and she wasn't yet sure whether it was a mistake.

"Who's had it in their hands besides you?" Trice asked.

"Nobody, ma'am. It has to go in for testing."

"Good," said Trice. "Keep it that way."

* * *

Rolph heard the washing house before he reached it. A room that loud on a rest day meant either a fight or something for sale, and nobody was shouting, so it was something for sale.

He came in sideways through the press with his elbows ready and took the room's measure the way he always did, the way he'd been taught to take a room, by who stood nearest whom. Tovey by the bench, which meant everyone else was by Tovey. Sorvey a careful step behind, which was where Sorvey always stood when he wanted to be in a story without being blamed for it. The girls. A fifth son with his sleeves rolled up. And Tor, in the middle of it, with his back nearly against the bench and his shirts folded behind him and his face the color of the bench.

Rolph looked at the square. Plain blue lines on cheap wood, nothing much. Twenty people looking at it. For a moment he wanted very badly to understand it, to look at those lines and see what Tor saw. The moment passed. It usually did.

Mother would want one. Mother would want one before anyone else's mother had one, which was the whole point of wanting things, in Mother's opinion.

"Right!" he said, loud enough to turn heads. "Lovely. Marvelous. Very clever. Baker hasn't eaten in thirty hours and he's about to fall over, and if he falls over, nobody gets anything, do they? No. Trust me. I'm a merchant's son, I know a sale when I see one, and this isn't one, this is a boy who needs a plate of eggs." He had an arm round Tor's shoulders by then, and Tor's clean shirts bundled under the other arm. "The board, Little Tor. Pick up the board."

Tor picked up the board. He held it against his chest with both hands.

"Sorry," Tor said to the room in general. "I'm sorry. It has to go in for testing. I'm sorry."

"He's sorry," Rolph agreed. "Out of the way, if you'd be so kind, there's a love. Excuse us. Thank you. Excuse us."

The crowd parted for him. Then, as he steered Tor out into the courtyard and the cold air, Rolph heard it close up again behind them, and heard the footsteps, a lot of them, coming along after.

* * *

They came up the dormitory stair with half the washing house behind them. Rolph could hear them on the turns below, the scuff and patter of a crowd trying to look as if it just happened to be going the same way.

"Don't look back," he said. "It only encourages them."

Tor didn't look back. He was holding the board against his chest with both hands, the way he might have carried a hot loaf out of an oven, and taking the stairs one at a time, like an old man. Thirty hours on a stool in an isolation chamber did that to a body. Rolph had seen it before. He'd see it again; it was only a question of when.

Their door was the fourth along the top corridor, plain oak with a plain iron latch. Nothing about it said anything. Rolph liked that about it. As they came within a stride, something on the inside gave a soft wooden knock, the sound of a bar lifting out of its brackets, and the door eased an inch off the jamb by itself.

Behind them on the landing, somebody said, "Did you see—"

Rolph put Tor through the gap, followed him, and shut the door with his heel. On the inside the bar was already coming down again: a length of plain ash, dark with handling, lowering itself into its brackets as if somebody's hand were on it. It dropped home with a *clunk*. A moment later someone knocked, politely, and then less politely, and then voices conferred on the far side, and then feet went away down the corridor, not all of them.

"Well," Rolph said. "That's the door."

The room was a narrow one, two beds and two desks and one window, with the light coming in long and gray over the dormitory roofs. Tor's half was all paper. Paper on the desk, paper under the desk, scraps of pine and offcuts of board on the sill with half a pattern painted on each, a jar of brushes, a saucer of dried blue. Rolph's half had a bed, made, a shelf of books, and at the foot of the bed the chest.

The chest was the size of a bread bin, dark wood and darker iron, with a lock plate on the front that had no keyhole. It had come with him from home in his first year, which was the arrangement. Rolph went to it now and laid his palm on the lid, and the lock plate gave the same soft knock as the door had, and the lid came loose under his hand.

He took out a few coins for later. The rest of it gleamed up at him, yellow and heaped, the way it always did. He shut it again.

Tor had sat down on his own bed with the board on his knees. He was looking across at the door bar.

"It held," he said. "I was worried it wouldn't hold, with that many people. It's only meant to know two."

"It held," Rolph agreed.

Tor had made the door out of the chest, the winter before last. He'd asked first. He'd asked very carefully, the way he asked for everything, as if he expected the answer to be no and wanted it on record that he'd understood. Then he'd sat on the floor in front of the chest for an evening with his chin on his fists, and the next morning there had been a pattern painted on the inside of the door in that same cheap blue, and the bar lifted itself when they came home.

Rolph had sat on that same floor for most of an afternoon afterward, watching the bar. Up, down. Up, down. He had walked out into the corridor and come back in nine or ten times. He'd looked at the blue lines until they swam. He had understood exactly as much at the end of the afternoon as at the start, which was that it worked.

He looked at the board on Tor's knees now. The same blue lines, a different shape. They meant nothing to him at all.

"Thirty gold," he said. "Sara said thirty?"

"She was being kind."

"Sara's never been kind about a price in her life. It's not in her." He dropped onto his own bed opposite, boots and all, and leaned back against the wall. "Mother would want one."

Tor looked up.

"Mother would want one *first*," Rolph said. "That's the trouble. Before anyone else has one. You don't know what it's like, Little Tor. It's not about the laundry. Among the—" he waved a hand, a gesture that took in Tovey and the girls and the whole loud washing house, "—among the nobility, if you've got a new thing before anyone else has it, that's worth more than the thing. That's what they were all doing down there. Tovey wants it, so they want it, so their mothers will want it, and the one who gets it first gets to have people round to look at it. Trust me."

"For laundry," Tor said.

"For anything. A lamp. A lock." He nodded at the door. "If anyone knew about that, you'd have a queue down the stairs."

"There's a queue down the stairs now."

"There is a queue down the stairs now," Rolph allowed. "Well. You'll have to make one for her, then."

"Of course." Tor said it at once, before Rolph had finished. Then, under his breath, looking at nothing: "Twenty-four."

"Nobody asked you for twenty-four."

"Sara said that."

"Sara's right about most things." Rolph looked at the ceiling for a moment. "Bring it yourself."

"Sorry?"

"Hers. The one for Mother. Bring it yourself. Come home with me over the break. To the capital." He'd meant to ask in a week or two, when it would sound offhand. He'd written to Mother about Tor in the autumn, and she'd written back by the next post, which she never did, and she'd asked a great many questions. He kept that to himself.

Tor had gone still in a different way from before. "I couldn't."

"You could."

"Not on your holiday. Your family will want you to themselves, they won't want somebody underfoot the whole time, eating their food and taking up a bed and needing things explained. I'd be in the way. I'd only be a burden on them."

"A burden," Rolph said.

"I would."

"It's a big house, Little Tor. Big. Draughty. Full of forks. You could lose a regiment in it. Nobody would notice one baker."

"Forks?"

"Dozens. You'll survive the forks." He grinned at the ceiling. "Trust me. I'll write and tell them you're coming."

Tor looked at the board on his knees. "If your mother wouldn't mind," he said, very quietly, as if the answer might still be no.

"She'd mind if you didn't." Rolph put his hands behind his head. "If it did fruit, now. That'd be something. Apples. Plums. Beans. You'd not believe how much rots at home in a wet year. It's all anyone talks about at dinner some autumns, the accounts come in and it's so many bushels lost in the low barns, and so many in the carts on the road, and everyone looks very grave and passes the salt—"

He stopped, because Tor had stopped listening.

It was always easy to tell. Tor's face didn't change, exactly. It just stopped being pointed at you. He was looking at the board on his knees, and then through it, and his lips were moving.

"Cloth wants to let go," Tor said, not to Rolph. "Cloth's all little channels, it's easy, the water's only sitting in it. An apple's got a skin. The water's inside. You'd have to go slower. If you go fast the outside would seal, like a crust on a loaf before the middle's done, and then it would rot from the inside, worse than before—"

He got up. He didn't seem to notice he'd done it. He went to his desk, sat, pushed a stack of paper onto the floor to clear a space, found a pencil, and began to draw.

Rolph didn't move. He knew this, too.

"—and it's alive, isn't it? An apple. A bit. After it's picked. Does that matter? It might matter. Beans are drier to start with. Grain's nearly dry already, that's half the job done, but then you'd want to stop it partway, you don't want dust, you want—" Pencil scratching. A sheet pulled across. "—you want it to *stop*. Cloth doesn't care when you stop."

Outside, the tower bell struck the hour. Tor didn't look up. Rolph counted the strokes, out of habit, and then just watched him: the small hunched back in the shirt with the elbows gone thin, the pencil going, a hand coming up now and then to rub at his eyes and leaving a gray smudge across one of them. He was smiling a little, Rolph realized. He didn't think Tor knew.

It was funny, in its way. It was the funniest thing about Tor, how he could be lifted clean out of a room by a sentence. You could mention apples and lose him for a bell.

Rolph looked at the pattern growing under the pencil and, for a moment, wanted very badly to be lost like that. Then the moment passed, and he went on watching.

Some time later, the pencil stopped.

Tor sat very still. Then he turned round on the stool, and his face was pointed at Rolph again, and it was stricken.

"Sorry," he said. "Oh, I'm sorry. You were talking. You were telling me about your mother, and I just—"

"I was telling you about beans."

"I'm sorry."

"I'll live." Rolph swung his boots off the bed. "You haven't eaten. You're the color of that wall. Stay there and don't build anything while I'm gone." He went to the door. The bar lifted for him. "I mean it. Nothing."

The corridor outside was empty except for two first-years sitting on the top step, who stood up very quickly when they saw him and pretended to be going down.

* * *

He came back with a tray: a heap of eggs, half a loaf, a wedge of cheese, a pot of the cafeteria's dreadful tea, and two apples, because he was not above a joke. Tor ate everything except one apple, which he turned in his fingers for some time and then set on the desk beside his drawings, as if it were a reference.

The afternoon went slowly after that, which was what Rolph had wanted. Tor ate, and dozed sitting up against the wall, and woke, and ate the end of the loaf, and moved stiffly to the desk to write something down, and moved stiffly back again. He winced when he stretched. Every so often he said, "Sorry," about nothing in particular, and Rolph said, "Stop that," and Tor said, "Sorry."

Rolph lay on his own bed with a book open on his chest. It was *Principles of Field Theory, Third Year*, and he was, in a general way, studying it. He had been on the same page for some time. Mostly he watched Tor, over the top of it, and kept the room quiet.

Nobody knocked again. Once someone tried the door, gently, and the bar didn't lift, and they went away.

By the sixth bell Tor was asleep on top of his blankets with his boots still on and one hand trailing toward the floor. Rolph got up and took the boots off him. He looked at the drawings on the desk on his way back: arrows, half-patterns, a little picture of what might have been a plum, a word underlined three times. He couldn't read the word. He put out the lamp.

* * *

He woke in the dark to the sound of a pencil.

There was a candle lit on Tor's desk, the stub of one, and Tor was bent over it in his shirt, writing fast. Not drawing. Writing, line after line, the pencil going so hard Rolph could hear it bite. Tor was muttering under his breath, too low to make out, and once he laughed, a short surprised sound, and wrote faster.

"Tor," Rolph said.

Nothing.

Rolph lay and listened to the pencil for a while. Then he turned over and went back to sleep.

* * *

When he woke again it was barely gray at the window, and Tor's bed was empty and made, and so was Tor's corner of the desk, which was a thing Rolph had never seen. The board was gone. The door bar was down. Tor had gone out and the lock had shut itself behind him.

On the desk, squared neatly at the edge, were the night's pages.

Rolph picked up the top one. It was covered edge to edge in Tor's small cramped hand, the lines so close they nearly touched, words running into one another without spaces, broken every few inches by a scrap of pattern or an arrow looping back to something earlier. In the margin, in capitals, underlined twice: CRUST. Below that: *slower, ask about skins, NOT dust*. The rest of it might have been in another language. It might have been in several.

He turned it the other way up, in case that helped. It didn't.

Rolph put the page back exactly where it had been, squared it with the others, and went to see about breakfast.

* * *

He had come early on purpose. The copy-work was three days late, and he'd judged that the building master would be gentlest before breakfast, when there was nobody else to be ungentle to. So he was sitting on the bench outside the office with his three lamp-squares wrapped in a cloth on his knees when the scholarship boy came down the corridor at a near run, holding a board against his chest.

The first-year knew who he was. Everyone did, a little. The small third-year from the woods, the one who'd been shut in the isolation chambers half of last week. He had a gray smudge under one eye and his hair was wet at the front, as if he'd washed his face and forgotten to finish.

The boy knocked. The door opened before he'd taken his hand away.

"Baker."

"Sir. I'm sorry it's so early, sir. I finished it. I mean, I think I finished it. I tested it yesterday, at the washing house, it worked on shirts and trousers and a towel, and wool, I think, someone's sleeves, and I wanted to bring it to you before—"

"Give it here."

The building master was a long, gray, folded sort of man in an apron over his tunic, with a magnifier on a cord round his neck. He took the board in both hands and turned it to the window. He brought the magnifier up. He looked at the paint for a long time, the corners especially, tipping the board so the light ran across the lines. He touched one edge with a fingernail and looked at the fingernail.

The first-year, on the bench, held very still and tried to look like part of it.

"Washing house," said the building master. He handed the board back to Baker, took him by the upper arm, and walked him out of the office. In the corridor his eye fell on the bench. "You. What's that?"

"Copy-work, sir. Lamp-squares. Three. They're late, sir. I'm sorry."

"Bring them. I need a witness."

The first-year didn't know what for. He brought them.

They crossed the dormitory court at the building master's pace, which was very fast, with Baker half a step behind, explaining. "It's only a field for cloth, sir, it lifts the water out of the fibers, it doesn't heat anything. I used a closed pattern so it wouldn't spread. The pine's not good, I know the pine's not good, I'm sorry about the pine, it was what I had—"

"Yes," said the building master.

The washing house was empty at that hour, gray and dripping. One tap ran into one tub with a sound like a small stream. The building master let go of Baker's arm, took the board out of his hands, and set it on the bench. Then he took Baker by the collar and the back of the belt, the way you'd lift a sack, and put him in the tub.

The first-year dropped his lamp-squares.

Baker went under all the way. Water slopped over the rim in a sheet. For a count of about two there was nothing but the tub and the building master's arm in it to the elbow; then the master straightened, and Baker came up on his own, sitting in the water to his chest, hair flat to his head, his mouth already open.

"—sorry, sir," Baker said, and coughed.

"Out."

Baker climbed out. He stood on the flagstones and poured. Water ran off his tunic and his trousers and out of his boots and in a little stream from the end of his nose. The building master picked up the board, looked at Baker, looked at the board, and stepped three paces back, so that Baker stood between him and the tub. Then he touched the middle of the pattern with two fingers.

The water left.

It went out of Baker's clothes all at once, beading on the brown wool and running together and sliding down him to the floor in a clear sheet, so that for a moment he stood in a puddle of his own shape. His tunic lightened. His trousers lightened. His sleeves, which had clung to his arms, let go of them. The water ran out of his collar and down his neck and onto the stones and kept running.

Baker stood in his dry clothes with his hair still plastered flat and dripping, because hair was not cloth, and blinked. A drop fell off his eyebrow.

"Hold out your hands," said the building master.

Baker held out his hands. The building master looked at them, front and back. He took one by the wrist and pressed the thumbnail, and watched the color come back. He looked into Baker's eyes, one and then the other, close, as if he were reading small print.

"Any pain?"

"No, sir."

"Dizziness? Thirst?"

"No, sir. My boots are still wet. Inside, I mean. I think it's the leather. Leather's different, it's—"

"Yes," said the building master. He let go of the wrist. "A field like this has to be shown safe for use around people. That's what this was. Now it has been." He turned to the first-year, who had not yet picked up his lamp-squares. "You saw that."

"Sir," said the first-year. "Sir, you *drowned* him."

"I didn't drown him. He's standing there."

"You put him in a *tub*."

"That's where the water was." The building master turned the board over, looked at the back, turned it face up again, and tucked it under his arm. "I'll keep this. Baker, go to your classes. When anyone asks about last week, you were doing a building exercise for my class."

Baker's hair dripped. "But I wasn't, sir. It wasn't assigned. I just—"

"It is now."

"Yes, sir." Baker hesitated. "Will that be enough, sir? So they don't send me home?"

The building master looked at him. It was not a long look. It was the look of a man checking whether he'd heard a word correctly, and finding that he had, and deciding not to pursue it.

"Go to class, Baker," he said. To the first-year: "Pick those up. Bring them to my office. They'd better be good."

* * *

Doris came into the meditation room with a thing in her hands, as she did every morning, and set it on the low table in the middle of the circle of mats.

This morning it was a square of black glass, about the size of a palm, rimmed in something that was not quite bone and not quite horn. It had come out of the ground somewhere in the hill country, she'd been told, in a jar with a great many others like it. It was perfectly smooth on the front. Light lay on it without going in. She had owned it for eleven years and had never got to the bottom of it, which was why it was on the table.

The students came in and sat. She counted them without looking up. Twenty-two. Twenty-three.

Twenty-four was Baker, and Baker did not sit. He stood at the edge of her circle with his hair wet.

"Ma'am," he said. "About last week. I'm sorry. I was doing a building exercise for my building master's class." He said it like a lesson he'd been given to repeat. Then he kept going, which nobody had told him to do. "Only, it was a working trance, the deep kind, and that's—I mean, what we do here is the lighter kind of the same thing, isn't it, ma'am? So I was meditating. The whole time. Thirty hours, nearly. So I thought, if it's all the same to you, perhaps I wasn't really absent. From this. In a way."

He stopped. He seemed to hear himself.

"I'm sorry," he said again.

Doris looked at him. He was very pale. His hair was wet and his clothes were completely dry, which was interesting, and his hands were holding each other in front of him.

She nodded once, toward his mat.

He sat.

She let the room settle. It took the usual time. Breath slowing. The small sounds of people trying not to make small sounds. Then she looked at the glass, and they looked at the glass, and the room went quiet in the way it did.

Most of them went down slowly, and not far. Baker went down the way a stone goes into a well. One breath he was a boy with wet hair; the next there was nobody home, and the black glass had all of him. He always did that. She had noticed it in his first year, and noticed it again every year since, and she had not yet decided what she thought of it.

She sat with her hands in her lap and watched him not move for most of a bell.

* * *

The theory master was standing in the doorway when they came in, which he never did, rubbing his hands together, which he did constantly, and when he saw Baker he actually said, "Ha!"

Dorgal took his usual seat, third bench, by the window. He took out his notes. He arranged his pens. He did not look round.

"Baker! There you are. Sit, sit, no, there, at the front, where I can see you. I've been talking to your building master." The theory master's eyebrows went up and stayed up. "At breakfast. At *length*. He was insufferable. I had to leave before the porridge."

"I'm sorry, sir," said Baker.

"Sorry! Ha!" said the theory master, and went to his desk, still rubbing his hands.

Dorgal uncapped a pen. So it worked, then, the little laundry board. He'd assumed it would. He had watched it work, yesterday, on the fifth son's sleeves, and on Baker's shirts, and on the floor of the washing house, which had been a great deal wetter afterward than before. And then Tovey had stood there and asked if it could be bought, and Baker had said *it isn't mine to sell* as if he were confessing to a theft.

Thirty gold. On *pine*.

"Now then. Fields of discrimination," said the theory master, writing the words across the board in his big looping hand. "Which you all know. Which every first-year knows, and every third-year forgets, in my experience, round about the time they start to think they're clever. Everything that lives has a vitality field. Yes? Everything. The moss on that wall. The flies on the moss. Sorvey."

Dorgal looked up.

"The more complex the creature, the stronger and more complex its field. Who's more complex, Sorvey, you or the moss?"

"On a good day, sir, me."

A laugh, small. The theory master grinned. "On a good day. And a human field, being very strong and very complicated, naturally resists any simple field you set against it. It shrugs them off. To touch living tissue directly takes a specific, focused effort, and that, ladies and gentlemen, is why you can't kill your roommate with a lamp-square, however much you might like to. Distinguishing living from not-living. Write it down. It'll be on everything I ever give you."

Dorgal wrote it down. It was on everything already. Two benches ahead, Baker was writing too, steadily, head bent, the way he wrote everything, as if the theory master were dictating instructions for something he meant to build that afternoon.

The theory master talked for most of a bell. Twice in that time he stopped in the middle of a sentence and looked at Baker and smiled, for no reason at all, and then went on.

Baker didn't see either time. Dorgal did.

At the end, as they gathered their things, the theory master said, "Baker. A word, later in the week. Nothing urgent. Something I'd like to show you." He rubbed his hands. "Ha!"

"Yes, sir," said Baker. "I'm sorry, sir."

Dorgal capped his pen and put it away, and found that he'd pressed hard enough on the last line to tear the paper.

* * *

"He put you in the *tub*?"

"In the tub. All the way in."

Rolph laughed so hard he had to put his spoon down. Half the long table looked round. He didn't care. "Oh, I'd have paid. I'd have *paid*, Little Tor. Your face. Tell me about your face."

"I don't know about my face. I was under the water."

"Tell me about the first-year, then."

"He said 'you drowned him.'"

"He didn't."

"He said it twice."

The cafeteria was full and loud and smelled of the stew, which was the stew it always was on the first day of the week. Rolph had saved them the end of a bench by the windows. Tor had come in looking like a boy who'd been put in a tub, and eaten two bowls without seeming to notice, and was now working through a third more slowly, telling the morning a piece at a time, between mouthfuls.

He'd got as far as Doris. Rolph had heard about the black glass and was waiting for the theory master, who by all accounts had been very odd indeed.

"He said *ha*," Tor said. "A lot. I think he's pleased about something."

"I should think he is."

"Do you think it's about the building master? They had breakfast. He said the building master was insufferable."

"I think," Rolph said, "it might possibly be about you."

Tor looked at him as if he'd made a joke he couldn't quite follow, and went back to his stew.

Rolph opened his mouth to try again, and then saw Tor's spoon stop.

It stopped halfway, and stayed there. Tor was looking at something over Rolph's shoulder. His face did not change at all, which with Tor was always worse than if it had.

Rolph turned.

Kolb was crossing the cafeteria. He was not hurrying. He was a tall man, middle-aged by the look of him, and completely bald, with a pale scar running from the corner of one eye down into his jaw. He wore a sleeveless jerkin, and there wasn't much of him that wasn't cord: long arms roped with it, a neck like a bundle of it, none of the bulk of a wrestler. He walked between the tables the way he walked the yard, with the noise of the room going down a little on either side of him and coming back up after he'd passed. He reached their bench and stopped. He did not sit.

"Baker," he said.

Tor put his spoon down. He stood. "Sir."

"Sit down."

Tor sat.

"Fifth day," Kolb said. His voice was low and quite pleasant, the voice of a man remarking on the weather to an acquaintance. Nobody beyond their end of the bench could have heard it. "You weren't in my yard."

"No, sir. I'm sorry, sir. I was doing a building exercise for—"

"I don't care." Kolb said it without heat. "I don't have building exercises, Baker. I have a yard. You owe it an afternoon. We'll start collecting today." He considered Tor for a moment longer. "I'll see you at the bell."

He went. The noise came back up behind him.

Rolph turned back. Tor was sitting very straight, with his hands flat on the table on either side of his bowl. He was looking at the stew.

"Listen," Rolph said. "Little Tor. Listen to me. He's all bark."

Tor nodded.

"Well. Mostly bark. He does this every term to somebody, it's practically a hobby. And I'll be there, all right? I'll partner you. He can't run you any harder than he runs me, and he's been trying for three years, and look at me. Fine. Perfectly fine."

Tor nodded again. His eyes hadn't moved from the stew.

"You'll be sore," Rolph said. "That's all. You'll be sore and you'll complain and I'll rub liniment on your back and you'll say sorry about it the whole time, and in a week he'll have forgotten. Tor. Are you hearing me?"

"Yes," Tor said. "Sorry. Yes."

He wasn't. Rolph could see it. He'd seen Tor lifted out of a room by a sentence about apples; this was the other thing, the thing that came for him in rooms full of people and shut him up inside himself, and nothing Rolph said was going to reach through it. Rolph knew how to get Tor out of a washing house. He knew how to get him out of a crowd, out of Tovey's attention, out of a dinner with nobles. He had no idea in the world how to get him out of this.

He put his hand on Tor's shoulder. Tor didn't seem to feel it.

The tower bell began to strike.

Tor stood up at the first stroke, carefully, the way he'd taken the stairs the day before. He picked up his bowl, then put it down again, as if he'd forgotten where it went. He said, "Sorry," to nobody. Then he walked out of the cafeteria toward the yard, very straight and a little too slowly, and Rolph's hand slid off his shoulder and he didn't notice that either.

Rolph left both bowls where they were and went after him.
