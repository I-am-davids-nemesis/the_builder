* * *

The yard lay behind the long hall, a square of packed sand the color of old bread, fenced on three sides by weapon racks and on the fourth by a wall that had been part of something else before the school was built against it. In the middle stood the pell. It was an oak trunk as thick as a man, sunk to half its height in the ground, with the bark long gone and the wood at chest height beaten into a pale, furred mess like a chewed pencil.

Kolb was hitting it.

He was using a practice waster, a wooden blade about as slender as a fence post, and he wasn't hurrying. He set his feet, turned from the hips, and struck. The sound was flat and enormous. The racks rattled. A film of dust jumped off the top of the wall and hung there. Rolph, coming through the gate two steps behind Tor, felt it in his back teeth.

Kolb set his feet again.

"Sir," Tor said.

The waster came around. The pell took it. Something small fell off the wall.

"Baker." Kolb lowered the waster and turned, and he was smiling, which Rolph had seen perhaps twice in three years and had not enjoyed either time. "Good. You're early. I like early." His eyes moved. "Rolph. Go away."

"Sir, I said I'd partner him, I thought—"

"And I've said go away. That's two things said. Mine's the one that counts." He considered. "You can stand by the rack. Stand by the rack and be quiet, and you can watch. Most people pay to watch me."

Rolph went to the rack. He did not go any farther than the rack.

"Know what that is, Baker?"

"A pell, sir."

"What does it do?"

Tor looked at it. Rolph could see him taking the question seriously, which was the trouble with Tor; he took everything seriously, including things that had been put to him only so that he could be wrong.

"It stands there, sir," Tor said. "Mostly."

Kolb laughed. It was short and sounded genuinely pleased, and it made Rolph like this even less. "It does. It's very good at it. Watch."

He hit the pell again. This time Rolph saw the trunk move, a shudder that went down into the ground and came back up through the soles of his boots.

"That," Kolb said, in the same pleasant voice, "would kill you. Not hurt you. Kill you. Your ribs would go in and your heart would stop and you'd lie down on my sand and be dead, and I'd have to write to your mother. Unless you had armor on, or a shield field. Have you got a shield on you, Baker?"

"No, sir."

"No. Most people haven't." He leaned the waster against the pell and unhooked something from his belt. Rolph had seen it hanging there for three years and taken it for a tool: a plain hilt a hand and a half long, wrapped in cord, with a line of small marks running around the guard, too regular to be nicks.

Kolb held it out to one side, and there was a blade on it.

It didn't slide out or unfold. It was simply there, the way a thing is there when you turn around and look at it: four feet of dull gray, the length of a boy's arm and a half again. It had no edge that Rolph could see. When Kolb turned his wrist, the blade went edge-on to him and wasn't there at all: a hilt, and a long gray nothing, and then, as the wrist turned back, a blade again.

"Cutter," Kolb said. "You'll have seen pictures."

Tor nodded. He had gone the particular shade of pale that Rolph associated with dinners where there were too many forks.

Kolb walked to the pell with the cutter held loosely in one hand, as if it were a switch for cows, and swung it once, level, the way a man waves off a fly.

There was no sound at all. The blade didn't slow; it went through the oak the way a hand goes through smoke, and came out the other side still traveling, and Kolb let it finish its swing. Nothing else happened, for a moment. Then the top half of the pell, which was taller than Kolb, leaned very slightly to one side, as if it had remembered an appointment, and slid off the bottom half and fell over onto the sand with a thump that Rolph felt in his knees. It rolled a little way and stopped. The cut face was smooth as a tabletop. You could have eaten off it.

"Armor doesn't do much about that," Kolb said. "A shield does, if it's a good one. Most aren't." "You build things, Baker."

"Sir, I—a little—"

"I hear you build them very well. I hear it at breakfast whether I want to or not." The blade was gone; Rolph didn't see it go. Kolb hooked the hilt back on his belt. "So build me a shield. Stops that." He nodded at the waster. "And stops that." He tapped the hilt. "Two weeks."

"Yes, sir," Tor said. Then, because he was Tor: "For how long should it stop them, sir?"

"Twenty minutes."

"Twenty minutes, sir?"

"Of me," Kolb said. "Hitting it. With you inside."

There was a short silence in the yard.

"Thank you, sir," said Tor.

Rolph shut his eyes.

"Sir," he said, from the rack, "sir, with respect, he's a builder, he isn't—"

"Which is why I'm not asking him to fight me," Kolb said, without turning around. "I'm asking him to build. Different thing. Easier. I've seen him fight." He looked Tor over, the dust on his boots, the tunic, the hands, the way a man looks at a horse he's been told is sound. Rolph could not tell what he saw. "Now. Warm up. Run to town and back."

"Town's five miles!" said Rolph.

"So it's ten, there and back. Your arithmetic's coming along." Kolb tilted his head at Tor. "What do I always tell you, Baker? About a real fight?"

"That I should run away from it, sir."

"Good. Practice."

And the strange thing, the thing Rolph would keep turning over all afternoon while he did two hundred cuts at the remains of the pell for having spoken twice, was that Tor looked relieved. His shoulders came down. He bent and tightened his bootlaces with something close to cheer, and then he set off through the gate at an easy lope, as if someone had finally asked him to do a thing he knew how to do.

Kolb watched him go. He watched the whole way to the bend in the road, and his face, the scar and all, did nothing that Rolph could read.

"Nice and loose," Kolb said, to nobody. Then: "Rolph. You're still here. Why are you still here?"

* * *

She'd been taking cabbages into Hollin on this road for nineteen years, and in nineteen years she had seen a great many of Kolb's lot go by, because the town well was where he sent them to and from. They came past in ones and twos, purple in the face and blowing like bellows, and they slapped the stone of the well before they turned for home, because otherwise they'd be asked whether they had, and they couldn't lie to Kolb. Everybody knew that. Their boots were good and their faces were miserable, and they were the best entertainment the road had.

This one was small. He was the smallest she'd ever seen in the school's brown, and when she first saw him, a mile short of Hollin, he wasn't running at all. He was standing by the old marker stone, the gray one that was smoother than any stone had a right to be, with letters on it nobody could read. He had his hands on his hips and his back to the town, and he was looking back the way he'd come, up the long slope to where the school's bell tower stood on the ridge like a finger.

He looked at it for a long while. Then he turned his head and looked down the other road, the east one, that went off into the trees.

She pulled up the mule.

"You want a ride back, love? Nobody'll know. I've had worse than you sitting on my cabbages."

He jumped. He hadn't heard the cart, which took some doing; the near wheel squealed like a pig. "Oh! No, ma'am. Thank you, ma'am. I'm meant to be running."

"Looked like you were meant to be standing."

"Yes, ma'am. Sorry." He looked down the east road again. "Ma'am, which way is Two Rivers? From here?"

She pointed down the east road, into the trees. "That way. Four days walking, if you're quick. Five if you're sensible." She looked at him harder. "Why? You thinking of going?"

He didn't answer. He kept looking down the road. He had a face that showed everything and seemed to have no idea it did, and what it showed just then made her want, absurdly, to give him a cabbage.

"Kolb's lot, are you," she said, to change the subject. "What's he got you running for?"

"To warm up, ma'am."

"Warm up for what?"

He thought about it, honestly, like a child asked a sum. "I'm not certain, ma'am. I think possibly to be killed."

She laughed. He didn't, which made her stop.

She picked up the knife she kept on the board for cutting stalks and cut a cabbage free of its neighbor, because she was going to give him one after all and he could carry it or not. The blade went through the stalk in one stroke, and the cabbage rolled over against the side of the cart, heavy as a head.

He was watching the knife.

"It's a line," he said.

"It's a knife, love."

"No, ma'am, sorry, I mean, a blow's a weight. It comes in all over, you spread it out and it's—but an edge is a line, it all comes in at one place, so you can't spread it, you'd have to—" He stopped. His lips kept moving. "Unless you didn't stop it. Unless you turned it."

"Turned what?"

But he'd gone. Not back up the slope to the school, and not down the east road either. He went past her cart toward Hollin at a run, talking to himself, his hands making shapes in the air in front of him like a man describing a fish.

She followed him in at the mule's pace. By the time she reached the square he was across it, past the well without slapping the stone, past the chandler's, out the far side and away up the mill road, still talking. A woman at the pump called after him that the school was the other way. He didn't hear her.

She sold her cabbages. It took most of half an hour. She'd loaded the empties and was buying a twist of salt when he came back down the mill road, and she wouldn't have believed it was the same boy if not for his size. He was running hard and easy at once, the way a dog runs when it's forgotten it's tired, and he was still talking. As he passed the well he reached out and slapped the stone, the way they all did, as though he'd only just remembered he was supposed to have been there.

Then he went off up the school road at a pace none of Kolb's lot had ever gone up it, and she lost sight of him at the marker stone.

She'd kept the cabbage for him. She ate it herself, in the end, in a soup, and thought about him more than she meant to.

* * *

The yard was full by the time he came back: thirty or so in brown, pairing off along the fence, and the two of them in black, on the bench by the water butt where nobody else sat because nobody else was ever quite invited to.

Sara saw him first. He came through the gate at a run, and he was coming in faster than he'd gone out, which she knew because Rolph had told the whole yard how he'd gone out, at length. He was dust to the knees and sweat-dark down the back of his tunic, and he was talking under his breath. He nearly ran into Kolb.

"Baker. You're late."

"Sorry, sir. I went a bit past the town."

"How far past?"

He thought. "There was a mill, sir."

Kolb's expression didn't change, but he nodded once, as if somebody had told him the weather would hold. Then he turned his head. "Black. Over here."

Sara stood up. Trice stood up beside her, unhurried, and they crossed the sand together. Tor watched them come with the polite, blank face of someone being introduced to strangers, and then, when they were two strides away, his face changed completely.

"Oh," he said. "Miss Sara. Miss—Trice. I didn't—you're in *black*."

"We are, Master Baker," said Trice. "Do keep up."

"Unarmed," Kolb said. "Baker, you've got black, both of them, by turns. Trice first. Go."

Sara stepped back to the line scratched in the sand and folded her arms, and watched Trice open the way she always opened against boys, which was with a kick straight up between the legs, because it saved time.

Tor wasn't there when it arrived. He made a noise like a startled hen and was simply somewhere else, a foot to the left, and then he did what Sara would not have guessed in a hundred tries: he turned around and started to run away.

"Baker," said Kolb. "Spar."

Tor stopped. He turned back. "Sorry, sir," he said, and then to Trice, with a little bob of the head, "sorry," and put his hands up.

Trice's mouth went thin.

What followed, Sara thought, was not what she'd been told about him, and it was not what anybody in the yard had expected either, because along the fence the pairs had stopped pretending to practice. Trice was very good. Sara had known that for years, and had the bruises to prove it. Her kicks came from everywhere: low to the knee, high to the head, a front kick that changed its mind halfway and came around from the side. And Tor wasn't there for any of them. He wasn't fast so much as early. He moved before she did, small and quick and apologetic, and twice he said "sorry" when she missed him, as if her missing were his fault.

Trice did not like that. Sara saw her not liking it, the set of the jaw, the little extra breath before each attack, and she knew what was coming a beat before it came. Trice always got showy when she was cross.

The wheel kick was beautiful. Trice spun into it, her whole body turning like a door on its hinge, her heel coming around at head height fast enough to whistle.

Tor stepped inside it. He didn't go back from it, the way anybody sensible would. He went forward, into the middle of the spin where the heel couldn't reach, and caught her around the waist as she came around, and turned with her, and they both went down on the sand with Trice underneath.

Somebody along the fence said, "*Oh*," out loud.

On the ground it went on going wrong for Trice. He was smaller than she was and he didn't seem to care. He was all elbows and patience, and every time she twisted he was already where she was twisting to, saying sorry, and she was going to be pinned in a moment. Sara could see it, and so could Trice.

Then there was a knife at his throat.

Sara didn't see where it came from. She never did, with Trice. It was simply there, small and black-handled, the flat of it lying along the side of his neck under the jaw, and Tor went absolutely still.

"Unarmed" was a word Trice had always treated as advice.

For one breath nothing moved in the whole yard.

Then the sand moved.

It slid away from him all at once, in a ring, the way water jumps away from a drop of oil. It hissed. Grit and small stones skittered outward across the yard, and Trice went with them. She didn't let go so much as get put somewhere else: lifted off him, knife hand and all, and pushed back along the ground a full arm's length, her heels scoring two lines in the sand. The knife was still in her hand, held out toward him, and the point of it had stopped against nothing a hand's breadth from his throat. She leaned on it. It didn't go any farther.

Sara looked for the device. Her eyes did it before she'd decided to: wrist, neck, belt, the hem of the tunic, the places people kept shield fields when they could afford them. Nothing on his wrists. Nothing at his throat but the ties of his tunic. He might have something sewn in somewhere. A personal shield that would hold off a blade at that distance, all the way around, and push a grown girl along the ground while it did it—her father had a rule about things like that. You didn't put a price on them out loud, because whoever was listening would know you'd never been near one.

"Trice," she said. "Off. It's a shield. The knife won't help."

Trice got up. She put the knife away, somewhere, the way it had come. She looked at Tor sitting in his clean ring of sand with his hands up in front of his face, and her expression was one Sara knew. It meant *all right, then*.

She took her hand out of her pouch and threw.

The powder went out in a white burst and stopped in the air around him. It didn't fall. It clung, all over, to nothing, a fine white shell the shape of a large egg with Tor somewhere in the middle of it. For a moment it was almost pretty. Then Sara saw the shape inside it put its hands to its face, and turn its head one way and the other, blind. And then go still in a different way. Its shoulders weren't moving.

He wasn't breathing. He couldn't. Whatever kept the knife out was keeping the air out too.

"Tor," Sara said sharply.

The egg collapsed. All the powder came down at once in a soft white slump, and Tor came out of the bottom of it on his hands and knees, coughing so hard he couldn't stop, white from his hair to his elbows, like a loaf that had been rolled in flour and left on the board.

He coughed for a long time. In between coughs he was talking. Sara went close enough to hear it.

"—air," he was saying, to the sand. "It has to let the air—*hkk*—air in. Air but not—not *that*. Not dust. Sorry." This last was to Trice. "Sorry, did I hurt you? I didn't mean to push."

Trice was looking at him with her head a little on one side. She didn't answer him. She turned to Kolb.

"Sir," she said, "he has a shield."

"A good one," Sara said. "Sir, that's not a fair bout. Whatever it is, it held a knife off at—"

"And a perfectly good handful of—" said Trice.

Kolb laughed.

It was a real laugh, a big one, with his head back, and it went right across the yard and made the pairs along the fence look at each other. Sara had never heard it before. She didn't think anyone had.

"He hasn't got a shield," Kolb said. "That's my homework you're complaining about."

"Your—"

"He's building me one. Two weeks. Looks like it'll work, too." He was still smiling, which was frightening. "Which means I'm going to have to hit it properly."

On the sand, Tor stopped coughing long enough to look up at him with an expression of the most profound courtesy and dismay.

"Air, Baker," said Kolb.

"Yes, sir. Sorry, sir. I noticed."

Kolb laughed again and walked away down the fence, and the pairs along it hurriedly remembered they were practicing.

Sara looked at Tor. He was sitting back on his heels now, trying to beat the powder out of his hair and only moving it about. Not a device. Nothing sewn in. He was the smallest boy in the yard, by a long way, and he was saying sorry to Trice again.

She looked at him the way she'd once seen her father look at a ring a man had sold him as brass.

"Right," said Kolb, from the far end of the yard. "Again. Sara, your turn."

* * *

By the fourth bout, Trice had a list.

He went left when he was frightened. He watched hands, not faces, which was sensible and meant faces were worth using. He couldn't be feinted, not really, because he didn't commit to anything he hadn't seen, and you couldn't sell a boy a lie when he wasn't buying. He was early, always, a half beat before you'd decided. And he did not, at any point in the whole long afternoon, do the thing with the sand again.

She watched for it every time. She put Sara's knife to his throat herself once, borrowed, from behind, as an experiment. He froze, and said, "Oh, sorry," as if he'd stepped on her foot, and tapped the sand to concede. No ring. No push. Either he couldn't do it twice, or he'd decided not to, and she found she didn't care for either answer.

Sara put him down twice. Sara was plainer than Trice and harder to read, and he kept trying to read her anyway, which cost him. Both times, she helped him up. Both times, he thanked her for throwing him.

"You don't thank people for that," Trice told him.

"Sorry," he said. "It was a very good throw."

He landed exactly one real blow all afternoon, a palm against her shoulder that she'd left open on purpose to see what he'd do with it, and he spent the next exchange apologizing so sincerely that she hit him in the stomach out of sheer exasperation, and then he apologized for that.

The bell went on the tower. The yard emptied the way yards do, everybody suddenly tired. Tor stood in the middle of it, still faintly white at the hairline, and bowed to each of them in turn, very correctly, as if they'd all three been at a dance.

"Thank you, Miss Sara. Thank you, Miss—" a hesitation, while he decided what he was allowed to call her— "Trice. I'm sorry about your shoulder."

"I left it open, Master Baker."

"Yes," he said, "but I hit it."

He went off toward the dormitory, walking a little stiffly and talking to himself again. Trice heard "air" and "line" and "turn it, not stop it," and then he was through the arch and gone.

She sat down on the bench by the water butt. Sara sat beside her.

The account, as far as Trice could total it, came out roughly even, which was itself a loss. She had come into the yard expecting a scholarship boy who'd be grateful for a lesson. She was leaving with a list, a sore shoulder, a question she couldn't put a price on, and a strong sense of owing something to somebody, which she disliked very much.

"Who benefits," she said, half to herself, "from making a boy like that run ten miles?"

"He ran more than ten," said Sara. "Rolph said there was a mill."

They sat with that for a while. Neither of them said anything about the sand.

* * *

He came back with the liniment and found Tor at the desk, which was the least surprising thing that had happened all day.

There was still white in Tor's hair. There was white in his eyebrows and in the creases of his neck, and a fine white tidemark on the collar of his tunic where it had been wet and dried. He had two sheets of paper in front of him. The left-hand one was the food-drying field, nearly finished: the familiar cramped lines, the sigil drawn and redrawn, a column of small numbers down the side that Rolph understood were about apples. The right-hand one was new, and nearly empty. At the top of it Tor had written AIR and underlined it three times, so hard the pen had gone through.

"Shirt off," Rolph said. "I promised."

"You don't have to—"

"I said I'd rub liniment on your back and you'd apologize the whole time, and I keep my promises. Off."

Tor took off his tunic without getting up. His back was a map of the afternoon: a long red stripe across the shoulder blades, a yellowing patch on his ribs the size of a heel, a neat round bruise on the upper arm that Rolph thought was probably Sara's thumb. Rolph uncorked the bottle. The smell of camphor and something green filled the room.

"So," he said. "You lived."

"For now." Tor dipped his pen. "Two weeks."

"He's not actually going to kill you."

"He said twenty minutes, Rolph."

"He says lots of things."

"He cut the pell in half."

Rolph opened his mouth, and found that he had nothing to put in it, and shut it again. He put liniment on the stripe across Tor's shoulders instead.

"Sorry," Tor said. "Ow. Sorry."

"There it is."

"Did you see the—at the end, with Trice—"

"I saw the cloud from the far end of the yard. I thought you'd exploded." Rolph worked the liniment in. "Half the second-years are saying you've got a shield sewn into your drawers. The other half are saying you don't, and they're the ones who are worried." He paused. "Did you mean to do that? The push. With the sand."

Tor thought about it, the pen hovering. "I think so," he said. "It seemed like the thing to do. Is that—was it wrong?"

Rolph's hands were still on Tor's back. They stayed there a moment longer than they needed to.

"No," Rolph said. "Not wrong." He picked the bottle up again. "Do you know how many people in this school have been beaten by both of those two in one afternoon? It's a very short list. You should be proud."

"I'm not sure it's the sort of list one's proud of."

"It's a list with you at the top of it. That's a start."

Tor didn't answer. He'd finished the last line on the left-hand sheet. He blotted it, looked at it for a moment, and set it carefully to one side, squared to the edge of the desk, done. Then he drew the right-hand sheet toward him, under AIR, and wrote a single word: *Dust*. He drew a line between the two, and then a little gate in the line, and then crossed the gate out.

"Air but not dust," he said, to the paper. "A knife's a line. Turn it, don't stop it."

Rolph put liniment on the heel-print on Tor's ribs. Tor said, "Ow, sorry," without looking up, and kept writing.

* * *

The building room was the long one on the north side, where the light came in flat and even all morning and the benches were scarred by forty years of other people's mistakes. The morning after the sand, twenty of them sat at it in two rows, copying lamp-squares. It was the second week of lamp-squares. There would be a third.

Dorgal had laid out his three pens in a row and was using the middle one. Across the aisle and one bench down, Baker was using whatever pen had come to hand, and was holding it, Dorgal noticed, rather carefully, because there was a bruise on the back of that hand the size and color of a plum. He moved like an old man when he reached for the ink. He had moved like an old man coming in, too, and two of the second-years at the back had made a little show of flinching away from him, and Baker had apologized to them.

The whole school had heard about the sand by now. Dorgal had heard it four times before breakfast, each version larger. In the last one Trice had been thrown over the wall.

"Interesting," he'd said, each time.

He bent over his square. The sigil for a lamp-square was simple, a circle, a cross, a little hooked line for the light to come out of, and the trick of it was doing the simple thing perfectly forty times without once getting bored. Dorgal was very good at not getting bored. He'd been trained to it.

The door opened. A porter in the school's gray came in, went to the building master's desk, and gave him a card.

The building master read it. He was a long gray folded sort of man, and he read the way he inspected work, holding the card up to the window and tipping it so the light ran across it. Dorgal, who had very good eyes, saw the card catch the light. It was heavy and cream-colored, and pressed into one corner was a mark he could have drawn in the dark: a ring of knotted rope with a letter inside it.

He looked down at his square.

The building master put the card in his apron. He looked out of the window for a moment, down at the court. Then he said, "Baker. With me."

Baker's head came up. He had the face of a boy who has been called to the front of a room many times and never once for anything good. He put his pen down, wiped it, put it down again in a slightly better place, and stood, carefully.

"Off to be dunked again, Baker?" said Dorgal, without looking up. "Do try to float this time."

Somebody laughed. Baker said, "I'll try," quite seriously, and followed the building master out.

Dorgal finished his circle. He started on the cross. On the second stroke the nib of the middle pen split with a small, clean sound and put a blot the size of a fingernail in the middle of the square.

He looked at it for a while. Then he set the pen down at the end of the row and took up the left-hand one.

From where he sat he could see a slice of the court through the window, between the frame and the corner of the dormitory. There was a carriage in it. It was black and plain and very well kept, with a pair of grays in the traces, and on the door, small, the same knotted ring. The coachman was standing at the horses' heads, a broad man in a brown coat, talking to one of them.

Abel had grown a beard, Dorgal noticed. It didn't suit him.

He went back to his square. He started a new one. He didn't look at the window again for almost a quarter of a bell, which was, he felt, a creditable effort.

* * *

The boy stopped two steps inside the door.

That was the first thing Douglas Sorvey noticed about him, and it was the most interesting thing he'd see all morning. Most people came all the way into a room. They came in until something stopped them: a chair, a desk, a person. The boy had stopped where nothing stopped him, with the door still close behind him and the building master's shoulder half in the way, and he'd stopped there as if he'd walked up to the edge of something. Sorvey had seen men stop like that. Men who'd been in rooms with him before. He'd never seen a boy do it on the first try.

He was smaller than he'd been described. He'd been described at length, over two dinners.

"Sit down, Scholar Baker," Sorvey said, and smiled.

He was in the building master's chair, because the building master had offered it to him, and because it faced the door. There was one other chair. The boy looked at it, then at the master, who nodded. The boy sat on the front third of it with his hands on his knees. There was a bruise on one hand like a plum.

"Sir," the boy said. "Good morning, sir."

"Good morning. My name's Sorvey. I buy things." He let that sit. "I'm told you've made a thing that dries laundry."

"Yes, sir. It's not really anything. It's only a field for cloth, it lifts the water out, it's on pine, the pine's not very good—"

"I've seen it," said Sorvey. He had; the master had shown it to him that morning, on the desk, in the clean light. He had held it in his hand and dried his own handkerchief with it, twice, and then he had put it down and said nothing for some time. "It's on pine. It works. I'd like as many as you can make." He took out a small purse and set it on the desk, not open, just present. "Two gold each, Scholar Baker. I'll send the boards and the paint. I'll send a cart for them when they're done. You'll never have to carry one anywhere or speak to anyone about it. You build them, I take them, you're paid."

He watched the boy hear *two gold*.

It went into him like water into dry ground. His eyes went to the purse and away again, fast, as though looking at it were rude. Sorvey had expected that, more or less. A baker's son on a king's charity, the master had said. Two gold was a month of bread in the woods. What he hadn't expected was the next thing: the boy's hands tightening on his knees, and his shoulders coming a quarter-inch forward, the way a man's do when he's decided to get up and leave and hasn't told his legs yet.

He wasn't going to take it. He thought it was a fortune, and he wasn't going to take it.

"That's very generous, sir," the boy said.

"It is."

"Only, I'm afraid I—" The boy stopped. He looked at the building master, who looked at the window. He looked at Sorvey's hands, which were resting on the desk, and stayed on them a moment. Sorvey didn't move them. "I've already got someone," the boy said. "To do that. Represent me. Miss Sara is. Representing me. In this." A pause, short and dreadful. "I think."

"You think," said Sorvey.

"She is. Sir." Then, as if he were checking the sum after he'd written it down: "She priced it, at the laundry, and Miss Trice said—and then they—yes. Miss Sara Debri. She is."

Sorvey looked at him for a while. The boy looked back, very politely, for as long as he could, which was longer than most grown men managed, and then looked at the desk.

"Debri," Sorvey said.

The building master shifted his weight by the window. Sorvey didn't look around at him.

Debri's girl would have told him a figure, at that laundry. Sorvey could guess the figure, give or take. And the boy had heard it, and heard two, and called two *generous*, and still sat on the front of his chair with his hands tight on his knees, saying no. Not for the money. Sorvey turned that over and found that he liked it, which was a rare and slightly unpleasant feeling.

"Then I won't tread on the Debris' toes," he said. "Not over laundry." He picked up the purse and put it back inside his coat. "But I'll tell you what I'll do, Scholar Baker. Anything new you make, anything at all, whether it dries a shirt or lights a lamp or does something nobody's thought of, I'll pay for the making of it. Before it's made, if you like. You tell me what you want to try, and I'll pay for the trying." He smiled again. "Failures included."

"Thank you, sir," the boy said. He said it the way you'd say it to a large dog that had decided, for now, not to bite.

Sorvey took a card from his waistcoat and held it out across the desk. Heavy, cream, the knotted ring in the corner. The boy took it by the very edges, the way you'd hold a plate that had come out of the oven, and didn't look at it.

"Keep it somewhere you'll find it," Sorvey said. He stood. He was not especially tall, but he took up the room when he stood, and the boy was on his feet a beat later without seeming to have decided to be. "Master. Thank you for the use of your chair."

The building master said nothing at all. Sorvey shook his hand anyway, and found it stiff.

At the door he stopped beside the boy, close, and the boy stood very still.

"You've a good eye for a room, Scholar Baker," he said pleasantly. "Who taught you that?"

"Nobody, sir," said the boy. "Sorry, sir."

Sorvey nodded, as if that were the answer he'd expected, and went out, pulling on his gloves.

* * *

He was four days late with the lamp-squares now, and he'd thought, wrongly, that the building master's door would be the quietest place in the school to wait.

The door opened and a man came out. He was pulling on gloves, gray ones, a finger at a time, and he wasn't tall, and there was nothing whatever about him to be frightened of. The first-year was frightened of him at once and completely, the way a rabbit is frightened of a shadow. The man looked down at him on the bench.

"Good morning," the man said.

The first-year's mouth opened. Nothing came out of it.

The man smiled, as if that were a perfectly good answer, and went off down the corridor at an easy walk. His boots made no noise on the boards, which the first-year noticed, and then wished he hadn't.

He had left the door ajar. Through it, the first-year could see a slice of the office: the end of the desk, the window, the building master's back, and the scholarship boy, Baker, standing with something white held flat between his fingertips.

"That was Sorvey," the building master said.

"Yes, sir. He seemed very—" Baker stopped. "He seemed very sure, sir."

"He is a buyer, Baker. One of the biggest in the north. He came here because I asked him to." There was nothing in the master's voice at all, which, the first-year had learned in the washing house, was where the master kept the most. "And you've sent him away."

"I'm sorry, sir. I didn't mean to be ungrateful. Only, Miss Sara—"

"Yes. Debri." A pause. The master turned from the window. The first-year could see his face now, long and gray and closed. "You've a great deal to learn about the trade, Baker. Let me tell you one piece of it for nothing." He picked up something from the desk, put it down again. "When a builder's piece goes through testing, it is not unusual for the builder to remember the testing. Testing is work. Whoever tests a piece puts his name to it. It wouldn't be a bad idea, when your money comes in, to see that he's not forgotten."

On the bench, the first-year put both hands over his mouth.

"Oh," said Baker. He sounded, of all things, relieved. "Oh, of course, sir. That's only fair. I hadn't thought. I'm sorry, I should have thought of it myself, testing's ever so much work." He nodded several times. "Who does the testing, sir? I'll go and find him. I'll ask Miss Sara how much is usual."

There was a silence in the office.

It went on for some time. The first-year, who could see the building master's face, watched something happen in it that he would never be able to describe afterward, though he tried, that night, to three people. It wasn't anger. It might have been something like pain. It might, very possibly, have been the beginning of a laugh, strangled at birth.

"Go back to class, Baker," the building master said.

"Yes, sir. Thank you, sir. Sorry, sir."

Baker came out. He nearly walked into the bench, and stopped, and his face lit with recognition. "Oh, hello! It's you. Are you late again?"

"He was asking you for a—" the first-year began, and stopped, because the boy was looking at him with such open, interested courtesy that the rest of the sentence simply died.

"For a what?"

"Nothing."

"He's very fair, I think," Baker said. "Don't you? He thinks of things." He looked down at the card between his fingertips, and for the first time seemed to see it: the cream, the weight, the little knotted ring in the corner. His face did something complicated. He put the card into his tunic, carefully, at arm's length, the way you'd put away something that might go off. "Good luck with your squares."

He went off up the corridor toward the north room, walking stiffly, like an old man.

The first-year sat on the bench. After a while, the building master appeared in the doorway and held out his hand without a word, and the first-year gave him the lamp-squares. The master looked at the top one, tipped it to the light, and handed it back.

"Again," he said. "The cross is crooked."

He shut the door.

* * *

Baker had gone out at the first bell after lunch and he came back with the sun in the gate.

Petra was the only one still in the yard, because Kolb had kept her back to work on her guard, and because, if she was honest, she'd wanted to see whether he came back at all. Thirty miles. Kolb had said it the way he said everything, as if it were the weather, and the whole yard had gone quiet, and Baker had said, "Yes, sir," and gone. Three times to Hollin and back. Somebody had laid a bet he'd be found asleep in a ditch. Somebody else had laid a bet he'd keep going and never stop, all the way home to wherever it was he came from, and Petra had thought that was unkind, and also not impossible.

He came through the gate at a jog that was barely a jog. His face was gray with dust, except where sweat had cut lines down it, and his tunic had gone dark and then dry and then dark again, in rings, like a tree. He stopped in front of Kolb and stood there swaying slightly.

"Sir."

"Baker." Kolb looked at him for a moment. Then he picked up something that had been leaning against the rack and held it out.

It was an iron bar, the length of a waster and the thickness of a broom handle. Petra had lifted it once, in her first year, for a dare. She'd put it down again quickly.

"The pell," Kolb said. "Until you can't."

The pell was new, a fresh trunk sunk in yesterday's hole, still pale and smelling of sap. Baker took the bar. His arms dropped a few inches when its weight came on them, then came back up. He walked to the pell and set his feet, and hit it.

It was a terrible blow. It didn't shake anything. The bar bounced off the wood with a sound like a dropped pot, and Baker's whole body rang with it, she could see it go through him, and he set his feet again and hit it again.

She lost count somewhere past sixty. The blows got slower, and lower. Somewhere in there Baker started talking, under his breath, and she drifted near enough to hear it, pretending to fix her bootlace. It wasn't complaining. It was about weight, and lines, and something being spread, and air. He'd hit the pell and say a word, and hit it and say another word, like a man reciting a lesson with a hammer.

The bar came down on the sand. He bent for it. He got it as high as his knees, and his arms stopped. They didn't drop it; they simply stopped, and stayed there, trembling, and he looked down at them with an expression of polite surprise, as though they belonged to somebody he'd only just met.

"Enough," said Kolb, from the gate. He'd been watching. Petra hadn't seen him come back. "Go and eat, Baker."

He went. Petra caught up with him at the arch, and took the iron bar out of his hands, because he was still holding it and didn't seem to know how to let go.

"Oh," he said. "Thank you. That's very kind. Thank you, um—"

He looked at her face with enormous, apologetic effort, the way you'd look at a word on the tip of your tongue.

"Petra," said Petra.

"Petra. Yes. Sorry. I knew that."

He hadn't. He never did. She'd beaten him in the yard eleven times since his first year and he'd thanked her by name for none of them. She found, as she always did, that she didn't mind; it was hard to mind, when he was so sorry about it. She smiled at him, and he blinked, as if that had surprised him too, and went off toward the dormitory with his arms hanging at his sides like a pair of wet sleeves.

* * *

They'd changed for it, which in hindsight had been a waste of an hour.

Trice had taken her hair down, which she did perhaps twice a term and never without a reason. Sara was in green, with sleeves too thin for the dormitory corridors, and had spent ten minutes deciding which of three combs. A second-year coming up the stairs had looked at them twice and missed a step. It was, Trice had judged at the time, a reasonable return on the investment.

Baker opened the door with his elbow.

He couldn't use his hands, it turned out. He held them up to show them, a little sheepishly, both of them trembling like leaves. "Sorry," he said. "Kolb. The pell. I can't quite— Please come in. Sorry. It's a mess."

It was. The desk was a drift of paper. The bed was a drift of paper. On the floor by the desk, a sheet with AIR at the top had been weighted down with a boot. There was a tray with a teapot on it, and a cup, and Baker had evidently been trying to drink from the cup by bending down to it, because there was a ring of tea on the desk and a damp patch on his collar.

He looked at them standing in his doorway. Trice watched him take them in: the hair, the green, the combs. His face cleared with understanding.

"Oh," he said. "Are you going somewhere nice? I'm sorry, I won't keep you."

"We came here," said Trice.

"Oh! Before, you mean." He smiled, pleased to have worked it out. "That's very good of you. Please, sit. Anywhere. Not there, that's the food field. There. Sorry."

Trice sat. She did not look at Sara, because if she looked at Sara one of them would laugh, and it would not be Sara.

Sara poured the tea. She did it without asking, the way she did things, and held the cup for him while he drank, and he said "thank you" into it twice, and she said, "Tor. When did you last eat?" and he said, "Lunch, I think," and Trice could see Sara deciding not to ask which day's.

"You had a visitor this morning," Trice said.

He looked surprised. "How did you—"

"Everybody knows. There's a first-year who's told it to half the school." She folded her hands. "Douglas Sorvey offered you two gold a board for the laundry field, and you told him Sara represents you."

He went red, from the collar up. "I'm sorry. I shouldn't have said it without asking. Only, at the laundry, when Miss Sara said thirty, and then you said— I thought it was sort of— and he was very—" He stopped. "I'm sorry," he said again, to Sara this time.

"Don't be," said Sara. "You did exactly right."

"Did I?"

"Who benefits, Master Baker," said Trice, "from a man paying two gold for a thing worth thirty?"

He thought about it, honestly, the way he thought about everything. "Him," he said.

"Him. And from paying for your *failures*?" She'd heard that part too. The first-year had been very thorough. "He'll pay for every failure you ever make, and then he'll own every failure you ever make, and the successes that come after them, and you'll find one morning that there's a carriage in the court for you, and it's not taking you anywhere you'd have chosen. That's how the Sorveys buy. They buy early, and then they own the stable."

"He did seem," said Baker slowly, "very sure."

"He's always very sure," said Trice. "It's his best quality."

Sara put the cup down. She'd let Trice have the politics; now it was hers. "Twelve gold," she said. "Each. That's what the Debri house will pay you, for as many as you can make. And twenty if you can get it onto a metal plate."

Baker made a sound that wasn't a word.

"Twelve," he said. "He said two, and I thought two was—"

"I know what he said." Sara's voice was quite gentle. "Pine warps, Tor. Paint flakes. It's a beautiful field and it's on a board that'll be firewood in five years, and the people who buy fields like this know it, and that's why it's thirty and not three hundred. Put it on copper, or brass, anything that won't warp, and it lasts. Lasting is what people pay for."

He wasn't looking at her anymore. He was looking past her, at the lamp on the desk, at its brass foot, and his lips were moving. Trice had seen him do that in the yard yesterday with a knife against his neck. She'd seen it on the sand, coughing up powder. She was beginning to know what it meant.

"You'd have to etch it," he said, to the lamp. "Paint won't sit on metal, not properly. You'd etch it, with acid. The store has— I'd need to think about the depth of the line—" He stopped. He looked at Sara. "Sorry. Yes. Twenty. That's— yes."

"Good," said Sara.

Then something crossed his face, and stayed.

"Only," he said, "I might not be able to. To make very many. Not past two weeks." He looked at his hands, still trembling on his knees. "Kolb's set me a shield. It has to stop his blows and a cutter, for twenty minutes, with me inside it. And if it doesn't—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. "I'd hate to promise the house a lot of boards and then be— not able to deliver them."

Trice looked at him for a long moment. Of all the things she'd come prepared for tonight, a scholarship boy apologizing in advance for the inconvenience of his own death had not been on the list.

"Master Baker," she said. "We have every confidence in you. As it happens, we heard Kolb discussing you with the head of our department yesterday, after the bell. He said you'd manage it. He said he wouldn't have set it otherwise."

"Your department?"

"Mm," said Trice. "And besides, the school doesn't kill students. Hardly ever. They're paid by the head."

He looked at her with wide, grateful eyes.

"Oh," he said. "That's— thank you. That's very reassuring." He meant it. She could see that he meant it. He thought about it a moment longer, and his face cleared further. "So he only said it to make me work harder. So that I'd take it seriously."

"Yes," said Sara.

"That's very clever of him." A pause. "He did cut the pell in half, though."

Nobody had anything to say to that.

"There's one more thing," Sara said, after a moment, in a slightly different voice, the voice her father used, Trice suspected, when a contract was about to be real. "If I'm to represent you. I'd do the selling, and the carting, and the arguing, and you'd do the building and nothing else. And the split would be ten in the hundred."

"Ten," said Baker.

"Ten in the hundred. On everything sold."

He thought about it. His lips moved, briefly. "Is that usual?"

"For a first contract it's usual."

"Then yes," he said, and smiled at her, a real smile, wide and relieved. "Yes. Thank you. That's very kind."

Trice looked at Sara. Sara was looking at Baker, and didn't look back.

"Good," Sara said. "Then that's done." She stood, and so did Trice, and Baker got up so fast he knocked the boot off the AIR sheet. "I'd give you a card," Sara said. She made a small gesture down at the green, which had no pockets, and nowhere about it that would hold so much as a pin. "But. If you need us, ask Rolph. Or Tovey. Either of them can find us."

Baker laughed politely. It was a very small laugh, of the kind people give when they've been told a joke in a language they only half speak.

"Goodnight, Master Baker," said Trice.

"Goodnight. Thank you. Thank you for coming. Before your— before you go wherever you're going. I hope it's nice."

The door closed behind them. The bar dropped into place on the other side with a soft wooden knock.

* * *

She was at the top of the stairs before she realized she'd left her comb on his bed.

"Go on," she told Trice. "I'll catch you."

The corridor was dark between the lamps, and cold, and her sleeves were, as Trice had told her they would be, too thin for it. She went back along it quietly, because it was late and because she always went quietly, and she had her hand up to knock when she heard him through the door.

He was talking to himself. He did that; she'd noticed it in the yard. It wasn't loud.

"Ask Tovey," he was saying. "Ask Count Thompson. To go and *fetch* them. For me." A small breath of a laugh. "Very good. That's very good." Then, more quietly, and not as if it were funny at all: "I suppose I deserved that."

Sara stood with her hand raised.

The pen started, on the other side of the door. Scratch, and scratch. "Copper," he said. "Not pine. Copper, acid, the depth of the line. Air but not dust. Twelve, twenty." A pause. "Ten in the hundred." Another pause. "Very kind."

She lowered her hand. She didn't knock. She went back down the corridor, and down the stairs, and found Trice waiting at the bottom with her arms folded against the cold.

"Where's your comb?"

"I'll get it tomorrow." Sara put her arm through Trice's, for the warmth. "He thinks we were joking. About Tovey."

"Of course he does."

"He thinks Tovey would be insulted to be asked."

"Tovey would be delighted to be asked. He'd go and get us himself. He'd bring a lantern." Trice walked a few steps. "And Rolph?"

Sara thought about it.

"He didn't even hear Rolph," she said.

They went out into the court. The bell tower stood up black against the stars. Behind them, on the top floor of the dormitory, one window was still lit, and stayed lit, long after they'd stopped looking back at it.

* * *

The Barrow had been loud, and Rolph had been louder, and he came up the dormitory stairs with the pleasant certainty of a man who has been right in public several times in one evening.

The bar lifted for him before he touched the door. Tor was at the desk. Of course he was at the desk. The lamp was turned low, and there was paper everywhere, and Tor's hands were lying on top of it like two things somebody had left there.

"Little Tor!" Rolph shut the door with his heel. "You're famous."

"I'm not."

"You're famous in the Barrow, which is the only place that counts. Do you know what I heard tonight? I heard a second-year from House Calder tell a girl from the town that your laundry board can dry a cloak in the rain while you're wearing it. I heard a fifth-year say his father would pay forty for one. Forty! I heard Tovey's lot at the end table arguing about whether you'd sell to them first, on account of Tovey asking first, which is a point of precedence I'd have given good money to watch them settle." He dropped onto his own bed, which creaked. "Everybody wants one. Everybody."

Tor said nothing. He was looking at his hands.

"What's the matter with your hands?"

"Kolb. The pell. With an iron bar. Until I couldn't." Tor flexed his fingers, slowly. They worked, more or less. "It's coming back."

"Good. Because here's the thing." Rolph sat up. He'd been thinking about this all the way up the hill, and in the way of things thought about up a hill after the Barrow, it had grown very fine. "Mother. Mother has to have them first. Not one: ten. Ten boards, so she can give them out to her friends, and her friends have friends, and inside a month every great house in the—everyone who matters will have seen one in Mother's hands first, and the rest of them will be breaking down Sara's door. You understand? It's the best thing that could happen to you. It's practically a favor."

"I haven't got anything to make them on," said Tor.

"You've got pine."

"Sara says pine warps." Tor said it with an air of grievance that Rolph had never heard from him before, and it took Rolph a moment to recognize it, because it was so unlike him: he was sulking. Or he was doing an impression of somebody sulking, a very poor one, with his chin down and his mouth turned in. "Sara says pine's firewood in five years. Sara says it has to be metal. I haven't got any metal, Rolph. I haven't got any acid. I've got a cup of cold tea that I can't lift."

"Then buy metal."

"With what?"

Rolph got up. He went to the chest at the foot of his bed, the lockbox, the one whose lock Tor had copied for their door, and laid his hand flat on the lid, and it clicked. He took out a purse. He counted coins into his palm, gold, while Tor watched with an expression of rising alarm.

"Fifty-five," Rolph said. He put them in a little stack on the desk, next to Tor's useless hands. "Half up front. That's how it's done."

"Half of *what*?"

"Of a hundred and ten. Eleven a board. I heard Debri's paying you twelve, and I'm your friend, so I get the friend price."

Tor looked at the stack. "That's one gold off for friendship."

"Exactly."

"That seems backward."

"It's how friendship works among merchants," said Rolph, with dignity. "You wouldn't understand."

Tor's mouth twitched. He put it back into its sulk at once, but Rolph had seen it.

"I'd need them soon," Rolph said, more seriously. "That's the only thing. It's Mother's birthday on fifth day next week, and the express to the capital takes three days, and it goes when it goes, and I want them on it."

Tor looked up. "Her birthday's fifth day? Like the queen's?"

"Just like the queen's," Rolph said, and laughed.

"Do we get that day off? For the queen's?"

"Not a chance," said Rolph. "You'd have to ask Kolb." He laughed again, a little longer than the joke was worth, and lay back down on his bed with his hands behind his head and looked at the ceiling. "Anyway. No hurry. Any time before the express. Tomorrow. The day after."

He heard the chair scrape.

When he looked, Tor was on his feet, and he had the stack of gold in both hands, held against his chest the way he'd hold a loaf, and he was already looking at the door. His face was still arranged in its sulk. His eyes had gone somewhere else entirely: to a place, Rolph knew, where a hundred copper squares were lying in rows.

"The store closes at the ninth bell," Rolph said.

"I know," said Tor. "I'll knock."

* * *

He'd been asleep an hour when the knocking started, and he lay and listened to it for some time in the hope that it was a dream.

It wasn't. It was polite. It was the politest knocking he'd heard in thirty-one years behind the counter of the Lairdgren student store: three soft taps, a long pause, three more, as if whoever was doing it were sorry about every one of them. He got up, and put on his slippers, and put the nightcap straight, and went through the dark shop with his candle muttering things about students that he'd have been fired for saying in daylight.

It was the little one. The baker's boy from the woods, on the king's money, who came in once a month for soap and ink and the cheapest pine and counted his coppers out on the counter twice over to be sure. He stood on the step in the cold with his hands full.

"I'm terribly sorry," the boy said. "I know you're closed. I'm so sorry. Only, I need some copper squares. And some acid."

"It's past the ninth bell."

"Yes. I'm sorry."

"Copper's dear," the storekeeper said. He said it the way you'd tell a child the river was deep. "Five-inch squares. Three copper a square, and it's not cheap acid, either, the good stuff, for etching. Come back in the morning with your—"

"How many have you got?"

The storekeeper looked at him. "Squares? A crate. A hundred, near enough."

"I'll take them all," said the boy. "And enough acid for a hundred. And a basin, if you've got one that won't mind. Sorry." And he held out his hands, and opened them, and there was gold in them.

Not a coin. A heap. The candlelight ran over it like water.

The storekeeper had a moment, standing in his doorway in his nightcap, of considering several possibilities, all of them unworthy and most of them involving the watch. Then he looked at the boy's face again, which was the most honest face he'd seen on a Lairdgren student in thirty-one years, and was apologizing, and he let them go.

"Come in," he said. "Watch the step."

He sold him the crate. He sold him the acid, two stone jars of it, stoppered and waxed, and a lead-lined basin, and a pair of tongs, and some rags, and, because the boy asked anxiously whether the acid would hurt his hands, a pair of oiled gloves that he'd been trying to get rid of for six years. The boy paid in gold and waited, apologizing, while the storekeeper made change in silver and copper and counted it twice, as the boy did, out of a sort of fellow-feeling.

The crate was heavier than the boy. He got it onto his shoulder anyway, with the jars in a sling across his back, and went off across the court in the dark, very slowly, listing to one side like a small overloaded ship.

The storekeeper shut the door and stood in his shop for a while, holding his candle. Then he went and looked at the gold in the till, to be sure it was still there. It was.

He went back to bed. He didn't sleep for some time.

* * *

The smell woke him. Or he hadn't been asleep; he'd been lying in the half-sleep of a man who has had three ales and a triumph, listening to the scrape of a crate and the clink of jars and Tor saying sorry to the door frame. Then the smell came, sharp and sour and green, like a copper kettle left to boil dry, and Rolph sat up with his eyes watering.

Tor had opened the window. He had the basin on the sill and the gloves on his hands, which were enormous on him, and he was lowering a square of copper into the basin with the tongs. The lamp stood beside it. In its light the copper was the color of a new coin, and where Tor had drawn on it with something waxy, the lines stayed bright, and everywhere else, as Rolph watched, the metal began very faintly to fizz.

"What is that?"

"Acid. Sorry. I've got the window open."

"I can see that. I can *taste* that."

"It's only for the lines." Tor lifted the square out, turned it to the lamp, and looked at it with his head on one side, the way Rolph had seen him look at bread when he talked about home. Then he rinsed it in the jug and set it on a rag with the others. There were a lot of others. They lay in rows across the floor between the beds, in ranks of ten, each one with the drying field bitten into it in fine dark lines, a smell coming off them like a cut penny.

"How many have you done?"

"Seventy-two," said Tor. "Seventy-three."

Rolph got out of bed and walked carefully between the rows to the window. The night outside was clear and cold, and the bell tower stood up black over the court, and somewhere down the hill a dog was barking at the stars. He looked down at the squares. Even he could see that some were better than others. Most of them were clean, the lines crisp and even. A few weren't. On one, the acid had crept under the wax and the line had gone wide and furry. On another, a corner of the pattern had simply not taken.

Tor had made a separate pile of those. Small. Five or six.

"What about these?"

"Those are crooked." Tor didn't look around. "They'll work. They're just crooked. I'll give those away."

"Give them—Tor, those are *eleven gold*—"

"They're crooked," Tor said, patiently, as if that settled it. "Davie can have one. He can't keep his shirts dry, he's got no idea, it's awful. And the washing house should have one, for the—for the people who do the washing, the real washing, the sheets. And Hob, in the kitchens, his mother takes in laundry in Hollin. And the other scholarship boys in the east wing. They can share." He lowered another square into the basin. "Seventy-four."

Rolph opened his mouth to say several things about trade, and about how a field didn't stop being worth eleven gold because one of its lines was furry, and about how people who'd never had to count coppers on a counter were the only ones who could afford to be careless about crooked things.

He closed it again. He sat down on the end of his bed instead, in the cold, in the smell, and watched.

At some point he must have lain down. When he opened his eyes the lamp had moved. All the squares were gone from the floor but a few rows, and Tor was crouched by the door, putting the rest into a sack. He did it by tens. He counted under his breath. Fifty went in the sack. Fifty stayed in a neat stack on the desk, squared to the edge.

"Where are you going?"

"Workroom." Tor stood up, and the sack clanked. "You need to sleep. And the fumes. I'll do half there and half here tomorrow. Yours'll be in the first ten. I'll do yours first."

"Tor, it's the middle of the night."

"Yes," said Tor. "Sorry." And then, at the door, with the sack on his shoulder and his face still with, somewhere in it, the remains of that sulk he'd been wearing for Rolph's benefit three hours ago: "Fifty-five gold. Honestly, Rolph."

The bar dropped behind him.

Rolph lay in the dark with the window still open and the cold coming in, and the smell of acid and copper, and thought about his mother opening ten boards on her birthday, and the faces of the women around her. He thought about Tor kneeling between the rows on the floor, sorting out the crooked ones for a boy in the kitchens whose mother took in washing.

He didn't get back to sleep for some time either.

* * *

The workroom on the east stair was the only one with a stove that drew, and at this hour it was always empty, which was why Dorgal used it.

He had a board in front of him, pine, painted. He'd painted it from memory. He had a very good memory, and he'd stood in the washing house two days ago and looked at Baker's square for as long as he'd looked at anything in his life, and then he'd gone away and drawn it eleven times, and this was the eleventh. It was perfect. He'd checked every line against his notes. The proportions were right. The hook at the lower left, the doubled crossing near the center, the thing like a fishbone along the top edge: all right.

It did nothing.

He'd sat with it for two bells. He'd done everything he'd been taught to do. He'd built the idea in his mind the way the masters said, clear and whole, and held it, and pressed it into the board through the sigil the way you pressed a seal into wax. He'd felt the trance come and the work go out of him. And then he'd wet a rag and laid it on the board, and the rag had stayed wet.

He was looking at it, wet, when the door opened.

He had the cloth over the board before he'd thought about it. It was a reflex he was not proud of.

"Oh!" Baker stopped in the doorway with a sack on his shoulder that clanked. "Sorry. I didn't think anyone would—I'm so sorry. Do you mind if I—"

"My dear Baker," said Dorgal. "It's a workroom. Work."

Baker came in. He put the sack down by the stove with enormous care, as if it might wake, and drew up the other stool to the far end of the bench, and began laying things out of it in a row. Squares. Copper. Ten of them, side by side, each with the pattern etched into it in fine dark lines. Dorgal could see the hook at the lower left from where he sat, and the doubled crossing, and the fishbone.

"I heard you had a visitor this morning," Dorgal said.

"Yes! Mr. Sorvey." Baker looked up, pleased and a little rueful, the face of a man remembering a meeting that had gone better than he'd feared. Nothing else in it at all. "Do you know him?"

There was a pause.

"Everyone knows him," Dorgal said.

"He frightened me a bit, honestly." Baker set the tenth square straight. "Sorry. I expect he's very nice."

"He isn't," said Dorgal. "Particularly."

Baker looked at him for a moment, curious, and then, being Baker, seemed to decide it wasn't his business, and went back to his squares.

Dorgal watched him start.

He didn't know what he'd expected. Something like what he'd been doing himself for two bells: the stillness, the long breath, the slow press. Baker put his hand flat over the first square, not touching it, and closed his eyes partway, and his lips moved. Then he laid one finger on the etched line at the lower left and began to trace it. He traced the hook, and the crossing, and the fishbone, slowly, the way a child traces letters. Halfway along the fishbone his finger came off the line altogether, and wandered, and stopped on bare copper, and he didn't seem to notice, and went on with his lips moving and his finger resting on nothing.

After a while he opened his eyes and put the square to one side and took the next.

Dorgal looked at the first one. He reached over, when Baker's eyes were closed again, and laid the back of his hand against the copper. It was cool. That was all.

Then he wet the corner of his own rag at the jug and laid it across Baker's square, and watched the water come up out of the cloth in a hundred tiny beads and run off onto the bench.

He took the rag back. He folded it. He put it on his knee.

"Baker," he said. His voice sounded quite normal, which he was proud of. "Your finger came off the line. On the first one."

"Did it?" Baker opened his eyes. "Oh. Probably. I'm tired. It doesn't matter."

"It doesn't *matter*?"

"Not really." Baker looked at him, surprised to be asked. "The sigil's only there to keep my place. And so people know what it's for, after. You could do it on a blank square if you wanted to, it's only harder, you lose track of where you are." He thought about it. "It's like a recipe. You don't need it to make the bread. It just helps if you're doing forty loaves."

There was a long silence in the workroom. The stove ticked.

"Interesting," said Dorgal.

He looked down at the cloth over his own board, and the eleven perfect drawings in his notes, and the two bells, and the years before the two bells.

Baker had gone back to work.

The second batch took longer than the first. Dorgal knew, because he stayed. He'd meant to leave. He found he couldn't leave before Baker did; something to do with pride, he supposed, though it didn't feel like any pride he'd met before. He got out a lamp-square and copied it, badly, for appearances. The tower bell went, and went again. By the third batch Baker was yawning. He yawned in the middle of a square, enormously, and stopped, and looked at the square with deep reproach, and said, "Sorry. Again," and started it over from the hook. He talked to them. Dorgal heard him. "You're the same as the last one, aren't you," Baker told a square, kindly. "Yes. You are. Just like him."

Once, he lost count, and had to lay the whole batch out and count them, and there were nine, and he looked under the bench for a long time before he found the tenth in his own lap.

Some time after the bell that Dorgal didn't count, Baker stood up, and stretched, and his back cracked like a stick. He began putting the squares into the sack by tens. He did it slowly. He kept stopping to flex his hands. When it was done there was one square left on the bench, a little apart from the others. It had a furry line along the fishbone where the acid had crept.

"Would you like that one?" Baker said. "It's crooked. It works, it's just crooked. I'm giving the crooked ones away."

Dorgal looked at the square. Eleven gold, at Debri prices. More at his father's, now that his father had been refused.

"I couldn't possibly," he said.

"It's only crooked."

"Then give it to someone who needs it."

Baker considered him, with that curious, uncritical look, and then nodded as if Dorgal had said something sensible, and put the square in the sack with the others.

"Goodnight," he said. "Thank you for the company. It's much nicer, doing copies with someone there."

He heaved the sack onto his shoulder. It clanked. He went out, and Dorgal heard him on the stair, slow and uneven, one step and then a pause and then the next, as if one of his legs had gone to sleep.

The outer door banged. Footsteps crossed the stones of the court below the window, unevenly, and faded.

Dorgal sat for a while. Then he lifted the cloth off his own board, and looked at it: the perfect hook, the perfect crossing, the perfect fishbone, in good paint on good pine, exactly right in every particular, and doing nothing whatever.

He laid one finger on the line at the lower left. He began, slowly, to trace it.

Then he lifted the finger off, and let it wander.

The rod bucked in her hand, and Tovey went back three steps across the flagstones as if a door had been slammed in his chest.

He didn't fall. She'd known he wouldn't. He stood where it had put him, swaying a little, with his big hands loose at his sides, and the sparks came off him again: off his hair, off the shoulders of his coat, a thin crackling spill of them, blue-white, that lit the stones around his feet and went out. In that light his face was quite empty. Not angry. She'd seen Tovey angry, twice. Angry was a frown, and three cold words, and somebody suddenly finding they had somewhere else to be. This was nothing. This was a face with nobody standing behind it.

How it had started didn't matter now. What mattered was the next thing.

Her fingers were going. That was the next thing. She could feel the rod in her right hand only as a weight at the end of her arm, as if she were holding it through three pairs of gloves. Her left hand was worse. Her legs were worse than that. It came off him the way the sparks did, the numbness, and it was always worst when he looked at her, and he was looking at her now.

She brought the rod up. She couldn't feel her thumb on the catch. She pressed where she thought the catch was, and the rod bucked, and Tovey rocked back a step, and then came forward again, and she backed away and her heel came down on nothing she could feel, and she nearly went over.

Three charges left. Perhaps two. The rod was for getting away from someone, not for holding off something the size of a cart horse that didn't stop.

She kept backing. The court was dark. There was a moon somewhere behind the clouds, enough to make the bell tower a black shape on a dark gray sky, and a lamp at the gate lodge on the far side, a long way off, and no one in it, or no one awake. The dormitory windows were all dark but one. Nobody was coming. She'd known that, too, and had decided some time ago to stop hoping for it.

Movement, at the edge of her eye. Not Tovey.

The door at the foot of the east stair had opened, and someone had come out of it. Someone small. They had a sack over one shoulder, and when they took a step, the sack clanked. Then they stopped, and stood on one leg, and held the other up in front of them and looked at it.

There was exactly one student at Lairdgren that small.

He shook the leg. He put it down, and it didn't seem to land where he'd put it, because he lurched. He said something to it. She couldn't hear the words, but she knew the tone at once, across the whole width of the court: the same tone he'd used to the pell, and to the squares, and to her shoulder. Polite. Disappointed. *Please.* He slapped his own thigh with his free hand, twice, as if he were waking up a dog, and took another step, and lurched again.

He hadn't seen them. He was coming across the court on the diagonal toward the dormitory, very slowly, with his whole attention on his own feet.

*Go to bed, Baker.*

Tovey hit her.

She saw it coming and it didn't help. Her arms came up and weren't her arms. His fist went through them and into her side, and the air left her all at once in a sound she hadn't meant to make, high and sharp, which went right across the court and came back off the wall of the long hall. Her feet went somewhere. She was on one knee. The rod was still in her hand, she thought; she could see it, anyway.

Across the court, the small figure had stopped.

He'd heard. He turned toward the noise. And then, and she could see this quite clearly even in the dark, because she'd watched him do it once already in the yard, yesterday, with Trice's heel coming at him, he turned away from it. He took two lurching steps toward the dormitory door. That was sensible. It was the most sensible thing she'd ever seen him do. Kolb would have been proud.

He stopped.

His head came back around. He stood there, swaying, the sack on his shoulder. She thought, later, that he must have been working it out. A cry in the dark. A girl's voice. *Whose* voice.

"Baker," she said. It came out as a croak. "Run."

He came toward them.

He didn't run, because he couldn't. He came the way a cart comes with one wheel off: forward, and sideways, and forward. His left leg had stopped bending at all. He was holding the neck of the sack in both hands now, because one hand wouldn't close on it. And he was talking. As he got closer she could hear it, low and fast and entirely to himself, in that voice like a boy reciting a lesson.

"—better have a very good reason," he was saying. "Out at this hour. Very good reason. Fighting. At this hour. Miss Trice. Honestly."

Tovey had turned back to her. He was standing over her now. The sparks came off him in a long crackling sheet, blue-white, and in their light she could see the stones around her hands, every crack in them, and her own breath going out in smoke. She brought the rod up. Her arm went halfway and stopped, as though it had run into something. She could feel nothing below the elbow at all. He looked down at her, and it got worse, much worse, a cold going through her like water through cloth, and she thought, very clearly, *well, this is stupid*, and then—

*Clank.*

Tovey didn't move.

*Clank.* The sack swung into his back, just below the shoulders, and the squares inside it slid and chimed against each other like a dropped tray of spoons. Tor stood behind him, a head and a half shorter, with his feet planted wide because that was the only way he could stand, and swung the sack again, two-handed, with his whole body. *Clank.* "Sorry," he said. *Clank.* "Sorry. Leave her alone. Sorry."

Fifty copper squares, Trice thought, distantly. Eleven gold each. Twelve, at Debri prices. Twenty on metal. She found that she wanted, very much, to laugh, and that she couldn't feel her face to do it.

*Clank.*

Tovey turned around.

He did it slowly, the way a big animal turns, the whole body coming around behind the head. And the moment his eyes came off her, the cold in Trice's arms let go a little, an inch, so that she could feel the stones under her palms again, gritty and wet.

The moment his eyes went onto Tor, Tor's knees went.

She saw it. He didn't fall, quite. His legs simply stopped holding him up properly, and he sagged, and the sack dropped from his hands and hit the flagstones with a crash that went all the way around the court. He put out a hand to nothing. He stayed up, swaying, bent forward, looking up.

The clouds moved off the moon.

It wasn't much light. It was enough. It came down gray across the court and onto Tovey's face, the broad jaw, the dark brows, the plain cloak with its brooch; and Trice, from the ground, watched Tor see it.

His mouth opened. It made the shape of the name without any sound. Then his face did a thing she'd seen it do at the laundry, two days ago, when Tovey had walked in with half the school behind him: it went very white and very still and very polite, all at once, as if every fear he'd ever had about the man had just been proved right in a single stroke.

*No,* Trice thought. *That's not—he isn't—*

Tovey looked at him a moment longer. Then he stopped looking at him: his eyes simply went past him, unfocused, the way a man stops looking at a nail just before he brings the hammer down.

And he swung.
