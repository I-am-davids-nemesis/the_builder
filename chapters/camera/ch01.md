# Chapter One

* * *

The tower bell at Lairdgren struck the first hour of the rest day.

The tower stood at the corner of the main courtyard, square and plain, with the bell hung open at the top under a little peaked roof. Its upper courses were gray stone, weathered and patched. Its lowest three were something else: a smooth, seamless gray, without joints or tool marks, like stone that had been poured. There was frost on the courtyard flags and on the stone above. There was none on the bottom three courses.

The bell's echo came back off the chapel roof, a beat behind each stroke. In the dormitory windows round the court, nothing moved. On a line strung between two windows, a row of brown tunics hung stiff with cold.

Under the east range, down six worn steps, a corridor ran the length of the undercroft. Six doors faced onto it, each with a sliding shutter at eye height and a slate hanging on a nail beside it. A woman sat on a stool at the end of the corridor with a shawl round her shoulders and a lump of chalk in her lap. At the bell she got up and walked to the fourth door.

The slate beside it read, in chalk: *BAKER, T. — In: late, fourth day.* Under that ran a long tally of short strokes. She added one. She ran her thumb along them. Her lips moved.

She slid the shutter back.

Inside was a stone cell, whitewashed, with a bench along one wall, a stool in the middle, a high window the size of a loaf, and a lamp burned down to a blue bead of flame. On the bench lay a square of pale pine with a pattern painted on it in blue, and beside it a saucer of the same blue paint and a brush laid across the rim.

On the stool sat a young man. He was small, narrow in the shoulder, and might have been anywhere from fifteen to twenty. He sat upright, hands flat on his thighs, eyes open and fixed on the pine. His face was gray. His chest did not visibly move.

The woman looked at him for a while. Then she looked at the board on the bench. The paint on it had a dull, settled skin, without shine.

"Well, then," she said.

The young man blinked.

He drew a long breath, slow and deep, and let it go. His hands came up off his thighs. He looked down at them, turning them over.

She unbolted the door.

"What bell?" His voice cracked. "I'm sorry. What bell is it, ma'am?"

"First. Rest day."

He stared at her. His lips moved, very slightly.

"Rest day," he said. "Then I've missed—fifth day. All of fifth day." The color went out of his face, what color there was. "Oh, no. Oh, they'll send me home."

The woman said nothing.

He tried to stand. His knees buckled and he caught the edge of the bench with both hands and stayed there, bent, breathing hard. Then he straightened, slowly, in stages. Something in his back cracked audibly.

"Sorry," he said, apparently to his back.

"You'll want feeding."

"Yes, ma'am. Thank you, ma'am. In a moment." He had picked up the board. He held it close to his face and tilted it toward the lamp. He touched one corner lightly with a fingertip and looked at the fingertip. It was clean. He let out a breath that was nearly a laugh.

"It's dry," he said.

"So I see."

"It's not meant to be dry yet." He turned it over, looked at the back, turned it face up. "I don't know if it works. I don't know if it does anything at all. I have to see." He went toward the door with the board held against his chest, in short careful steps. "I need something wet. Laundry. I need laundry. Thank you, ma'am, I'm sorry, thank you."

"Sign the slate."

He came back and took the chalk from her and wrote under her line, his hand unsteady: *Out: first bell, rest day.* He paused, then added: *Thank you.*

He went up the steps into the gray morning. His footsteps crossed the court, uneven and quick, toward the dormitories.

The woman stood in the cell doorway. She looked at the empty stool, then at the saucer of paint. She bent and touched the paint in the saucer with one finger. It was still wet. She looked at her finger, wiped it on her shawl, then went and wiped the slate clean with the heel of her hand.

She climbed the steps toward the smell of bread.

* * *

The washing house stood at the back of the dormitory court, a long low room of wet stone with one pump at the near end and a drain down the middle of the floor. Along the wall stood the tubs: white, glazed inside like dinner plates, each on four iron feet cast in the shape of an animal's paws. A copper steamed in the far corner over a banked fire. It was the rest day, a little after the second bell, and the room smelled of ash and wool and other people's stockings.

There were two students in it.

The nearer was Davie. He had his sleeves rolled to the elbow and his tunic dark with splashes down the front. He pushed a shirt under the water with both hands, swirled it, hauled it out and wrung it, and held it up to the window. It was gray. He dropped it back in the tub and pushed it under again.

Three tubs down, Tor Baker was making foam.

He was small for a third-year, and his brown tunic had gone shiny at the elbows. He rubbed a pale lump against a collar, folded the cloth over on itself, and worked it with his knuckles in short hard strokes. The water in his tub rose white around his wrists, crackling softly. When he pushed it aside, it clung to his forearms.

Davie stopped wringing. He looked at Tor's tub. He looked at his own. The water in it lay flat and gray. He picked up his tub's bucket, walked to the pump, worked the handle, watched the water run clear into the bucket, carried it back, and poured it in. He pushed the shirt under again. He scrubbed it against the side of the tub hard enough to slop water over the rim. Some of it went in his eye. He said a word, not loudly.

Tor looked up.

"Your water," Davie said. He was looking at the wall above Tor's head. "Is that from the near pump? Only mine's come out rather poor."

"It's the same pump, sir." Tor looked at Davie's tub, then at his empty red hands, then at the empty bench beside him. He was quiet for a moment. "Would you like some soap, sir? I've plenty. I bought too much."

He held up the pale lump. It was gray and cracked.

Davie stared at it.

"Is that what that is," he said.

"Yes, sir." Tor came over, wiping his hands on his tunic. "You wet it and rub it into the dirty parts. Collars and cuffs, mostly, and under the arms. Then you work the cloth against itself, like this." He showed him, with Davie's own shirt, three short strokes and a fold. "Like kneading, a bit. You don't have to be gentle with linen. You do with wool, or it shrinks to fit a cat." He set the soap on the edge of the tub. "Rinse after, or it itches."

"I knew that," Davie said.

"Yes, sir."

Tor went back to his own tub. He did not look round. Davie picked up the soap, turned it over, and rubbed it into a cuff. He folded the cuff over and worked it with his knuckles. A thin froth rose. He kept working it for some time after it was clean.

Tor wrung out his things and laid them along the bench in a row: two shirts, a pair of trousers, a towel. Then he took a square of wood from inside his tunic and set it down beside them. It was pine, a hand across, unsealed, with a pattern painted on it in blue. The paint had cracked along the grain.

He put both hands flat on the bench on either side of it and looked at it.

"Right," he said, under his breath. "Corners first. Don't rush it."

"My dear Baker."

Dorgal Sorvey stood in the doorway with one shoulder against the frame. He wore a tunic of the same brown as everyone's, cut closer.

"They've found you a trade at last," he said. "I always said you had a washerwoman's hands. Do you take in mending? I've a cuff that wants turning, and you'd be doing a kindness to a man of limited means."

"I'm sorry," Tor said. "I'm only doing my own."

Dorgal's eyes moved down the row of tubs to Davie. Davie bent over his washing and scrubbed at the cuff. He didn't look up. His ears had gone red.

Dorgal came in and leaned over the bench. "And what's this? Quaint. Very quaint. Does it do anything?"

"I don't know yet," Tor said. "That is, I think so. I hope so."

The door opened again. Tovey came in first. He was tall, and he wore the school's brown with a plain dark cloak over it, fastened at the shoulder with a brooch. Behind him came eleven or twelve students, and behind them more, crowding the doorway and the court outside.

"Sorvey," Tovey said.

Dorgal straightened from the bench. He smiled. He said nothing else.

"Scholar Baker."

Tor took his hands off the bench. "Sir. My lord. I'm sorry, I'll be out of your way in just a moment, I'm nearly done."

Tovey waited until he'd finished. "You built something. I'd like to see it work."

"It's for drying, sir. Laundry." Tor looked at the square. "It's probably nothing. I've missed a whole day of classes for it, so if it's nothing, they'll likely send me home."

Tovey looked at him. He did not smile. Behind him, someone laughed politely.

"Proceed," Tovey said.

Tor touched the middle of the painted square with two fingers.

For a breath, nothing happened. Then the shirts on the bench darkened all at once, and water welled out of the weave in beads, ran together, and slid off the cloth and over the edge of the bench in a thin clear sheet. It pattered on the flagstones. It spread in a fan toward the drain and toward the front row of shoes, which stepped back. The shirts lay flat and pale and dry. The towel had stiffened, like a towel left in the sun.

Davie yelped and held up his arms. His sleeves had been wet to the elbow. They were dry.

Nobody said anything. The copper ticked in the corner.

"Can it be bought?" Tovey said.

"I— it has to go to my building master, sir. For testing. It isn't mine to sell. I'm sorry."

"I asked whether it could be bought. Not whether you would be in trouble." Tovey frowned. "Why would you be in trouble?"

Tor opened his mouth and closed it.

"My mother keeps a large house," Tovey said. "In the wet months the linen hangs a week in the gallery and still smells of the river. She would like one."

Behind him the room started up at once. *Can you make another? I'll take one. Two, my sister's at the Ridge house and it's damp as a well. How long does it last? Does it do wool?* Tor turned toward each voice as it came. The color went out of his face.

Tovey looked back over his shoulder at the crowd. He looked at them for some time. Then he faced the bench again and said nothing.

Two girls came in through the doorway and the crowd made room for them without anyone stepping aside. The taller one wore her hair pinned up close and plain. The other had a leather satchel on a long strap and ink on two fingers.

Trice, the taller of the two, looked at the wet floor, at the crowd, and at the bench. "Well," she said. "That's going to be a problem for somebody."

She crossed to the bench without hurrying, stepping around the edge of the puddle. Sara followed a step behind.

"Master Baker," Trice said. "What have you done?"

"It dries clothes, ma'am. That's all."

"That's all," Trice said. She turned her head. "Sara. What would it fetch?"

Sara crouched by the bench. She didn't touch the square. She looked at it from one side, then the other, then bent close to the paint and drew back.

"Pine," she said. "Unsealed. That's house paint, the blue they sell by the pot for doors. It'll cup in a damp room inside a year, and the paint will flake before that." She stood. "My father says, price the board, then the work, then the years. The years are where they cheat you. You're buying a season, maybe two. On ironwood, with the lines set in silver, you'd be having a very different conversation." She brushed off her knees. "As it is, thirty gold."

Somebody whistled.

"Thirty," Trice said. "Against what?"

"Against anyone in the capital having one, which they don't." Sara looked at Tor. "Tor. Thirty gold. Did you hear me?"

"It's only paint," Tor said.

"I know it's only paint. That's why it's thirty and not three hundred."

Tor was looking past her at the crowd. His eyes went from face to face along the wall and his lips moved. "Ten. Eleven. Twelve." He reached the doorway and started on the faces there. Then he looked down at the square on the bench.

"Tor," Sara said. "Nobody's asked you for twenty of them."

"Twenty-three," Tor said, quietly. He looked up. "Sorry. What?"

Trice watched him for a moment with her head a little on one side.

"Who's had it in their hands besides you?"

"Nobody, ma'am. It has to go in for testing."

"Good," said Trice. "Keep it that way."

Someone in the court outside said, loudly, "Excuse me. Pardon. Coming through, mind your feet," and the crowd in the doorway gave way to a broad-shouldered third-year with his sleeves pushed up and his collar open. He stopped just inside. He looked at Tovey, then at Dorgal, then at Trice and Sara, then at Davie, then at the twenty-odd faces along the wall. Then he looked at the square on the bench and stood still for a moment, longer than he had looked at anything else.

"Right!" Rolph said. "Lovely. Marvelous. Very clever. Baker hasn't eaten in thirty hours and he's about to fall over, and if he falls over, nobody gets anything, do they? No. Trust me. I'm a merchant's son, I know a sale when I see one, and this isn't one. This is a boy who needs a plate of eggs."

By then he had an arm round Tor's shoulders and Tor's dry shirts bundled under the other arm.

"The board, Little Tor. Pick up the board."

Tor picked up the board and held it against his chest with both hands.

"Sorry," he said to the room. "I'm sorry. It has to go in for testing. I'm sorry."

"He's sorry," Rolph agreed. "Out of the way, if you'd be so kind. Excuse us. Thank you. Excuse us."

The crowd parted. Rolph steered Tor out into the court. Behind them the crowd closed up again, and after a moment, a good many feet followed them across the wet stones.

* * *

They came up the dormitory stair with half the washing house behind them. On the turns below, feet scuffed and stopped and started again whenever Rolph glanced down.

"Don't look back," Rolph said. "It only encourages them."

Tor didn't look back. He held the board flat against his chest with one arm and took the stairs one at a time, his free hand on the rail at each step. At the top he stood for a moment with his eyes shut.

Their door was the fourth along the top corridor: plain oak, a plain iron latch, no keyhole. When the two of them came within a stride of it, something on the inside knocked softly, wood against wood, and the door eased an inch off the jamb by itself.

On the landing behind them, someone said, "Did you see—"

Rolph put Tor through the gap, followed, and shut the door with his heel. On the inside, a bar of dark ash was lowering itself into a pair of iron brackets with nobody touching it. A pattern was painted on the planks above the brackets in blue: lines, angles, a ring. The bar dropped home with a *clunk*.

Someone knocked, politely. Then louder. Voices murmured on the other side. Feet went away down the corridor, though not all of them.

"Well," Rolph said. "That's the door."

The room was narrow: two beds, two desks, one window, gray light over the dormitory roofs. On the left, paper. Paper heaped on the desk, paper under the desk, pine offcuts on the sill with half-finished patterns painted on them, a jar of brushes, a saucer crusted with dried blue. On the right, a made bed, a shelf of books, and at its foot a chest the size of a bread bin, dark wood bound in darker iron. The lock plate on its front had no keyhole. It had a pattern on it, cut into the iron, very fine.

Rolph went to the chest and laid his palm on the lid. The plate knocked softly, the same sound as the door, and the lid came loose. He lifted it.

The chest was full of gold coins. Not lined with them; full, heaped above the rim of a tray inside. Rolph took out three, put them in his pocket, and shut the lid. It knocked again.

"Pocket money," he said, to nobody.

Tor had sat down on the left-hand bed with the board on his knees. He was looking at the door bar. "It held. I was worried it wouldn't, with that many people. It's only meant to know two."

"It held." Rolph dropped onto his own bed, boots and all, and leaned back against the wall. "You know I sat and watched that thing for an afternoon? After you made it. Up, down. Up, down. I went out and came back in about ten times."

"I remember. You asked me to explain it."

"And you did. For an hour. I understood the word 'bar.'" He grinned. He looked at the board on Tor's knees, the blue lines on the pine, for a while, without grinning. Then: "Thirty gold. Sara said thirty?"

"She was being kind."

"Sara's never been kind about a price in her life." He tipped his head back against the wall. "Mother would want one."

Tor looked up.

"Mother would want one *first*. That's the trouble. Before anyone else has one." Rolph waved a hand, a wide gesture that took in the door and the stairs and the washing house beyond them. "You don't know what it's like, Little Tor. It's not about the laundry. Among the nobility, if you've got a new thing before anyone else has it, that's worth more than the thing. That's what they were all doing down there. Tovey wants it, so they want it, so their mothers will want it, and whoever gets it first gets to have people round to look at it. Trust me."

"For laundry," Tor said.

"For anything. A lamp. A lock." Rolph nodded at the door. "If anyone knew about that, you'd have a queue down the stairs."

"There's a queue down the stairs now."

"There is a queue down the stairs now," Rolph allowed. "Well. You'll have to make one for her."

"Of course," Tor said, before Rolph had quite finished. Then, more quietly, looking at nothing: "Twenty-four."

"Nobody asked you for twenty-four."

"Sara said that."

"Sara's right about most things." Rolph looked at the ceiling for a moment. "Bring it yourself."

"Sorry?"

"Hers. The one for Mother. Bring it yourself. Come home with me over the break. To the capital." He said it lightly, still looking up.

Tor sat very still. "I couldn't."

"You could."

"Not on your holiday. Your family will want you to themselves, they won't want somebody underfoot the whole time, eating their food and taking up a bed and needing things explained. I'd be in the way. I'd only be a burden on them."

"A burden," Rolph said.

"I would."

"It's a big house, Little Tor. Big. Draughty. Full of forks. You could lose a regiment in it. Nobody would notice one baker."

"Forks?"

"Dozens. You'll survive the forks." He grinned at the ceiling. "Trust me. I'll write and tell them you're coming. I'll write tonight."

Tor looked down at the board on his knees. "If your mother wouldn't mind," he said, very quietly.

"She'd mind if you didn't." Rolph put his hands behind his head. "If it did fruit, now. That'd be something. Apples. Plums. Beans. You'd not believe how much rots at home in a wet year. Some autumns it's all anyone talks about at dinner. The accounts come in and it's so many bushels lost in the low barns, and so many in the carts on the road, and everyone looks very grave and passes the salt—"

He stopped.

Tor was looking down at the board on his knees. His face hadn't changed. His lips were moving.

"Cloth wants to let go," Tor said. He wasn't looking at Rolph. "Cloth's all little channels, it's easy, the water's only sitting in it. An apple's got a skin. The water's inside. You'd have to go slower. If you go fast, the outside would seal, like a crust on a loaf before the middle's done, and then it would rot from the inside, worse than before—"

He stood up, crossed to his desk, sat, and swept a stack of paper off it onto the floor with his forearm. He found a pencil in the jar of brushes and began to draw.

Rolph stayed where he was, hands behind his head.

"—and it's alive, isn't it? An apple. A bit. After it's picked. Does that matter? It might matter. Beans are drier to start with. Grain's nearly dry already, that's half the job done, but then you'd want to stop it partway, you don't want dust, you want—" The pencil scratched. He pulled a fresh sheet across. "—you want it to *stop*. Cloth doesn't care when you stop."

The tower bell struck the hour outside. Tor didn't look up. Rolph's lips moved with the strokes, counting, and then were still. He watched Tor's back. Tor rubbed at his eye with the side of his hand and left a gray smudge of pencil across it. He was smiling slightly.

Rolph watched for a long time. Once he opened his mouth as if to say something, and closed it again.

The pencil stopped.

Tor sat still. Then he turned round on the stool, his face suddenly pointed at Rolph.

"Sorry," he said. "Oh, I'm sorry. You were talking. You were telling me about your mother, and I just—"

"I was telling you about beans."

"I'm sorry."

"I'll live." Rolph swung his boots down. "You haven't eaten. You're the color of that wall. Stay there and don't build anything while I'm gone." At the door the bar lifted for him with its soft knock. "I mean it. Nothing."

In the corridor, two first-years were sitting on the top step. They stood up very fast and started down the stairs.

* * *

He came back with a tray: a heap of eggs, half a loaf, a wedge of cheese, a pot of tea, and two apples. He set it on the end of Tor's bed. "The tea's dreadful. The apples are a joke. Eat."

Tor ate the eggs, the bread, the cheese and one apple. He held the second apple for some time, turning it in his fingers, pressing a thumb into the skin, and then set it on the desk beside his drawings.

The afternoon went slowly. Tor dozed sitting up against the wall, woke, ate the heel of the loaf, got up stiffly, wrote a line at the desk, and sat back down. When he stretched, he winced. Every so often he said, "Sorry," and Rolph said, "Stop that," and Tor said, "Sorry."

Rolph lay on his bed with a book open on his chest. The spine read *Principles of Field Theory, Third Year*. He turned a page twice in the course of the afternoon. Most of the time his eyes were over the top of it, on Tor.

Once, someone tried the door gently from outside. The bar did not lift. Whoever it was went away.

By the sixth bell Tor was asleep on top of his blankets with his boots on and one hand hanging toward the floor. Rolph got up, unlaced the boots, and pulled them off one at a time. Tor didn't wake. On the way back Rolph stopped at the desk and looked down at the drawings: arrows, half-patterns, a small round shape that might have been a plum, one word underlined three times. He bent closer to the word. He straightened up, and put out the lamp.

* * *

In the dark, a pencil.

A candle stub burned on Tor's desk. Tor sat over it in his shirt, writing, line after line, fast enough that the pencil could be heard biting into the paper. He was muttering under his breath, too low to make out. Once he laughed, a short surprised sound, and wrote faster.

"Tor," Rolph said from his bed.

The pencil went on.

Rolph lay looking at the ceiling for a while. Then he turned over toward the wall.

* * *

The window was barely gray. Tor's bed was empty, and made. Tor's corner of the desk was clear: paper stacked, brushes in the jar, the saucer of blue set square on top of the stack. The board was gone. The door bar was down in its brackets.

On the edge of the desk lay the night's pages, squared neatly.

Rolph, in his shirt, picked up the top one. It was covered edge to edge in small cramped writing, lines so close they nearly touched, words run together without spaces, broken every few inches by a scrap of pattern or an arrow looping back to something higher up. In the margin, in capitals, underlined twice: CRUST. Under that, smaller: *slower, ask about skins, NOT dust.*

He turned the page upside down and looked at it that way. He turned it back.

He put it down exactly where it had been, squared it against the others with two fingers, and went to find his boots.

* * *

The corridor outside the building master's office was still dark at the far end when Tor came down it at a near run, the board held against his chest. A first-year sat on the bench by the door with a cloth bundle on his knees. He looked up. Tor didn't see him.

Tor knocked. The door opened before he'd taken his hand away.

"Baker."

"Sir. I'm sorry it's so early, sir. I finished it. I mean, I think I finished it. I tested it yesterday, at the washing house. It worked on shirts and trousers and a towel, and wool, I think, someone's sleeves, and I wanted to bring it to you before—"

"Give it here."

The building master was tall and gray and narrow, in a leather apron over his tunic, with a magnifier on a cord round his neck. He took the board in both hands and turned it to the window. He brought the magnifier up. He tipped the board so the light ran across the paint, first one way and then the other, and held each corner close to the glass in turn. He ran a fingernail along one edge and looked at the fingernail.

On the bench, the first-year sat without moving.

"Washing house," said the building master. He handed the board back to Tor, took him by the upper arm, and steered him out. In the corridor he stopped in front of the bench. "You. What's that?"

"Copy-work, sir. Lamp-squares. Three. They're late, sir. I'm sorry."

"Bring them. I need a witness."

The first-year stood up and followed them.

They crossed the dormitory court fast, the building master's long legs setting the pace, Tor half a step behind and talking. "It's only a field for cloth, sir. It lifts the water out of the fibers, it doesn't heat anything. I used a closed pattern so it wouldn't spread. The pine's not good, I know the pine's not good, I'm sorry about the pine, it was what I had—"

"Yes," said the building master.

The washing house was empty, gray and dripping. One tap ran into one tub. The building master let go of Tor's arm, took the board from him and set it on the bench. Then he took Tor by the collar and the back of the belt and lifted him, and put him in the tub.

The first-year's bundle hit the floor. Something inside it clacked.

Tor went under all the way. Water slopped over the rim in a sheet. The building master's arm was in the tub to the elbow for a count of two. Then he straightened. Tor came up sitting, water to his chest, hair flat to his skull, mouth already open.

"—sorry, sir." He coughed.

"Out."

Tor climbed out and stood on the flagstones, streaming. Water ran from his tunic and trousers, out of the tops of his boots, off the end of his nose. The building master picked up the board, looked from it to Tor, and backed away three paces so that Tor stood between him and the tub. He touched the middle of the pattern with two fingers.

The water came out of Tor's clothes all at once. It beaded on the brown wool and ran together and slid down him in a clear sheet to the floor, where it spread round his boots in a puddle the shape of a boy. His tunic paled. His trousers paled. His sleeves let go of his arms.

Tor stood in dry clothes with his hair still plastered flat and dripping. A drop fell from his eyebrow. He blinked.

"Hold out your hands."

Tor held them out. The building master turned them over, front and back. He took one by the wrist and pressed hard on the thumbnail and let go, and watched it. He bent and looked into Tor's eyes, one and then the other, a hand's breadth away.

"Any pain?"

"No, sir."

"Dizziness? Thirst?"

"No, sir. My boots are still wet. Inside, I mean. I think it's the leather. Leather's different, it's—"

"Yes." The building master let go of the wrist. "A field like this has to be shown safe for use around people. That's what this was. Now it has been." He turned. "You saw that."

The first-year hadn't picked up his bundle. "Sir. Sir, you *drowned* him."

"I didn't drown him. He's standing there."

"You put him in a *tub*."

"That's where the water was." The building master turned the board over, looked at the back, turned it face up, and put it under his arm. "I'll keep this. Baker, go to your classes. When anyone asks about last week, you were doing a building exercise for my class."

Tor's hair dripped onto his collar. "But I wasn't, sir. It wasn't assigned. I just—"

"It is now."

"Yes, sir." A pause. "Will that be enough, sir? So they don't send me home?"

The building master looked at him for a moment, without any expression at all.

"Go to class, Baker." To the first-year: "Pick those up. Bring them to my office. They'd better be good."

* * *

The meditation room was round, with a ring of mats on the floor and a low table in the middle. Doris came in carrying something in both hands and set it down on the table without a word.

It was a square of black glass, about the size of a palm, smooth as still water on its face, set in a rim of something pale and grained that wasn't quite bone. Light lay on the glass without going into it.

The students came in and sat. Doris stood by the table with her hands folded. Her lips moved slightly as each one sat down.

Tor came in last, with his hair still wet, and stopped at the edge of the ring of mats.

"Ma'am," he said. "About last week. I'm sorry. I was doing a building exercise for my building master's class." He said it evenly, like a recitation. Then he went on. "Only, it was a working trance, the deep kind, and that's—I mean, what we do here is the lighter kind of the same thing, isn't it, ma'am? So I was meditating. The whole time. Thirty hours, nearly. So I thought, if it's all the same to you, perhaps I wasn't really absent. From this. In a way."

He stopped.

"I'm sorry," he said.

Doris looked at him: his wet hair, his dry tunic, his hands clasped in front of him. She nodded once toward the one empty mat.

Tor sat.

The room took a while to settle. Breathing slowed. Someone's stomach made a noise and someone else smothered a laugh. Then they were all looking at the black glass, and it was quiet.

Around the ring, shoulders dropped slowly, a few at a time. Tor's dropped all at once. His eyes went still on the glass and stayed still. His breathing slowed until it could barely be seen.

Doris sat on a stool by the wall with her hands in her lap. She watched the ring for most of a bell. For most of that bell, she was watching Tor.

* * *

The theory master was standing in his classroom doorway when they came in, rubbing his hands together. When he saw Tor he said, "Ha!"

Dorgal Sorvey went to the third bench by the window, sat, took out his notes, and laid out three pens in a row.

"Baker! There you are. Sit, sit, no, there, at the front, where I can see you. I've been talking to your building master." The theory master's eyebrows went up and stayed up. "At breakfast. At *length*. He was insufferable. I had to leave before the porridge."

"I'm sorry, sir," said Tor.

"Sorry! Ha!" The theory master went to his desk, still rubbing his hands.

Dorgal uncapped a pen.

"Now then. Fields of discrimination." The theory master wrote it across the board in large looping letters. "Which you all know. Which every first-year knows and every third-year forgets, in my experience, round about the time they start to think they're clever. Everything that lives has a vitality field. Yes? Everything. The moss on that wall. The flies on the moss. Sorvey."

Dorgal looked up.

"The more complex the creature, the stronger and more complex its field. Who's more complex, Sorvey, you or the moss?"

"On a good day, sir, me."

A small laugh went round the room. The theory master grinned. "On a good day. And a human field, being very strong and very complicated, naturally resists any simple field you set against it. It shrugs them off. To touch living tissue directly takes a specific, focused effort, and that, ladies and gentlemen, is why you can't kill your roommate with a lamp-square, however much you might like to. Distinguishing living from not-living. Write it down. It'll be on everything I ever give you."

They wrote it down. At the front bench, Tor wrote steadily, head bent.

The theory master talked for most of a bell. Twice he stopped in the middle of a sentence, looked at Tor, and smiled, then went on. Tor didn't look up either time. On the third bench, Dorgal did.

At the end, as they packed up, the theory master said, "Baker. A word, later in the week. Nothing urgent. Something I'd like to show you." He rubbed his hands. "Ha!"

"Yes, sir," said Tor. "I'm sorry, sir."

Dorgal capped his pen. The last line of his notes had torn through the paper. He folded the sheet over so the tear didn't show, and put it away.

* * *

"He put you in the *tub*?"

"In the tub. All the way in."

Rolph laughed so hard he had to put his spoon down. Half the long table looked round. "Oh, I'd have paid. I'd have *paid*, Little Tor. Your face. Tell me about your face."

"I don't know about my face. I was under the water."

"Tell me about the first-year, then."

"He said 'you drowned him.'"

"He didn't."

"He said it twice."

The cafeteria was full and loud and smelled of stew. Rolph and Tor had the end of a bench by the windows. Two empty bowls were stacked in front of Tor, and he was working slowly through a third.

"The theory master said *ha*," Tor said. "A lot. I think he's pleased about something."

"I should think he is."

"Do you think it's about the building master? They had breakfast. He said the building master was insufferable."

"I think," Rolph said, "it might possibly be about you."

Tor looked at him for a moment, then went back to his stew.

Rolph opened his mouth again. Tor's spoon stopped halfway to his mouth.

Rolph turned round.

Kolb was crossing the cafeteria. He was tall and bald, middle-aged by the look of him, with a pale scar running from the corner of one eye down into his jaw. His sleeveless jerkin showed long arms corded with muscle, lean rather than bulky. He walked between the tables without hurrying, and the noise at each table dropped as he passed and rose again behind him. He stopped at the end of their bench. He didn't sit.

"Baker."

Tor put down his spoon and stood up. "Sir."

"Sit down."

Tor sat.

"Fifth day," Kolb said. He spoke low and pleasantly, as if mentioning the weather. At the next table, nobody looked round. "You weren't in my yard."

"No, sir. I'm sorry, sir. I was doing a building exercise for—"

"I don't care." There was no heat in it. "I don't have building exercises, Baker. I have a yard. You owe it an afternoon. We'll start collecting today." He looked at Tor a moment longer. "I'll see you at the bell."

He walked away. The noise came back up behind him.

Tor sat very straight, both hands flat on the table on either side of his bowl, looking at the stew.

"Listen," Rolph said. "Little Tor. Listen to me. He's all bark."

Tor nodded.

"Well. Mostly bark. He does this every term to somebody, it's practically a hobby. And I'll be there, all right? I'll partner you. He can't run you any harder than he runs me, and he's been trying for three years, and look at me. Fine. Perfectly fine."

Tor nodded again. His eyes stayed on the bowl.

"You'll be sore," Rolph said. "That's all. You'll be sore and you'll complain and I'll rub liniment on your back and you'll say sorry about it the whole time, and in a week he'll have forgotten. Tor. Are you hearing me?"

"Yes," Tor said. "Sorry. Yes."

Rolph looked at him for a long moment, then put a hand on Tor's shoulder. Tor didn't move.

The tower bell began to strike.

At the first stroke Tor stood, slowly and carefully, and Rolph's hand fell away. Tor picked up his bowl, held it, and put it back down where it had been. "Sorry," he said, to no one. Then he walked out of the cafeteria toward the yard, very straight, a little too slowly.

Rolph left both bowls on the table and followed him out.
