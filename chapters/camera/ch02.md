# Chapter Two

* * *

The yard lay behind the long hall: a square of packed sand the color of old bread, weapon racks along three sides, and along the fourth a wall that had plainly belonged to something else before the school was built against it. In the middle stood the pell, an oak trunk as thick as a man, sunk to half its height. At chest height the wood was beaten pale and furred.

Kolb was hitting it with a wooden waster about as slender as a fence post. He set his feet, turned from the hips, and struck. The sound was flat and enormous. The racks rattled. Dust jumped off the top of the wall and hung there.

Tor came through the gate. Rolph came two steps behind him.

"Sir," Tor said.

The waster came around again. The pell took it. A chip of mortar fell off the wall.

"Baker." Kolb lowered the waster and turned. He was smiling. Rolph, seeing it, stopped walking. "Good. You're early. I like early." He looked past Tor. "Rolph. Go away."

"Sir, I said I'd partner him, I thought—"

"And I've said go away. That's two things said. Mine's the one that counts." A pause. "You can stand by the rack. Stand by the rack and be quiet, and you can watch. Most people pay to watch me."

Rolph went to the rack and stopped there.

"Know what that is, Baker?"

"A pell, sir."

"What does it do?"

Tor looked at the pell for some time.

"It stands there, sir," he said. "Mostly."

Kolb laughed, short and pleased. "It does. It's very good at it. Watch."

He hit it again. The trunk shuddered, all the way down into the ground. At the rack, Rolph shifted his feet.

"That," Kolb said, in the same pleasant voice, "would kill you. Not hurt you. Kill you. Your ribs would go in and your heart would stop and you'd lie down on my sand and be dead, and I'd have to write to your mother. Unless you had armor on, or a shield field. Have you got a shield on you, Baker?"

"No, sir."

"No. Most people haven't."

He leaned the waster against the pell and unhooked something from his belt: a plain hilt a hand and a half long, wrapped in cord, with a line of small regular marks around the guard. He held it out to one side.

There was a blade on it. It didn't slide out; it was simply there, four feet of dull gray. As Kolb turned his wrist, the blade went edge-on toward the gate and vanished: a hilt, and nothing, and then, as the wrist turned back, a blade again.

"Cutter," said Kolb. "You'll have seen pictures."

Tor nodded. His face had lost most of its color.

Kolb walked to the pell with the cutter held loosely in one hand and swung it once, level, the way a man waves off a fly.

There was no sound. The blade went through the oak without slowing and came out the other side, and Kolb let it finish its swing. For a moment nothing else happened. Then the top half of the pell, taller than Kolb, leaned a little to one side, slid off the bottom half, and fell onto the sand with a thump. It rolled a short way and stopped. The cut face was smooth as a tabletop.

"Armor doesn't do much about that," Kolb said. "A shield does, if it's a good one. Most aren't." "You build things, Baker."

"Sir, I—a little—"

"I hear you build them very well. I hear it at breakfast whether I want to or not." The blade was gone. He hooked the hilt back on his belt. "So build me a shield. Stops that." The waster. "And stops that." He tapped the hilt. "Two weeks."

"Yes, sir." Then: "For how long should it stop them, sir?"

"Twenty minutes."

"Twenty minutes, sir?"

"Of me," said Kolb. "Hitting it. With you inside."

Nobody in the yard said anything.

"Thank you, sir," said Tor.

At the rack, Rolph shut his eyes.

"Sir," he said, "sir, with respect, he's a builder, he isn't—"

"Which is why I'm not asking him to fight me," Kolb said, without turning. "I'm asking him to build. Different thing. Easier. I've seen him fight." He looked Tor up and down: boots, tunic, hands. "Now. Warm up. Run to town and back."

"Town's five miles!" said Rolph.

"So it's ten, there and back. Your arithmetic's coming along." Kolb tilted his head. "What do I always tell you, Baker? About a real fight?"

"That I should run away from it, sir."

"Good. Practice."

Tor's shoulders came down. He bent and retied both bootlaces, quickly, and straightened, and went out through the gate at an easy lope.

Kolb watched him all the way to the bend in the road.

"Nice and loose," he said.

Then he turned his head. "Rolph. You're still here. Why are you still here?"

* * *

A mile short of Hollin, where the road leveled out at the bottom of the long slope, there was a gray marker stone at the verge, smoother than any stone ought to be, with letters cut in it that nobody could read. Tor was standing beside it with his hands on his hips.

He wasn't running. He was looking back up the slope to the ridge, where the school's bell tower stood up against the sky. He looked at it for a long while. Then he turned his head and looked the other way, down a narrower road that went off east into the trees.

A cart came along the town road behind him, pulled by a mule, loaded with cabbages. Its near wheel squealed at every turn. The woman driving it pulled up beside him.

"You want a ride back, love? Nobody'll know. I've had worse than you sitting on my cabbages."

Tor jumped and turned around. "Oh! No, ma'am. Thank you, ma'am. I'm meant to be running."

"Looked like you were meant to be standing."

"Yes, ma'am. Sorry." He looked down the east road again. "Ma'am, which way is Two Rivers? From here?"

She pointed down the east road. "That way. Four days walking, if you're quick. Five if you're sensible." She looked at him. "Why? You thinking of going?"

He didn't answer. He went on looking down the road.

"Kolb's lot, are you," she said. "What's he got you running for?"

"To warm up, ma'am."

"Warm up for what?"

He considered it. "I'm not certain, ma'am. I think possibly to be killed."

She laughed. He didn't. She stopped.

She took up a short knife from the board beside her and cut a cabbage free of its stalk in one stroke. The cabbage rolled over against the side of the cart.

Tor was watching the knife.

"It's a line," he said.

"It's a knife, love."

"No, ma'am, sorry, I mean, a blow's a weight. It comes in all over, you spread it out and it's—but an edge is a line, it all comes in at one place, so you can't spread it, you'd have to—" He stopped talking aloud. His lips went on moving. "Unless you didn't stop it. Unless you turned it."

"Turned what?"

He was already running. He went past the cart toward Hollin, not back up the slope and not down the east road, talking to himself, his hands shaping something in the air in front of him.

In Hollin's square, there was a well with a worn stone lip, polished on one side where many hands had slapped it. Tor ran past it without touching it, past the chandler's, out the far side of the square and up the mill road. A woman at the pump called after him that the school was the other way. He didn't look around.

The bell in Hollin's little chapel struck the half hour. The cabbage cart stood unloading by the pump.

When Tor came back down the mill road, he was running faster than he had been going out. He was still talking. As he passed the well he reached out and slapped the stone without breaking stride, and went on across the square and out onto the school road, and up the long slope toward the tower.

The woman with the cart watched him go. She had one cabbage left on the board, set apart from the others. She looked at it, and then up the road, and then put it back in the cart.

* * *

By the time Tor came back through the gate, the yard was full: thirty or so students in brown, pairing off along the fence, and two in black, sitting on the bench by the water butt. There was space on the bench on either side of them.

Tor was dust to the knees and sweat-dark down his back, and he was talking under his breath. He nearly ran into Kolb.

"Baker. You're late."

"Sorry, sir. I went a bit past the town."

"How far past?"

"There was a mill, sir."

Kolb nodded once. Then he turned his head toward the bench. "Black. Over here."

The two in black stood and crossed the sand together. Tor watched them come with a polite, blank face. When they were two strides away, his face changed.

"Oh," he said. "Miss Sara. Miss—Trice. I didn't—you're in *black*."

"We are, Master Baker," said Trice. "Do keep up."

"Unarmed," said Kolb. "Baker, you've got black, both of them, by turns. Trice first. Go."

Sara stepped back to a line scratched in the sand and folded her arms. Trice opened with a kick straight up between Tor's legs.

Tor made a noise like a startled hen and was a foot to the left when it arrived. Then he turned around and started to run away.

"Baker," said Kolb. "Spar."

Tor stopped. He turned back. "Sorry, sir," he said, and to Trice, with a little bob of the head, "sorry," and put his hands up.

Trice's mouth went thin.

Along the fence, one pair after another stopped practicing and watched. Trice's kicks came low to the knee, high to the head, a front kick that changed halfway and came around from the side. Tor wasn't there for any of them. He moved before she did, small and quick. Twice, when she missed him, he said "sorry."

Trice took an extra breath before her next attack. She spun into a wheel kick, her whole body turning, her heel coming around at head height fast enough to whistle.

Tor stepped forward, inside the spin, caught her around the waist as she came around, turned with her, and took them both down onto the sand with Trice underneath.

Someone along the fence said, "*Oh*," out loud.

On the ground, Trice twisted, and Tor was already there. She twisted the other way, and he was there too. "Sorry," he said. "Sorry." Her arm was nearly pinned.

Then there was a knife at his throat: small, black-handled, its flat lying along the side of his neck under the jaw.

Tor went still. Nobody in the yard moved.

Then the sand moved. It slid away from Tor all at once, in a ring, with a hiss, grit and pebbles skittering outward across the yard, and Trice went with it. She was lifted off him and pushed back along the ground a full arm's length, her heels scoring two lines in the sand. Her knife hand was still stretched out toward him. The point had stopped against nothing, a hand's breadth from his throat. She leaned on it. It went no farther.

Sara's eyes went to Tor's wrists, then his neck, his belt, the hem of his tunic.

"Trice," she said. "Off. It's a shield. The knife won't help."

Trice got to her feet. The knife was no longer in her hand. She looked at Tor, sitting in his clean ring of sand with his hands up in front of his face. Then she took a handful of something out of a pouch at her belt, and threw it.

White powder burst around Tor and stopped in the air. It clung to nothing, a white shell in the shape of a large egg, with Tor inside it. Inside, his hands went to his face. His head turned one way, then the other. Then he went still in a different way. His shoulders had stopped moving.

"Tor," said Sara sharply.

The shell collapsed. The powder came down all at once in a soft white heap, and Tor came out of the bottom of it on his hands and knees, coughing, white from his hair to his elbows, like a loaf rolled in flour.

He coughed a long time. Between coughs, he talked to the sand.

"—air. It has to let the air—*hkk*—air in. Air but not—not *that*. Not dust. Sorry." He looked up at Trice. "Sorry, did I hurt you? I didn't mean to push."

Trice looked at him with her head a little on one side. Then she turned to Kolb.

"Sir," she said, "he has a shield."

"A good one," said Sara. "Sir, that's not a fair bout. Whatever it is, it held a knife off at—"

"And a perfectly good handful of—" said Trice.

Kolb put his head back and laughed. It carried right across the yard. Along the fence, students looked at one another.

"He hasn't got a shield," said Kolb. "That's my homework you're complaining about."

"Your—"

"He's building me one. Two weeks. Looks like it'll work, too." He was still smiling. "Which means I'm going to have to hit it properly."

Tor stopped coughing long enough to look up at him.

"Air, Baker," said Kolb.

"Yes, sir. Sorry, sir. I noticed."

Kolb laughed again and walked off down the fence, and the pairs along it went back to work.

Sara stood looking at Tor. He was sitting back on his heels, trying to beat the powder out of his hair and only moving it about. She looked at his wrists again. Then at the whole of him: the smallest boy in the yard, by a head.

"Right," Kolb called from the far end. "Again. Sara, your turn."

* * *

The bouts went on all afternoon, black and black by turns.

Trice put her knife to his throat once more, from behind, and held it there. Tor froze, said, "Oh, sorry," and tapped the sand. The sand didn't move. Trice took the knife away and looked at the back of his neck for a while before she stepped back.

Sara threw him twice. Both times she held out a hand and pulled him up, and both times he said, "Thank you."

"You don't thank people for that," said Trice.

"Sorry. It was a very good throw."

Once, Trice left her shoulder open. Tor put a palm into it, hard enough that she took a step back, and then spent the whole of the next exchange saying, "I'm so sorry, are you—I didn't—" until she hit him in the stomach. He folded over, straightened, and said, "Sorry."

The tower bell struck. The yard emptied. Tor stood in the middle of the sand, still white at the hairline, and bowed to each of them in turn, very correctly.

"Thank you, Miss Sara. Thank you, Miss—" a hesitation— "Trice. I'm sorry about your shoulder."

"I left it open, Master Baker."

"Yes," he said, "but I hit it."

He walked off toward the dormitory arch, a little stiffly, talking under his breath. "Air," he said, and "a line," and "turn it, not stop it," and then he was through the arch.

Trice sat down on the bench by the water butt. Sara sat down beside her.

"Who benefits," said Trice, "from making a boy like that run ten miles?"

"He ran more than ten," said Sara. "Rolph said there was a mill."

Neither of them said anything else. They sat on the bench and looked at the ring in the sand, which nobody had raked.

* * *

In their room, the lamp was lit and Tor was at the desk. There was still white in his hair and eyebrows, and a white tidemark on the collar of his tunic. Two sheets of paper lay in front of him. The left one was covered edge to edge in cramped lines, sigils drawn and redrawn, a column of small numbers down the margin. The right one was nearly empty. At the top it said AIR, underlined three times, so hard the pen had gone through.

Rolph came in with a brown bottle.

"Shirt off," he said. "I promised."

"You don't have to—"

"I said I'd rub liniment on your back and you'd apologize the whole time, and I keep my promises. Off."

Tor pulled his tunic over his head without getting up. Across his shoulder blades ran a long red stripe. There was a yellowing patch on his ribs the size of a heel, and a small round bruise on his upper arm. Rolph uncorked the bottle. The room filled with the smell of camphor.

"So," said Rolph. "You lived."

"For now." Tor dipped his pen. "Two weeks."

"He's not actually going to kill you."

"He said twenty minutes, Rolph."

"He says lots of things."

"He cut the pell in half."

Rolph opened his mouth, and closed it, and put liniment on the stripe.

"Sorry," said Tor. "Ow. Sorry."

"There it is."

"Did you see the—at the end, with Trice—"

"I saw the cloud from the far end of the yard. I thought you'd exploded." Rolph worked the liniment in. "Half the second-years are saying you've got a shield sewn into your drawers. The other half are saying you don't, and they're the ones who are worried." His hands slowed. "Did you mean to do that? The push. With the sand."

The pen hovered. "I think so," said Tor. "It seemed like the thing to do. Is that—was it wrong?"

Rolph's hands stopped on Tor's back. They stayed there.

"No," he said. "Not wrong." He picked up the bottle again. "Do you know how many people in this school have been beaten by both of those two in one afternoon? It's a very short list. You should be proud."

"I'm not sure it's the sort of list one's proud of."

"It's a list with you at the top of it. That's a start."

Tor didn't answer. He wrote the last line on the left-hand sheet, blotted it, and set it to one side, squared to the edge of the desk. He pulled the right-hand sheet toward him and, under AIR, wrote *Dust*. He drew a line between the two words, then a small gate in the line, then crossed the gate out.

"Air but not dust," he said, to the paper. "A knife's a line. Turn it, don't stop it."

Rolph put liniment on the bruise on his ribs.

"Ow, sorry," said Tor, and kept writing.

* * *

The building room ran along the north side of the hall, and the morning light came in flat and even across two rows of scarred benches. The next morning, twenty students sat at them, copying lamp-squares: a circle, a cross, a little hooked line, forty times over.

Dorgal had three pens laid out in a row and was using the middle one. Across the aisle and one bench down, Tor held his pen carefully. There was a bruise on the back of his hand the size and color of a plum. When he reached for the ink, he did it slowly, like an old man.

The door opened. A porter in gray crossed to the building master's desk and gave him a card. The building master held it up to the window and tipped it so the light ran across it. It was heavy and cream-colored, with a small mark pressed into one corner: a ring of knotted rope with a letter inside it.

Dorgal looked down at his square.

The building master put the card in his apron and looked out of the window, down into the court, for a moment. "Baker. With me."

Tor looked up. He put down his pen, wiped it, put it down again a little farther from the edge, and stood.

"Off to be dunked again, Baker?" said Dorgal, without raising his head. "Do try to float this time."

Someone laughed. "I'll try," said Tor, seriously, and followed the building master out.

Dorgal finished his circle and began the cross. On the second stroke the nib split with a small, clean sound and left a blot in the middle of the square. He looked at it. Then he laid the pen at the end of the row and picked up the left-hand one.

Through the window, between the frame and the corner of the dormitory, a black carriage stood in the court with a pair of grays in the traces. On its door was the same knotted ring. A broad man in a brown coat stood at the horses' heads, talking to one of them. He had a new-looking beard.

Dorgal looked at the carriage for some time. Then he started a new square.

* * *

The building master's office smelled of linseed and cold ashes. A man sat in the master's chair, behind the desk, facing the door. He wasn't large. He wore a dark coat, very well cut, and there were two rings on his right hand and one on his left. The hands under the rings were broad, the knuckles flattened. A painted square of pine lay on the desk in front of him beside a dry handkerchief, folded.

Tor came in behind the building master and stopped two steps inside the door. The door was still close at his back.

The man smiled. "Sit down, Scholar Baker."

There was one other chair. Tor looked at it, then at the building master, who nodded, and went to the window. Tor sat on the front third of the chair with his hands on his knees.

"Sir," he said. "Good morning, sir."

"Good morning. My name's Sorvey. I buy things. I'm told you've made a thing that dries laundry."

"Yes, sir. It's not really anything. It's only a field for cloth, it lifts the water out, it's on pine, the pine's not very good—"

"I've seen it." Sorvey touched the folded handkerchief with one finger. "It's on pine. It works. I'd like as many as you can make." He took a small purse from inside his coat and set it on the desk, closed. "Two gold each, Scholar Baker. I'll send the boards and the paint. I'll send a cart for them when they're done. You'll never have to carry one anywhere or speak to anyone about it. You build them, I take them, you're paid."

Tor's eyes went to the purse and away again, quickly. His hands tightened on his knees. His shoulders came forward a little.

"That's very generous, sir," he said.

"It is."

"Only, I'm afraid I—" He stopped. He looked at the building master, whose back was to the room. He looked at Sorvey's hands, resting on the desk. Sorvey didn't move them. "I've already got someone," said Tor. "To do that. Represent me. Miss Sara is. Representing me. In this." A pause. "I think."

"You think," said Sorvey.

"She is. Sir." Tor's lips moved, as if he were adding something up. "She priced it, at the laundry, and Miss Trice said—and then they—yes. Miss Sara Debri. She is."

Sorvey looked at him. Tor looked back for as long as he could, and then looked at the desk.

"Debri," said Sorvey.

At the window, the building master shifted his weight. Sorvey didn't look around.

"Then I won't tread on the Debris' toes," he said. "Not over laundry." He put the purse back inside his coat. "But I'll tell you what I'll do, Scholar Baker. Anything new you make, anything at all, whether it dries a shirt or lights a lamp or does something nobody's thought of, I'll pay for the making of it. Before it's made, if you like. You tell me what you want to try, and I'll pay for the trying." He smiled. "Failures included."

"Thank you, sir," said Tor.

Sorvey took a card from his waistcoat, cream, with the knotted ring in the corner, and held it out across the desk. Tor took it by the very edges and didn't look at it.

"Keep it somewhere you'll find it." Sorvey stood. Tor was on his feet a beat later. "Master. Thank you for the use of your chair."

The building master turned from the window. He didn't speak. Sorvey shook his hand.

At the door, Sorvey stopped beside Tor, close. Tor stood very still.

"You've a good eye for a room, Scholar Baker," said Sorvey pleasantly. "Who taught you that?"

"Nobody, sir," said Tor. "Sorry, sir."

Sorvey nodded and went out, pulling on a pair of gray gloves, a finger at a time.

* * *

In the corridor, the first-year sat on the bench with a cloth bundle of lamp-squares on his knees. Sorvey stopped in front of him, still working the second glove onto his hand, and looked down.

"Good morning," Sorvey said.

The first-year's mouth opened. Nothing came out.

Sorvey smiled and walked away down the corridor. His boots made no sound on the boards. The first-year watched him all the way to the corner.

Behind him, the office door stood ajar.

"That was Sorvey," the building master said.

"Yes, sir. He seemed very—" Tor stopped. "He seemed very sure, sir."

"He is a buyer, Baker. One of the biggest in the north. He came here because I asked him to." The master's voice was entirely flat. "And you've sent him away."

"I'm sorry, sir. I didn't mean to be ungrateful. Only, Miss Sara—"

"Yes. Debri." A pause. Something was picked up from the desk and put down. "You've a great deal to learn about the trade, Baker. Let me tell you one piece of it for nothing. When a builder's piece goes through testing, it is not unusual for the builder to remember the testing. Testing is work. Whoever tests a piece puts his name to it. It wouldn't be a bad idea, when your money comes in, to see that he's not forgotten."

On the bench, the first-year put both hands over his mouth.

"Oh," said Tor. "Oh, of course, sir. That's only fair. I hadn't thought. I'm sorry, I should have thought of it myself, testing's ever so much work. Who does the testing, sir? I'll go and find him. I'll ask Miss Sara how much is usual."

The office was silent. The first-year leaned, very slightly, toward the gap in the door. Through it he could see the end of the desk, and the building master's face, long and gray, and on it an expression that held for a long moment and gave nothing away except that it was holding.

"Go back to class, Baker."

"Yes, sir. Thank you, sir. Sorry, sir."

Tor came out and nearly walked into the bench. "Oh, hello! It's you. Are you late again?"

"He was asking you for a—" the first-year began, and stopped.

"For a what?"

"Nothing."

"He's very fair, I think," said Tor. "Don't you? He thinks of things."

He looked down at the card in his fingers: cream, heavy, the knotted ring in the corner. He held it a moment longer. Then he slid it into the front of his tunic, slowly, holding it well away from himself until the last moment.

"Good luck with your squares," he said, and went off up the corridor toward the north room, walking stiffly.

The first-year sat on the bench. After a while, the building master appeared in the doorway and held out his hand. The first-year gave him the lamp-squares. The master looked at the top one, tipped it to the light, and handed the bundle back.

"Again," he said. "The cross is crooked."

He shut the door.

* * *

The sun was low in the gate when Tor came back into the yard. His face was gray with dust, streaked where sweat had run, and his tunic had dried and soaked and dried again in rings. He was barely jogging. He stopped in front of Kolb and stood there, swaying a little.

The yard was empty except for Kolb and one tall girl by the rack, working through a guard drill on her own.

"Sir."

"Baker." Kolb picked up an iron bar that had been leaning on the rack, as long as a waster and as thick as a broom handle, and held it out. "The pell. Until you can't."

The pell was a new trunk, pale and sappy, set in the hole where yesterday's had stood. Tor took the bar. His arms sank under it, then came up. He walked to the pell, set his feet, and hit it.

The bar bounced off the wood with a sound like a dropped pot. Tor's whole body jerked with it. He set his feet again and hit it again.

The tall girl stopped her drill.

The blows went on, slower and lower. After a while Tor began to talk under his breath, one word to a blow. The girl bent to her bootlace a few strides away.

"Weight," said Tor, and hit the pell. "Spread." He hit it. "Line. Turn." He hit it. "Air."

The bar came down on the sand. He bent for it, and lifted it as far as his knees, and stopped. His arms stayed where they were, trembling. He looked down at them.

"Enough," said Kolb, from the gate. "Go and eat, Baker."

Tor walked toward the arch, still holding the bar. The tall girl caught up to him and took it out of his hands.

"Oh," he said. "Thank you. That's very kind. Thank you, um—"

He looked at her face for some time.

"Petra," she said.

"Petra. Yes. Sorry. I knew that."

She smiled at him. He blinked. Then he went on toward the dormitory, his arms hanging at his sides.

* * *

In the dormitory corridor, the lamps were far apart, and between them it was dark and cold. Two girls came up the stairs. One had dark hair down past her shoulders, loose; the other wore green, with thin sleeves and a comb in her hair. A second-year coming down passed them, looked back, and missed a step.

They stopped at the fourth door on the top corridor. Trice knocked.

The bar lifted inside with a wooden knock, and the door opened a little way, and then a little more, pushed by an elbow. Tor stood behind it holding up both hands, which were shaking.

"Sorry," he said. "Kolb. The pell. I can't quite— Please come in. Sorry. It's a mess."

Paper covered the desk and the bed. On the floor, a sheet with AIR at the top was held down by a boot. On the desk stood a tray with a teapot and a cup, and beside the cup a ring of spilled tea. The collar of Tor's tunic was damp.

He looked at the two of them in the doorway: the loose hair, the green, the comb.

"Oh," he said. "Are you going somewhere nice? I'm sorry, I won't keep you."

"We came here," said Trice.

"Oh! Before, you mean." He smiled. "That's very good of you. Please, sit. Anywhere. Not there, that's the food field. There. Sorry."

Trice sat. She looked straight ahead.

Sara poured the tea and held the cup to Tor's mouth while he drank. "Thank you," he said into it, twice.

"Tor. When did you last eat?"

"Lunch, I think."

Sara looked at him, and didn't ask the next question.

"You had a visitor this morning," said Trice.

"How did you—"

"Everybody knows. There's a first-year who's told it to half the school." She folded her hands in her lap. "Douglas Sorvey offered you two gold a board for the laundry field, and you told him Sara represents you."

Tor went red from the collar up. "I'm sorry. I shouldn't have said it without asking. Only, at the laundry, when Miss Sara said thirty, and then you said— I thought it was sort of— and he was very—" He turned to Sara. "I'm sorry."

"Don't be," said Sara. "You did exactly right."

"Did I?"

"Who benefits, Master Baker," said Trice, "from a man paying two gold for a thing worth thirty?"

Tor considered it. "Him."

"Him. And from paying for your *failures*? He'll pay for every failure you ever make, and then he'll own every failure you ever make, and the successes that come after them, and you'll find one morning that there's a carriage in the court for you, and it's not taking you anywhere you'd have chosen. That's how the Sorveys buy. They buy early, and then they own the stable."

"He did seem," said Tor slowly, "very sure."

"He's always very sure. It's his best quality."

Sara set down the cup. "Twelve gold," she said. "Each. That's what the Debri house will pay you, for as many as you can make. And twenty if you can get it onto a metal plate."

Tor made a sound that wasn't a word. "Twelve. He said two, and I thought two was—"

"I know what he said. Pine warps, Tor. Paint flakes. It's a beautiful field and it's on a board that'll be firewood in five years, and the people who buy fields like this know it, and that's why it's thirty and not three hundred. Put it on copper, or brass, anything that won't warp, and it lasts. Lasting is what people pay for."

Tor was no longer looking at her. He was looking past her, at the lamp on the desk, at its brass foot. His lips moved.

"You'd have to etch it," he said, to the lamp. "Paint won't sit on metal, not properly. You'd etch it, with acid. The store has— I'd need to think about the depth of the line—" He stopped and looked back at Sara. "Sorry. Yes. Twenty. That's— yes."

"Good," said Sara.

Tor's face changed.

"Only," he said, "I might not be able to. To make very many. Not past two weeks." He looked down at his hands on his knees. "Kolb's set me a shield. It has to stop his blows and a cutter, for twenty minutes, with me inside it. And if it doesn't—" He stopped. "I'd hate to promise the house a lot of boards and then be— not able to deliver them."

Trice looked at him for a long moment.

"Master Baker," she said. "We have every confidence in you. As it happens, we heard Kolb discussing you with the head of our department yesterday, after the bell. He said you'd manage it. He said he wouldn't have set it otherwise."

"Your department?"

"Mm," said Trice. "And besides, the school doesn't kill students. Hardly ever. They're paid by the head."

Tor looked at her with wide eyes.

"Oh," he said. "That's— thank you. That's very reassuring." He sat back a little. "So he only said it to make me work harder. So that I'd take it seriously."

"Yes," said Sara.

"That's very clever of him." A pause. "He did cut the pell in half, though."

Nobody spoke.

"There's one more thing," said Sara. Her voice had changed: slower, more even. "If I'm to represent you. I'd do the selling, and the carting, and the arguing, and you'd do the building and nothing else. And the split would be ten in the hundred."

"Ten," said Tor.

"Ten in the hundred. On everything sold."

His lips moved briefly. "Is that usual?"

"For a first contract it's usual."

"Then yes," he said, and smiled at her, wide. "Yes. Thank you. That's very kind."

Trice looked at Sara. Sara kept her eyes on Tor.

"Good," said Sara. "Then that's done."

She stood, and Trice stood, and Tor got up so quickly that his foot knocked the boot off the AIR sheet.

"I'd give you a card," said Sara. She made a small gesture down at the green dress, which had no pockets. "But. If you need us, ask Rolph. Or Tovey. Either of them can find us."

Tor gave a small, polite laugh.

"Goodnight, Master Baker," said Trice.

"Goodnight. Thank you. Thank you for coming. Before you go wherever you're going. I hope it's nice."

The door closed. The bar dropped into place with a soft knock.

* * *

At the head of the stairs, Sara put her hand to her hair, where the comb had been, and stopped.

"Go on," she said to Trice. "I'll catch you."

She went back along the corridor, quietly. At the fourth door she raised her hand to knock. From the other side came Tor's voice, low.

"Ask Tovey. Ask Count Thompson. To go and *fetch* them. For me." A short breath of a laugh. "Very good. That's very good." Then, quieter: "I suppose I deserved that."

Sara's hand stayed in the air.

Behind the door, a pen began to scratch. "Copper," said Tor's voice. "Not pine. Copper, acid, the depth of the line. Air but not dust. Twelve, twenty." A pause. "Ten in the hundred." A pause. "Very kind."

Sara lowered her hand and walked back down the corridor.

Trice was waiting at the foot of the stairs with her arms folded.

"Where's your comb?"

"I'll get it tomorrow." Sara put her arm through Trice's. "He thinks we were joking. About Tovey."

"Of course he does."

"He thinks Tovey would be insulted to be asked."

"Tovey would be delighted to be asked. He'd go and get us himself. He'd bring a lantern." They walked a few steps. "And Rolph?"

Sara was quiet for a moment.

"He didn't even hear Rolph," she said.

They went out into the court. The bell tower stood black against the stars. Behind them, one window on the top floor of the dormitory stayed lit.

* * *

The bar lifted before Rolph touched the door. He came in loudly and shut it with his heel. Tor was at the desk under a low lamp, in a drift of paper, his hands lying on top of it.

"Little Tor! You're famous."

"I'm not."

"You're famous in the Barrow, which is the only place that counts. Do you know what I heard tonight? I heard a second-year from House Calder tell a girl from the town that your laundry board can dry a cloak in the rain while you're wearing it. I heard a fifth-year say his father would pay forty for one. Forty! I heard Tovey's lot at the end table arguing about whether you'd sell to them first, on account of Tovey asking first." Rolph dropped onto his bed. It creaked. "Everybody wants one. Everybody."

Tor said nothing.

"What's the matter with your hands?"

"Kolb. The pell. With an iron bar. Until I couldn't." Tor flexed his fingers slowly. "It's coming back."

"Good. Because here's the thing." Rolph sat up. "Mother. Mother has to have them first. Not one: ten. Ten boards, so she can give them out to her friends, and her friends have friends, and inside a month every great house in the—everyone who matters will have seen one in Mother's hands first, and the rest of them will be breaking down Sara's door. You understand? It's the best thing that could happen to you. It's practically a favor."

"I haven't got anything to make them on," said Tor.

"You've got pine."

Tor put his chin down. The corners of his mouth turned in. "Sara says pine warps. Sara says pine's firewood in five years. Sara says it has to be metal. I haven't got any metal, Rolph. I haven't got any acid. I've got a cup of cold tea that I can't lift."

Rolph looked at him for a moment, his head tilted.

"Then buy metal."

"With what?"

Rolph went to the chest at the foot of his bed and laid his hand flat on the lid. It clicked. He took out a purse and counted gold into his palm. Tor's eyes went wider with every coin.

"Fifty-five." Rolph stacked them on the desk beside Tor's hands. "Half up front. That's how it's done."

"Half of *what*?"

"Of a hundred and ten. Eleven a board. I heard Debri's paying you twelve, and I'm your friend, so I get the friend price."

Tor looked at the stack. "That's one gold off for friendship."

"Exactly."

"That seems backward."

"It's how friendship works among merchants," said Rolph. "You wouldn't understand."

The corner of Tor's mouth twitched. He pulled it straight again.

"I'd need them soon," said Rolph. "That's the only thing. It's Mother's birthday on fifth day next week, and the express to the capital takes three days, and it goes when it goes, and I want them on it."

Tor looked up. "Her birthday's fifth day? Like the queen's?"

"Just like the queen's," said Rolph, and laughed.

"Do we get that day off? For the queen's?"

"Not a chance. You'd have to ask Kolb." Rolph laughed again, a little longer, and lay back on the bed with his hands behind his head. "Anyway. No hurry. Any time before the express. Tomorrow. The day after."

The chair scraped. Tor was on his feet with the stack of gold held against his chest in both hands, and he was looking at the door.

"The store closes at the ninth bell," said Rolph.

"I know," said Tor. "I'll knock."

* * *

The student store was dark. Three soft knocks sounded on its door, then a pause, then three more.

After a while a candle moved inside, and the door opened on an old man in a nightcap and slippers.

Tor stood on the step. "I'm terribly sorry," he said. "I know you're closed. I'm so sorry. Only, I need some copper squares. And some acid."

"It's past the ninth bell."

"Yes. I'm sorry."

"Copper's dear. Five-inch squares, three copper a square, and it's not cheap acid either, the good stuff, for etching. Come back in the morning with your—"

"How many have you got?"

"Squares? A crate. A hundred, near enough."

"I'll take them all," said Tor. "And enough acid for a hundred. And a basin, if you've got one that won't mind. Sorry." He held out his hands and opened them. They were full of gold.

The storekeeper looked at the gold for some time. He looked up the court, toward the gate, where a lamp burned by the porters' lodge. Then he looked back at Tor's face.

"Come in," he said. "Watch the step."

Inside, he set out a crate of copper squares on the counter, two stone jars stoppered with wax, a lead-lined basin, a pair of tongs, and a bundle of rags.

"Will the acid hurt my hands?" Tor asked.

The storekeeper reached under the counter and brought out a pair of oiled gloves, a little dusty. He blew on them and laid them on the pile.

He counted change out of the till in silver and copper, twice. Tor counted it after him, twice.

Tor got the crate onto his shoulder and the jars across his back in a sling, and went off across the dark court, slowly, listing to one side.

The storekeeper stood in his doorway with the candle until Tor reached the dormitory. Then he shut the door, went to the till, and opened it, and looked inside for a while before he closed it again.

* * *

The window of the room on the top floor stood open to the cold. On the sill sat a basin, and beside it the lamp, and Tor, in gloves much too big for him, lowered a copper square into the basin with tongs. The bare metal fizzed. Where he'd drawn the pattern on in something waxy, the lines stayed bright.

Rolph sat up in bed with his eyes watering. "What *is* that?"

"Acid. Sorry. I've got the window open."

"I can see that. I can *taste* that."

"It's only for the lines." Tor lifted the square out, tipped it to the lamp, rinsed it in the jug, and set it on a rag on the floor. The floor between the beds was covered in squares, laid out in rows of ten, each one with the drying field bitten into it in fine dark lines.

"How many have you done?"

"Seventy-two. Seventy-three."

Rolph got out of bed and stepped carefully between the rows to the window. He looked down at the squares. Most of the lines were crisp. On one, a line had gone wide and furry; on another, a corner of the pattern was missing. These lay in a small separate pile: five or six.

"What about these?"

"Those are crooked," said Tor, without looking around. "They'll work. They're just crooked. I'll give those away."

"Give them—Tor, those are *eleven gold*—"

"They're crooked." Tor lowered another square into the basin. "Davie can have one. He can't keep his shirts dry, he's got no idea, it's awful. And the washing house should have one, for the people who do the real washing, the sheets. And Hob, in the kitchens, his mother takes in laundry in Hollin. And the other scholarship boys in the east wing. They can share. Seventy-four."

Rolph opened his mouth. He shut it. He sat down on the end of his bed in the cold and watched.

Later, the lamp had moved to the floor by the door, and Rolph was lying down with his eyes closed. Tor crouched by the door, putting squares into a sack, ten at a time, counting under his breath. Fifty went into the sack. Fifty stayed on the desk in a squared-off stack.

Rolph opened his eyes. "Where are you going?"

"Workroom." Tor stood. The sack clanked. "You need to sleep. And the fumes. I'll do half there and half here tomorrow. Yours'll be in the first ten. I'll do yours first."

"Tor, it's the middle of the night."

"Yes. Sorry." At the door, with the sack on his shoulder, he put his chin down again. "Fifty-five gold. Honestly, Rolph."

The bar dropped behind him.

Rolph lay looking at the ceiling. The window was still open. After a long time he got up and went to the desk and looked at the small pile of crooked squares. He picked up the top one, turned it to the faint light from the window, and put it back exactly where it had been.

* * *

The workroom on the east stair had a stove that ticked. One lamp burned on the long bench. Dorgal sat beside it with a painted pine board in front of him and a sheaf of notes, a wet rag laid across the board. He lifted the rag. It was still wet. He put it down.

The door opened. In one movement, Dorgal pulled a cloth over the board.

"Oh!" Tor stood in the doorway with a clanking sack on his shoulder. "Sorry. I didn't think anyone would—I'm so sorry. Do you mind if I—"

"My dear Baker," said Dorgal. "It's a workroom. Work."

Tor set the sack down by the stove, gently, and took the stool at the far end of the bench. He laid out ten copper squares in a row. The pattern on each was the same: a hook at the lower left, a doubled crossing near the center, something like a fishbone along the top edge.

Dorgal looked at the squares. Then he looked at the cloth over his own board.

"I heard you had a visitor this morning," he said.

"Yes! Mr. Sorvey." Tor looked up, smiling a little. "Do you know him?"

Dorgal didn't answer at once.

"Everyone knows him," he said.

"He frightened me a bit, honestly. Sorry. I expect he's very nice."

"He isn't," said Dorgal. "Particularly."

Tor looked at him for a moment, then went back to his squares.

He held his hand flat above the first one, not touching it, and half closed his eyes. His lips moved. He laid a finger on the line at the lower left and traced it, slowly, around the hook and over the crossing and along the fishbone. Halfway along the fishbone, his finger slid off the line onto bare copper, and stayed there, and his lips kept moving.

After a while, he opened his eyes, set the square aside, and reached for the next.

Dorgal watched until Tor's eyes were closed again. Then he wet a corner of his rag at the jug and laid it over the finished square. Water rose out of the cloth in tiny beads and ran off onto the bench.

Dorgal folded the rag and put it on his knee.

"Baker," he said. "Your finger came off the line. On the first one."

"Did it?" Tor opened his eyes. "Oh. Probably. I'm tired. It doesn't matter."

"It doesn't *matter*?"

"Not really. The sigil's only there to keep my place. And so people know what it's for, after. You could do it on a blank square if you wanted to, it's only harder, you lose track of where you are." Tor thought. "It's like a recipe. You don't need it to make the bread. It just helps if you're doing forty loaves."

The stove ticked.

"Interesting," said Dorgal.

He looked at the cloth over his board. He looked at his notes, eleven pages, each with the same sigil drawn on it in careful ink. Tor had gone back to work.

The tower bell struck. Dorgal took out a lamp-square and a pen and began to copy it. The tower bell struck again.

By the third batch, Tor was yawning. He yawned in the middle of a square, stopped, looked at the square, said, "Sorry. Again," and began at the hook. He spoke to the squares, quietly. "You're the same as the last one, aren't you. Yes. You are. Just like him." Once he stopped, laid the batch out, counted nine, searched under the bench, and found the tenth in his lap.

The bell struck again. Dorgal's lamp-square lay unfinished in front of him.

At last Tor stood and stretched, and his back cracked. He put the squares into the sack by tens, slowly, stopping to flex his hands. One square stayed on the bench, a little apart, with a furry line along the fishbone.

"Would you like that one?" said Tor. "It's crooked. It works, it's just crooked. I'm giving the crooked ones away."

Dorgal looked at the square.

"I couldn't possibly," he said.

"It's only crooked."

"Then give it to someone who needs it."

Tor nodded, and put the square in the sack.

"Goodnight," he said. "Thank you for the company. It's much nicer, doing copies with someone there."

He heaved the sack onto his shoulder and went out. On the stair, his steps went down unevenly: one step, a pause, the next.

The outer door banged. Below the window, uneven footsteps crossed the stones of the court, and faded.

Dorgal lifted the cloth from his board. He laid one finger on the painted line at the lower left and began to trace it. Halfway around the hook, he lifted the finger and let it wander off the line.

* * *

The door at the foot of the east stair opened, and Tor came out into the court with the sack on his shoulder. It clanked. He took two steps and stopped.

He stood on one leg and held the other up in front of him, and looked at it. He shook it. He put it down. It landed a little to one side of where he'd aimed it, and he lurched.

"Come on," he said to it. "Please."

He slapped his thigh twice with his free hand, and took another step. The leg swung out stiffly from the hip. He flexed the fingers of his free hand, and they closed slowly, and only partway.

"Oh, no," he said quietly. "Oh, no, no. What did I do?" He looked back at the door he'd come out of, and at the dark windows of the workroom above it. "Sat on it. I sat on it wrong. That's all. Walk it off. Walk it off, Tor."

He started across the court on the diagonal, toward the dormitory, very slowly, looking at his feet.

At the far side of the court, beyond the bell tower's black bulk, there was a flash.

It was blue-white, a short crackling spill of light, low to the ground, gone almost as soon as it came. In it, for an instant, there were two figures. One was huge. The other was smaller, though still taller than Tor, and held something out at arm's length toward the big one. A sound came across the stones, a dull *whump*, and the big figure went back three steps as if shoved.

Then it was dark again.

Tor stopped. He stood swaying, the sack on his shoulder, looking toward where the flash had been.

Another spill of sparks. The big figure was coming forward again, with sparks coming off its head and shoulders as it moved, lighting the stones around its feet. The smaller one backed away, stumbling. It held its arm out. *Whump.* The big one rocked back a step, and came on.

The smaller figure was staggering badly, one hand pressed to its side.

Tor took a step backward. Then another. He turned toward the dormitory door, and took two lurching steps toward it.

In the dark across the court, there was a heavy sound, flesh on cloth, and a cry: high, sharp, a girl's voice. It came back off the wall of the long hall.

Tor stopped.

He stood with his back to the fight. His head came slowly around.

"Miss Trice?" he said, under his breath.

From across the court, hoarsely: "Baker. *Run.*"

He turned all the way around, and came toward them.

He couldn't run. His left leg had stopped bending; he swung it from the hip, and lurched, and swung it again. The sack slid off his shoulder, and he caught it by the neck in both hands, and carried it in front of him like that, low, banging against his knees. He was talking, low and fast.

"—better have a very good reason. Out at this hour. Very good reason. Fighting. At this hour. Miss Trice. Honestly."

The smaller figure was on one knee on the flagstones. The big one stood over her. The sparks came off it in a long crackling sheet now, and in their light Trice's face was turned up, white, her mouth open, her arm half raised with something short and dark in her fist. The arm stopped halfway and stayed there, shaking. The big figure's face, above the sparks, was in shadow.

Tor came up behind it.

He planted his feet wide, swung the sack back with his whole body, and brought it around into the middle of the big figure's back.

*Clank.*

The big figure didn't move.

*Clank.* The squares inside the sack slid and chimed together. "Sorry," said Tor. *Clank.* "Sorry. Leave her alone. Sorry."

On the ground, Trice made a sound that might have been a laugh.

*Clank.*

The big figure turned around. It turned slowly, the whole body coming around after the head.

Trice's raised arm dropped. She put both palms flat on the stones and pushed herself up an inch.

Tor's knees gave. He didn't fall. His legs simply stopped holding him properly, and he sagged forward, and the sack fell out of his hands and hit the flagstones with a crash that rang all the way around the court. He put a hand out to nothing. He stayed up, bent over, swaying, looking up.

The clouds moved off the moon.

The light came down gray across the court and onto the big figure: a broad jaw, dark brows, a plain dark cloak with a brooch at the shoulder. The face was entirely still. There was nothing in it at all.

Tor's mouth opened. It made the shape of a name, without sound.

*Tovey.*

Tor's face went white, and still, and then, all at once, very polite.

On the ground behind them, Trice shook her head, once.

Tovey looked down at Tor a moment longer. Then his eyes moved off him, past him, unfocused.

And he swung.
