# Chapter plan

Our chapter plan: what each chapter must do, and the planning fields from `AGENTS.md` §8. Plot facts come from `bible/canon-notes.md`; when the original's beats are written up, they go in `outline/plot-beats.md`.

## The book's engine

The book runs on a loop, and chapter one has to show it once, in small, so the reader learns to expect it (user's direction, October 9, 2026):

1. **Tor builds something.** A practical problem catches his attention and he solves it with magic.
2. **The build causes complications.** It works too well, breaks a rule, costs him something, or draws the wrong kind of attention.
3. **The complications give him his next idea.** He answers trouble with another build, and the loop starts again at a larger scale.

Notes for drafting:

- Each turn of the loop should **raise the stakes**: from a private inconvenience to school rules, then money, nobles, and the crown.
- Because we don't narrate Tor's thoughts (`AGENTS.md` §3), **step 3 must be visible**: muttering, a sketch on scrap wood, a question to the builder instructor, a sudden silence at the dinner table while the others keep talking. The reader should see the idea land before Tor explains it.
- The loop is Tor's, but **other characters supply the complications** and react to the builds. That's where their voices and tensions get room.
- Keep the routine (chapter one) as the baseline the loop disrupts. Later chapters can skip the routine or break it, because the reader already knows its shape.

## Chapter 1

### What the chapter must do

1. **Introduce the setting and the characters.**
2. **Run the engine once** (magic → complication → next idea).
3. **Set the pace of the school routine.**

### The laundry scene, beat by beat

All beats are canon (user, October 9, 2026) unless labeled.

1. **Tor washes clothes** to have wet laundry for the test. He's soaping and scrubbing; the water suds up.
2. **Davie is at the next tub, mystified.** His water won't suds up and he can't see why, because he doesn't know laundry takes soap. He's grumpy about having to do it at all, and afraid someone will notice he doesn't know how.
3. **Tor stops to help.** He teaches Davie basic laundry and lends him soap, *before* testing his field. Something small someone else needs comes ahead of the thing he has spent days on. This is the scene's main character beat for Tor.
4. **Dorgal comes in** and teases Tor about being a washerwoman.
   **Davie keeps his head down.** The mockery he was afraid of lands on Tor instead, and he stays out of it.
5. **Tovey comes in** to see how this new magic works, and **a crowd follows him**, because whatever Tovey is interested in, people want to see.
6. **The test:** the field dries the laundry in moments. (Whether it happens before or after Tovey arrives is assumed; after gives it the biggest audience.)
7. **Everyone wants one.** Tovey wants it for his mother. The crowd all want copies.
8. **Trice and Sara arrive**, after Tovey.
9. **Trice gets Sara to estimate what it's worth.** Value depends on what the magic is made on and how long it lasts. Tor's is cheap wood and paint, so it's worth far less than one on good material, but still **30 gold**, a significant sum.
10. **Tor can't give it to anyone.** He has to turn the prototype in for testing. He promises nothing, but **assumes he'll be making copies for all of them.**
11. **Rolph comes by** shortly after Trice and Sara, and gets Tor out of a social situation he isn't equipped to handle.

**Staging notes** (invented):

- **Davie's ignorance has to be visible, not explained.** He's working hard and getting nowhere: scrubbing, wringing, holding a shirt up to the light, glancing at Tor's suds and then his own flat gray water. The reader should work out what's missing a beat before Tor says it.
- **Tor's help comes the way his reaction rules say:** yes before Davie finishes asking sideways; no mockery; a practical lesson, possibly with a baking or home comparison. He shouldn't make it a kindness Davie has to thank him for, because that's what lets Davie accept it.
- **The scene keeps getting bigger.** One noble who needs help, then a rival who mocks, then Tovey and his crowd, then Trice putting a price on it. A private test of a laundry trick turns into a public event with a market value, which is the book's engine in small.
- **Dorgal and Tovey are a voice contrast in one scene.** Dorgal jokes at Tor's expense; Tovey never does (`tovey.md`, "Never").
- **Davie keeps his head down** while Dorgal teases Tor. He was just helped, and doesn't speak up for the person who helped him. It shouldn't be played as villainy; it's fear, and the reader should see it cost him something (a glance away, a shirt folded too carefully).
- **The perception gap, staged.** Tor is afraid of Tovey and treats him as a threat that could strike out at any slight: extra courtesy, careful words, keeping his distance. Tovey is not disdainful and not hostile; he's curious and wants a gift for his mother. Since we don't narrate Tor's thoughts, the gap shows as a mismatch between Tor's behavior and what Tovey actually says and does. The reader sees both; Tor sees only his fear. (See `bible/characters/tor.md`, the perception gap.)
- **The price is a character beat for Trice and Sara.** Trice asks; Sara answers. Trice knows the question that matters and who in the room can answer it. Sara's answer, broken down by material and how long it lasts, shows knowledge the reader didn't expect from her.
- **30 gold lands differently on each person.** To Tovey and the crowd it's a purchase. To Tor, a baker's son, it's probably more than his family sees in a long time (assumed), and he made it out of scrap. His reaction (disbelief, an apology, an urge to explain that it's only paint) is the scene's comic and painful peak.
- **Turning in the prototype is the hook.** Demand without supply: everyone wants one, he has one, and it isn't his to give.
- **The unspoken promise has to be visible.** Tor never says he'll make copies; he assumes it. With no narrated thoughts, the reader sees it from outside: he counts heads under his breath, asks someone how many students board at the school, looks at the wood square as a quantity, or mutters a figure of days. Nobody in the room hears a promise. The reader understands he has made one to himself. This is the engine's step 3, and the start of chapter two's trouble (how long would that take, and who said he had to?).
- **Another case of the perception gap.** The crowd want to buy; Tor hears an obligation. Nobody asked him for free copies for everyone; he sets himself that task. (invented framing of canon)
- **Rolph's rescue ends the scene.** He's the friend who knows how to do what Tor can't: talk his way out of a room full of nobles. His arrival should feel like air coming back in. Use his reaction rules (loud, physical, joking) and let him leave with Tor before anyone pins Tor down.
- **Sara's appraisal shows her background before anyone states it.** She prices the field as someone who has handled the best magic the continent has to offer and grew up around the capital. The reader can infer a merchant house from how she talks about cost and material. (invented staging of canon)

**Why the laundry scene works** (craft notes; invented):

- **It introduces magic through need.** The reader sees what the field is for in the hands of someone who can't manage without it, before anyone explains how building works.
- **It shows the class order through an inversion.** The noble can't do laundry and the baker's son can, which sets up the culture clash without a word of exposition.
- **Davie and Tor are both outsiders, in opposite ways.** Davie hides that he doesn't know something; Tor hides that he knows a great deal. Both fear being laughed at.
- **Reaction rules collide.** Davie asks for help sideways and won't admit ignorance; Tor says yes before the sentence ends and calls the field "not really anything". Neither will say what's actually happening, and the scene's humor and tension come from that.
- **It shows who Tor is before it shows what he can do.** He helps with the soap before he tests the magic, so the reader meets his character first.
- **The laundry is a setting the reader can see:** wet wool, cold water, a bar of soap and the suds it makes (or doesn't), a tub, steam, a painted square of cheap wood set down among the dripping shirts, and then a room too full of people in brown tunics.

### The room, beat by beat

All beats are canon (user, October 9, 2026) unless labeled.

1. **Rolph "rescues" Tor** from the laundry and brings him back to their room. People follow along, hoping for free things; the two of them shut the door on them.
2. **The lock.** The room has a magic lock: it identifies the room's occupants and magically lifts a simple door bar on the inside. Tor **copied** it from a chest of Rolph's, a magic lockbox **full of gold** that sits in the room.
3. **Rolph's interest in Tor's magic.** He is very interested in Tor and his work, in what looks like innocent envy: he once spent hours staring at the room lock.
4. **Rolph wants a copy for his mother** too.
5. **Rolph explains prestige.** Among nobility, having new magic before anyone else is a mark of significant prestige.
6. **The idea.** Rolph says it would be nice if the field worked on fruit and vegetables, since crop losses are a frequent topic at home.
7. **Tor goes off into a fugue** working out exactly how that would work and what he'd change to turn a clothes drying field into a food drying field. He goes to his desk and starts sketching. Rolph watches, patiently amused.
8. **Tor remembers he's in the middle of a conversation** and apologizes.
9. **The rest of the day:** Tor eats and recovers from the long trance, now and then making notes on the new idea. Rolph reads (studying, presumably) and keeps him company.
10. **That night** Tor falls asleep dreaming about the new project, wakes, and furiously scribbles notes that make perfect sense to him and are indecipherable to anyone else.
11. **Next morning** he gets an early start, to show the original field to his professor for testing. (This opens the last third.)

**Staging notes** (invented):

- **The closed door is a scene change in pressure.** The laundry was public and full of rank; the room is private, and the only place Tor isn't performing. The crowd outside, hoping for free things, is the laundry's demand following him home. Let it be heard (voices, a knock, the bar dropping) before it's shut out.
- **The lock does two jobs.** It shows the reader what copying is (Tor took an existing magic and reproduced it) next to the novel build of the drying field. And it puts Rolph's chest in view: a magic lockbox full of gold, in the room of a boy from a "merchant family." The narration shouldn't comment on it. Tor doesn't find it strange, so nobody in the scene says it's strange; the reader can.
- **Rolph's hints stack, and none is an admission.** The lockbox of gold; a mother who'd want new magic as a mark of status; "among nobility," said like someone who knows from inside; crop losses as dinner-table talk at home, which sounds like land and tenants more than trade. Each has an innocent explanation. Together they make a pattern the reader can see and Tor can't. (Track in `bible/reader-knowledge.md`.)
- **Rolph's envy should read as innocent on the surface.** Hours staring at a door lock is funny and a little sad: he wants to understand what Tor does and can't. The profile's darker version (envy he'd never admit) stays underneath. (`rolph.md`)
- **The prestige line explains the laundry crowd after the fact.** The reader now understands why Tovey's interest drew everyone, and why Tovey and Rolph both think of their mothers: new magic is a social currency. Tor hears it as information, not as pressure, which it is.
- **The fugue is the engine visible.** We don't narrate the idea. We show Tor stop mid-sentence, go to the desk, the scratch of the pen, Rolph's amused patience, the bell passing, and then Tor surfacing to apologize. The apology is his reaction rule (`tor.md`); Rolph's patience is the friendship.
- **The recovery is the trance's cost, shown in the body.** Hunger, stiffness, eating more than he means to, falling asleep early. Rolph's quiet reading beside him is the first calm the chapter gives the reader.
- **The night notes pay off later.** Make them concrete on the page: cramped lines, sigil fragments, arrows, words run together. Someone (Rolph in the morning, or the professor later) should try to read them and fail.

### 3. The routine and its pace

- Mark time in **bells**. Establish the day's fixed points (rising, meals, meditation, builder class, combat, the store, lights out) so the reader can feel when Tor is late, absent, or skipping. (canon elements; the order of the day is assumed)
- The routine has a rhythm: **short, regular beats** for the school day, set against the **long, timeless stretch** of the trance. The contrast between the two is the chapter's pacing.
- Show the routine **once, briskly**, as Tor rejoins it. The middle third takes him out of it (a day of recovery in his room), so the routine's clearest showing is likely the **last third**: the early start, the bells, the walk to the professor, the classes he has to face after missing days. (placement assumed)
- Use the routine to place characters: who sits where at meals, who partners whom in combat, who is in builder class with Tor. Rank shows through the routine. (`bible/places.md`: the school pressures rules and rank.)
