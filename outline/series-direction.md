# Series direction

Where we leave the original's plot, and what we put in its place. The original uses sex as a social lubricant, a plot device and a source of culture clash. We drop all of it (user, October 12, 2026; see `AGENTS.md` §9), keep the general shape of the plot, and accept that the book may drift far from its source.

For each change: the original beat, the job it did, and our replacement. Labels as in `codex/README.md`.

## The rule for replacements

Don't just cut a beat; find the job it did and give that job to something else. In the original, sex does roughly five jobs (assumed; our general reading, not yet checked against the user's knowledge of the books):

1. **Motive.** Powerful people want something from Tor.
2. **Relationship development.** Characters get closer, or prove they have.
3. **Culture clash.** A prudish backwoods baker's son among worldly nobles.
4. **Tradecraft.** The spy school's tools.
5. **Status.** Being wanted shows Tor's rising worth.

Our general replacements (proposed; not yet confirmed beat by beat):

1. **Motive:** houses want his work, his loyalty and his secret (what he is and who made him), through contracts, patronage and eventually knighthood.
2. **Relationships:** shared risk, debts paid, secrets kept or broken, and a small crew built around Tor's work.
3. **Culture clash:** see "The social glue" below.
4. **Tradecraft:** recruitment and cultivation (favors, staged rescues, engineered debts), never seduction.
5. **Status:** shown by who sends for him, where he's seated, which gifts arrive.

**Tone of the society** (user, October 12, 2026): the nobility should have a bit of **romanticized value**, a world the reader would enjoy visiting. Villains are the ones who take its customs too far. Avoid making the whole culture predatory or sad.

## The social glue: flirtation as a game of wit

**Chosen by the user, October 12, 2026.** The nobility's social lubricant is **flirtation as performance**: compliments, wordplay, small favors, dance cards, meaningful looks that mean nothing. Everyone does it with everyone; it's how people say hello, smooth over a quarrel, ask a favor, or show they belong. It's charming, and the reader should enjoy it. It stays non-physical: words, manners and gestures (`AGENTS.md` §9).

- **Why it baffles Tor:** he's a prude and completely honest, without an ounce of social deception. In Two Rivers a compliment means what it says, and a compliment like *that* means you're courting and should speak to her father. The scene is impenetrable to him: he takes pleasantries literally, answers rhetorical flirtation with sincere facts, and can't tell a game from a declaration. (user, October 12, 2026)
- **The comedy runs both ways:** nobles find his honesty either baffling or devastatingly charming, because nobody has ever *meant* it before. A sincere compliment from Tor lands harder than a season of practiced ones.
- **The royal house takes it up another level.** In the capital, at Rolph's family home, the game is played at its highest skill. Tor is completely unable to deal with **Rolph's mother, Connie, and his younger sisters**. (user, October 12, 2026)
- **Villain use (proposed):** reputation is everything, so a rumor, a misplaced letter or a staged moment can do real damage. Ward is the one who takes it too far.
- **Novelty magic as fashion** (proposed, secondary): new magic is prestige (canon), and Tor's builds can become the season's craze. Kept as a supporting idea, not the glue.
- **Ruled out as the center:** gambling and debt markers (too sad, per the user). Light, harmless betting is fine as background color.

## Rolph's fiancée and Count Ward

**Original (canon, user):** in the capital, Rolph's fiancée turns out to be pregnant by Count Ward, part of Ward's plot to destabilize the kingdom. She is "married" to a distant old noble who conveniently dies, and later miscarries. In later books she manages Tor's house outside the capital; Tor treats her well and never takes advantage of her.

**Jobs it does:** a scandal that makes the royal match impossible; Ward's lever on the succession; a victim with nowhere to go, who later joins Tor's household.

**Ours: a gift with a field in it** (Option A, chosen by the user, October 12, 2026). Ward arranges for her to receive a piece of jewelry she wears every day, with a hidden listening or influencing field built into it. Once it's found, she is a security breach in the royal household. She can't prove she didn't know, and the court can't risk her.

- **Tor's way in is his craft:** he notices the field, or must build something to find or neutralize it. (invented)
- **The miscarriage beat** becomes the device's destruction: the threat ends, but the damage is done. (invented)
- **The sham marriage stays:** a quiet marriage to an old lord far from court gets her out of the capital without a treason trial. He dies; she is a widow with a title, no money and nowhere to go, which leads to managing Tor's house. (canon shape; reason invented)
- **To decide:** her name, house and personality; Ward's profile; whether Rolph and she have a relationship worth showing before the scandal; whether her family's finances are also a lever (Option B, folded in lightly or not at all).

## The beach house

**Original (canon, user):** Trice, Sara, Rolph and Tor take a beach house holiday. Rolph and the girls occupy themselves in ways that leave Tor feeling left out. In the original this runs through the nobles' sexual culture.

**Ours: everyone else is secretly working** (direction liked by the user, October 12, 2026, especially Trice and Sara's training). The holiday is real, but it's also a cover.

- **Trice and Sara** are on a **special-school field exercise** set by their department head. (invented)
- **Rolph** has family business, which is to say crown business, under cover of sailing and calls on neighbors. (invented)
- **Tor** sees only that his friends keep disappearing, in pairs or alone, and coming back with stories that don't match. He assumes they're tired of him. The reader sees they're working, and sometimes protecting him. (invented; the perception gap, `AGENTS.md` §3)
- **Left out, Tor builds.** Something for the fishermen, the boats or the house staff; the build causes the next complication, so the holiday runs the engine instead of stalling it. (invented)
- Details of the exercise: see "Beach house: the girls' exercise" below (proposed).

### Beach house: the girls' exercise (proposed)

Training that looks like a holiday from outside and reads clearly to the reader:

- **Surveillance and tailing** in the village; **disguise** (Trice selling fish in the market, cutting Tor dead when he waves).
- **Getting invited:** a neighboring house the girls must get inside of, socially or otherwise.
- **Night work:** climbing, swimming, locks. What Tor sees is wet boots by the door at dawn and sand on the stairs.
- **Odd questions:** they ask Tor very specific things ("How would you open a lock like this one? Asking for a story.") and borrow his builds.
- **The exercise target could be Tor himself:** find out what the builder is working on. He tells them freely the first time they ask, which ruins the exercise ("You can't just *tell* us."). Light on the surface, with Trice's "whose interests does she serve" thread underneath.
- **Payoff:** skills practiced here are the ones the girls use in the capital to help uncover Ward's jewel.
